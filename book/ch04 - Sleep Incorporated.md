# Chapter 4 - Sleep Incorporated

I’d like you to think about the foundation of your sleep system as a hierarchy, like a company. I’m going to call it Sleep Incorporated. And, you’re the CEO.

This company makes only one thing: a good night's sleep. But you can't place an order directly. That's the uncomfortable truth from Chapter 3: sleep is something your body *creates*, once the conditions are right. You can't sit down in the evening and *decide* to produce it. It's an output, and outputs require everything upstream to work properly.

As CEO you can’t reach in and operate the machinery yourself. You can’t do anyone’s job for them. What you can do is set the expectations: when your staff clock on, when they clock off, and a clean handover from the day shift to the night shift. Get that right and everyone works together, so the company produces the result on its own.

At the head of the company you have 4 key hormones. These are your executives.

They’re the four we landed on last chapter, not a complete biological picture, instead something a little bit less accurate, but way more useful: your 4 best leverage points. Your A-team. Each one doing its best, with its own job and its own shift.

Two of these executives strongly influence sleep. And two of them strongly influence awake.

Lets meet them.

**The Sleep Executives:**

**Adenosine**¹: your sleep pressure executive. The force that builds from the moment you wake up, making you good and tired by bedtime.

**Melatonin**: your sleep timing executive. Watching the clock and signalling your body night has arrived and it's time to sleep.

**The Wake Executives:**

**Cortisol**: your waking-energy executive. Gets you up in the morning, sharpens your focus, and keeps you going through the day.

**Dopamine**¹: your motivation executive. Wired to keep you stimulated and moving forward.

Below the executives sit the managers, a dozen or so supporting hormones. They take their lead from the top and translate it into the more detailed operations of the body: mood, temperature, appetite, recovery. They matter, but for the most part they follow.

Below the managers are the workers, the neurotransmitters that carry the moment-to-moment instructions, firing and inhibiting, keeping the machinery moving. There are a lot of them. They are very busy. And they almost entirely depend on clear direction from above.

If the executives are working together, the whole company functions properly. The managers know what to do. The workers carry it out. And you sleep.[^2]

When the executives are working against each other (cortisol still high when it should be falling, dopamine fed all evening when it should be quieting), the managers get conflicting instructions which they pass straight to the workers. Some are ready for you to sleep, others are working hard to keep you awake. The result is chaos. You're lying awake at midnight, mind still running, wondering why you can't drift off.

Once I learned the executive level was where I had influence, and the key was to get them working together, I needed to understand exactly what that looked like. How each one should move, hour by hour, across a full day. So I started mapping it out, all of them together on a graph, throughout a 24 hour period.

That's when it clicked. 

Each one needed to rise and fall, in the right amounts, at the right time of day to arrive at the right point at bedtime. The concept was simple. At night, the sleep hormones need to be high, and the wake hormones need to be low. If I could get Sleep incorporated to generate those conditions, I could output sleep.

Here's what that looks like. This graph shows a lot we haven't talked about yet. For now its enough to know: hormones will rise and fall throughout the day. A good nights sleep depends on them landing in the right place at bedtime.

Once I had that picture in my head, everything else really started to fall into place. That hunch from the drive - daytime mattered more than the night - finally had a structure behind it. The habits that would help me sleep weren't just bedtime habits. Many of them were daytime ones. Sleep was downstream of everything else.

I also started to see why all the quick fixes I'd been trying were so unreliable. A sleeping pill, for instance, is like an external consultant. Coming from the outside, bypassing the executives and the managers, and delivering a message straight to the workers. "Forget the orders from the top… night shift starts now."

Sometimes they worked, sometimes they didn't. But they never fixed the root problem. The executives still weren't working together, and the system was still screaming top-down: wake up.

I'm not anti-sleeping pills. I've come to realise sometimes they're the only thing that gives immediate relief, and used short-term they can help while you train the system underneath. If you're already on them, I wouldn't recommend stopping. Instead build the foundation first, then speak to your doctor.

The graph above, which I will take you through in detail in the next four chapters, shows each of the hormones moving as they should. When they arrive at the correct place at bedtime, that's four executives working together.

When they don't... that's what I lived with for more than 20 years.

Middle of the night. Wide awake. Heart going. Thoughts running through tomorrow's meeting, solving problems, worrying about the future. Just running. Creating that desperate frustration where you're exhausted to your bones but your mind won't shut up. I didn't feel like I needed a good night's sleep. I felt like I needed a month of them!

And the good news I realised - this might not be a quirk of my personality or a sign that I am fundamentally broken.

Cortisol hadn't come down. Dopamine had been topped up all evening (screens and notifications), my mind never got a break. Melatonin was trying to rise, but it was fighting two executives still insisting on keeping me awake. Adenosine was high. The tiredness was real. But the rest of the company wasn't listening. Four executives, working against each other.

None of them were bad at their jobs. They were good staff, all four of them, working hard. But nobody had ever made it clear when their shifts should start and end. So in the middle of the night, when I needed it most, the handover never turned up.

Not broken, just not well trained.

I was the CEO of Sleep Incorporated, but I just didn't know it. I kept hiring consultants, one fix after another, never understanding why nothing stuck. The company kept underperforming and I kept looking for someone to step in and help.

The answer wasn't a new consultant. It was learning how the business actually worked, and training it, every day.

And that's what I want to give you: an understanding of the four executives, the hierarchy that follows their lead, and how to turn a company working against itself into one that works together.

That takes three things. A reliable body clock, so the executives know when to clock on and when to clock off. A strong calming system, so the day shift hands over cleanly to the night shift. And a CEO who stops calling the wrong executives in at the wrong times.

None of it changes overnight. Like any training, it comes from daily habits, and every habit in this book does one of those three jobs. Each one sends the company a signal, activating or calming. Send the same signals every day, and the executives learn their shifts.

Before the habits, though, you need to know the executives. The next four chapters take each one in turn: what it does, what disrupts it, and what you can do in an ordinary day to support it.

By the time you've read all four, you'll have something I didn't have for more than 20 years, a map of your own system, and a clear sense of what you can do to start supporting it.

[^1] Technically, adenosine is a neuromodulator and dopamine is mainly a neurotransmitter, so neither is strictly a hormone. But in the roles that matter here, both act the way hormones do: broad signals that set your body's state and rise and fall across the day. For simplicity, all four are called hormones throughout the book.

[^2] A note on exceptions. For some women, sleep deteriorates sharply in perimenopause or menopause as progesterone and oestrogen drop, a problem at the manager level that the executives alone can't fully override. If menopause is the only thing that changed, HRT may be enough to restore the conditions that make sleep possible again. But if your sleep was already fragile, or menopause has knocked other things out of rhythm, you'll likely need both, HRT and the work in this book. Rarer diseases can disrupt managers or workers directly. In all of these cases the framework still helps, but it's a starting point, not the whole answer, and your doctor is the right person to take it further with.
