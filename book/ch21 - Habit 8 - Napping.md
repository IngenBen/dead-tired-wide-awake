# Chapter 21 - Habit 8: Napping

Napping is one of those habits that feels completely logical when you're exhausted.

I used to love napping. I was sleep-deprived, always struggling to fall asleep and stay asleep, waking at two or three in the morning and lying there for hours. No matter which way you looked at it, I wasn't getting enough rest. So my thinking was simple: get as much as I can, whenever and however I can. If my body was desperate for sleep, the answer was more sleep. More of it, any time it was available.

It made perfect sense. But it was also making things worse.

The irony was that napping wasn't even reliable. Sometimes I'd lie down in the afternoon, genuinely exhausted, and sleep just wouldn't come. Tired but wired: the same state that was keeping me awake at night was following me into the day. So I'd get up feeling worse than before. Frustrated, unrested, and no closer to catching up.

What I didn't understand was that the naps I did manage were part of the problem. Especially when they ran long or landed too close to bedtime, I was spending down the very resource that was supposed to help me sleep that night. The short-term relief felt like progress. It wasn't. I was taking out a loan to pay a debt I was already struggling to pay back.

The standard advice on napping is reasonable. Keep it short: twenty minutes at most. Don't nap within seven to eight hours of your bedtime. And if you genuinely need one for safety (if you're about to get behind the wheel and you're not sure you should) then nap. That's not a habit question. That's a judgement call, and safety wins.

The reason for the rules comes down to adenosine: the sleep pressure that's been building since you woke up. Napping clears some of that out. A short nap at the right time eases the worst of it without doing much damage. A long nap, or one too close to bedtime, and you arrive at your sleep window with the pressure already partially reduced. The Sleep Executives don't have enough to work with. The timing shifts. And that night you're wide awake when you shouldn't be.

Naps are a tool, not a habit. Something to reach for when you need to get through the next few hours. Not a way to recover what last night didn't give you.

The problem was that understanding this didn't make the exhaustion go away.

There were times when I needed to drive (weekend sport, holiday road-trips) and I was genuinely worried about it. I knew driving like this was dangerous. So I'd lie down beforehand and try to nap. Sometimes it worked, and it was just enough to feel safe. Other times, nothing. On to the next trick. Coffee and something sweet.

The obvious solutions weren't working, and I knew they were costing me. That's when it occurred to me that I was already doing something in the evenings that was helping: not because I was trying to sleep, but because I'd stopped trying.

I'd been meditating for a few weeks by this point. I was consistent enough that I knew what the body scan felt like and what it was doing.

So instead of lying down and trying to fall asleep, I lay down and meditated. Fifteen minutes. Alarm set.

That was the whole change.

It worked for two reasons. The first was that I was getting something real out of it regardless of whether sleep came. Another session of nervous system training. The same work I was doing at night, now running during the day as well. The calming system getting stronger. And later in the afternoon, if the fatigue built again, I could run another. Unlike a nap, it costs nothing.

The second was that the pressure lifted. I wasn't lying there trying to do something my body was refusing to do. I wasn't failing at napping. I was meditating, and I was doing it fine. Whether I drifted off or not was almost beside the point: the rest was already happening.

The intention is simple. Lie down wherever you are (couch, bed, floor) and do the body scan for about fifteen minutes, with the alarm set to twenty as a safety net. If you can't lie down, sit somewhere quiet. Don't expect a transformation. What you're after is a tiny bump in alertness: enough to get you through the next couple of hours.

If you drift off and sleep, the alarm catches you at twenty minutes, before you overshoot. A nap that short won't hurt your sleep that night.

If you don't sleep, even better. You've finished the meditation, you get up quieter than when you lay down, and you've added another calming system session to the day. Nothing borrowed. Nothing owed.
