# Chapter 6 - Melatonin

It felt like I had a window.

Thirty, maybe forty minutes in the evening when sleep felt genuinely possible. Heavy eyelids. Body calming. Bed calling my name.

Miss it, and the night was gone.

It didn't matter how tired I was. If something disrupted that window, I'd lie in bed waiting for it to come back. It usually took hours. Sometimes I'd give up, go downstairs, and watch television until one or two in the morning, sometimes later, running out the clock until adenosine finally took me by force.

The frustration made it worse. Getting annoyed at being awake, then annoyed at being annoyed. Once I hit that loop, I was more awake than when I went to bed.

For years I had one explanation: I was just a bad sleeper. A bit weird. I have a sleep window. 

At some point I tried melatonin supplements. The logic seemed sound: it's a sleep signal, it's natural, more must be better. I used them for a while, but they didn't do much. I filed it under things that don't work for me and went back to running out the clock. I didn't realise the whole thing had a perfectly understandable hormonal explanation.

Melatonin is not a sleeping pill your body makes. It's a signal.

As the day fades and light drops, your brain begins releasing it. Slowly at first, then more steeply through the evening. It tells the system: night is coming, begin the sequence. Core body temperature drops slightly. Alertness falls. Sleep incorporated prepares.

But the signal doesn't force sleep. It enables it. It moves you toward the conditions where sleep becomes possible.

Those conditions converge in a narrow window: melatonin rising steeply, adenosine high from a full day of wakefulness, cortisol low, core body temperature falling, and the body clock's evening burst of alertness finally standing down. Researchers call that moment the sleep gate opening.

When they all arrive together, you feel the strongest pull toward sleep. That's the 30–40 minute window I was feeling. 

The timing is surprisingly regular. Melatonin starts to rise about two hours before your usual bedtime, and around then the gate opens and sleep becomes possible. Over the next couple of hours melatonin climbs at its fastest, and that steep stretch lands right around bedtime. That's where my window sat. Then the climb slows, and melatonin peaks in the middle of the night, a few hours after you've fallen asleep.

Melatonin continues to rise afterward, but more slowly. So does adenosine if you're still awake. The pull to sleep doesn't go away. What changes is the Wake Executives' chance to get back to work. The longer you lie there awake, the more room there is for a thought to land: something from today, something for tomorrow, or just the fact that you're still awake. Your mind picks it up and starts working on it, and that's what brings cortisol back in. If your Wake Executives never fully stood down this evening, it doesn't take much. At this point, with the Wake Executives still at their desks and cortisol behind them, you've missed the window. You're exhausted but wide awake.

For someone with a well-trained sleep system, there's flexibility across a longer stretch of the evening. The Wake Executives are calm and ready to hand over. The Sleep Executives don't have to battle them out the door.

For someone who's slept badly for years, it's different. The Wake Executives stay elevated, spike unexpectedly, refuse to go home. That flexibility disappears. Sleep becomes a contest. The pull to sleep is only strong enough to win in the window, before your mind finds something to work on, if at all.

For me, this explained a great deal. The frustration. The television until early hours of the morning. I'd been fighting a clock I didn't know existed.

So why do people who sleep badly miss the window? Three reasons mostly.

**Light**. Melatonin production is tightly coupled to the absence of light. Bright light in the evening, particularly the short-wavelength light that screens produce, tells the brain it's still daytime. The signal gets delayed, or somewhat suppressed.

**Irregular schedule**. Melatonin doesn't operate on demand. It follows a rhythm, and that rhythm is trained, gradually, by consistent behaviour. Go to bed and wake up at wildly different times each night and the system loses its reference point. The rise still happens, but it happens at the wrong time, or flattens, or arrives too late.

**Stress and hyperarousal**. Even when melatonin is present and rising on schedule, a nervous system running too hot can override it. The signal is there. But if the mind is still running (thoughts looping, stuck in go-mode, still activating when it should be calming), melatonin can't win that contest. That's cortisol's problem.

The supplements I'd tried made sense to me back then, but they missed the problem. The signal was already there. Everything was fighting it, and extra melatonin won't fix that.

Dim light in the evening, a consistent bedtime and morning light exposure, covered in the habit chapters, give the system the signals it needs to set the rhythm. Do those reliably and the timing takes care of itself. Calm the nervous system too, and you have a window that opens on schedule, with the right conditions for melatonin to win the contest once it does.

But getting to sleep is only half of melatonin's job.

The window is about falling asleep. Staying asleep is a different job. After the sharp rise, melatonin levels off, holding high through the middle of the night. Then production stops. At this point melatonin should be high enough, that even though it begins to drop, there's enough to carry you through until morning. Still doing its job until the Wake Executives are ready to take over.

For a lot of people who sleep badly, that peak sits lower than it should. The melatonin still comes, but weaker. So when production stops, there's less melatonin to carry you through the exact hours when the night is most fragile, making your sleep easier to break. A noise, a full bladder, a stray spike of stress (things a stronger sleep signal would absorb) are now enough to surface you. You wake at two or three in the morning and can't work out why.

You might be thinking: if the signal runs low through the night, why not top it up? Take some melatonin to hold sleep through the night, not just to open it.

That's the thinking behind slow-release melatonin, a version built to trickle out over hours instead of arriving in one early pulse, meant to hold a steadier level through to the morning. 

The evidence on that is currently thin. The best result is around thirteen extra minutes of sleep. And if you're only getting four hours of sleep a night, you're probably looking for a better fix than that. 

Your instinct might be the same when you miss the window. A small dose might recreate the signal. 

It's not a bad instinct, but by then it won't do much. Your own signal is already there. Melatonin is a timing signal. Take it late and you can nudge the body to expect sleep later. The morning routine later in the book is the better way to train it back into line. If you still want to try melatonin, take it earlier: two or three hours before your usual bedtime. Even then, expect minutes, not hours.

So melatonin can fall short in two directions. Arriving too late to open the window cleanly, or running too low to hold your sleep through the night. Most people who sleep badly experience both. The good news is the same habits discussed above work for both. Consistent timing and a steady rhythm tend to produce a strong signal.

Taking melatonin is not all bad though. Where it really earns its place is when your sleep is on the wrong schedule.

**Jet lag, shift work, any time your sleep is on the wrong schedule**

Here, shifting the clock is exactly the point. When you cross time zones or a new roster puts you to bed at a different hour, the melatonin signal isn't disrupted so much as stranded. Your body is still signalling night according to where, or when, it was set. The timing depends on the job. After a long flight, a small dose at your new local bedtime helps your clock catch up. To move your bedtime earlier, take it about an hour before the new bedtime, and get up a little earlier into bright light. For shift work, melatonin may help a little with daytime sleep, but light does more of the work.

**An important note on dosage**

Most over-the-counter melatonin runs far higher than what the body naturally produces. Take too much, and residual melatonin can still be circulating by morning, producing grogginess. And a high dose doesn't seem to add much to the effect, because what seems to matter is when the signal arrives, not how big it is. The extra just lingers and may blunt your sensitivity to the natural signal. That's why 0.5mg works. It's enough to send the signal. More isn't more.

Looking back, melatonin wasn't my problem.

My body was making melatonin, strong enough that I could feel the window. But an irregular sleep schedule meant it wasn't always arriving on time. And through the night, it was probably running low.

A signal that arrives late and one that runs low are both a rhythm problem. And setting the rhythm with timing and light is far better than high dose melatonin supplements.

Understanding what melatonin does (and what it doesn't) went some way to explaining why some nights sleep came the moment my head hit the pillow, and others it didn't come for hours.

Melatonin wasn't giving a strong, consistent signal. Which might have been manageable on its own. But something else was fighting it. Another executive walking back into the building and switching the lights on right as I was wanting to get to sleep.

That was cortisol's problem. And cortisol has a great deal to answer for.
