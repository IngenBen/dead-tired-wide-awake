# Chapter 8 - Dopamine

Of the four executives, dopamine was the one I understood least. And probably the one doing the quietest damage.

It didn't announce itself. There were no dark thoughts, no moment where something had obviously gone wrong. The dopamine problem looked completely normal. It looked like everyone else's evening.

On nights when I went to bed before my wife, I'd reach for my phone just to fill the gap while I waited. She wasn't there yet, the phone was on the bedside table, and the reach was automatic - much little it was in every other spare minute of the day.

What happened next varied. Sometimes ten minutes. Sometimes longer than I'd like to admit. Either way, by the time I put the phone down, something had changed. I wasn't more tired. That comfortable relaxed feeling that should have been pulling me toward sleep had been replaced by restless and alert.

I noticed this pattern for a long time before I understood it. Even when I suspected the phone wasn't helping, I didn't know why.

If cortisol pushes you to act, dopamine keeps you wanting more.

Dopamine responds to opportunity. The brain releases it to say *this is worth pursuing*. When you're working on something interesting and time flies, that's dopamine. Without it time drags. 

When it comes to sleep, it's not as much of a problem during the day, as long as it comes back down in the evening.

You've probably felt this without a screen anywhere near you. The night before a holiday, a big game, a first day somewhere new. You're tired, but you're lying there looking forward to it, running through how it'll go. That's dopamine that hasn't come down, and it's another version of the busy mind. Not urgent, just eager.

The problem is these dopamine hits are addictive and readily available - social media, notification pings, short-form video, work emails. Take those in to your evening and the quieter signals may start to lose out. Including the calm that should be settling you into sleep.

The feed I'd built, or more accurately, the feed that had built itself around my behaviour, was heavy on outrage, gender wars, political corruption, woke versus traditional, instant karma clips. The kind of content that triggers a strong reaction, a wave of interest and a jolt of stress. Cortisol as the main course, dopamine on the side.

I knew, on some level, that it wasn't useful. Occasionally I'd catch myself and think: *what are you doing? This is rubbish.* So I built a habit. Whenever I noticed, I'd pivot to something else: how-to videos, something educational, or research on something I wanted to buy. I'm naturally curious, so that kind of content felt better. More productive. More like me.

What I didn't understand was that I still hadn't fixed anything.

Both types of content were doing similar things to my system. The outrage feed delivered fast, volatile spikes, a neurological shove, with cortisol in the mix. The curiosity content delivered slower, warmer signals, but now it was dopamine as the main course with cortisol on the side. Different feeling but still activating signals.  I'd traded what I recognised as a problem for something that felt like a solution. It was arguably better with the reduction in cortisol, but was still not helping with sleep.

This is how dopamine works against you. The brain doesn't care whether the spike comes from outrage or curiosity, from a conspiracy clip or a product review. What it responds to is novelty and unpredictability. Wanting more. The possibility that the next thing might be interesting. That's what drives the doomscrolling.

When I took note of how my body was responding it was most obvious with short-form videos. Interesting. Funny. Boring. Outrageous. Boring. Boring. Funny. The brain becoming alert, craving the next hit, not wanting to put the phone down.

Over time, this can recalibrate the reward system: fast, frequent, unpredictable spikes become the baseline.  Slower signals, including those coming from the Sleep Executives, are dull and boring by comparison, and you subconsciously fight or ignore them. 

That's the sleep problem. Dopamine is best known as the reward chemical and that's exactly the point: it keeps you stimulated and searching. A mind still searching is not a mind letting go for the night. The later you stay on the feed, the more it compounds: dopamine, the Wake Executive, stays activated, indifferent to the fact that sleep should be happening.

When I switched to more 'wholesome' content, it felt better, but it wasn't calming. Curiosity still activates you: *this is interesting, I want to know more*. Which is fine during the day, but not when you should be getting off to sleep.

The contrast that made this clear to me was the television. The right show, low-stakes, predictable, easy to follow, is genuinely passive. The brain isn't anticipating what comes next. It's just following along. That's different to an emotional drama, thriller or suspense. And very different to a social media feed. I'll come back to what to watch and what to avoid in Chapters 17 and 19.

Two things finally pushed me to change.

The first came from that podcast line, the one about not doing daytime things at night.

The second came when I wasn't looking for it.

I was trying to understand why experts were so concerned about screens and gaming for children. The warnings were everywhere, but I wanted to understand the mechanism.

What I found was dopamine.

Gaming, it turns out, is almost perfectly engineered to deliver it. Constant rewards. Immediate feedback. Unpredictable outcomes. Every well-made game is designed to keep the dopamine signal running hot. The same logic applies to social media. Built entirely to hook you in and keep you there.

At first I thought, surely this didn't apply to me, I wasn't a child. I wasn't gaming. I was an adult. I could watch product research and how-to videos. Surely that was different.

Maybe it is different. Maybe it doesn't affect adults as much as children. I'm not sure.

What I do know is that the mechanism doesn't change. Dopamine at night is working directly against the Sleep Executives. It doesn't matter whether the spike comes from gaming, swiping through short-form video, or a deep-dive into espresso machines.

Looking back, that pre-bed scrolling was almost certainly pushing me past my sleep window some nights. Enough dopamine, and the cortisol that came with it, to carry me past the point where I'd have dropped off, with plenty for my mind to keep working on. By the time I put the phone down, the window had closed.

I hadn't been unlucky on those nights. I'd been juicing up on dopamine and scrolling through my own sleep window.

Understanding and recognising that took a long time. And even once the biology made sense, it still took me a while before my behaviour caught up.
