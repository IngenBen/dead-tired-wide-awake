# Chapter 2 - The Turning Point

It was probably decades ago that I saw a doctor. He gave me something to help me sleep, but made it clear he didn't want to prescribe it again. What I really needed, he said, was to get to the root of it - see a psychologist, or someone like one. He didn't think a sleep study was worth it, because it didn't sound like apnea. And, that's mostly what sleep studies look for.

The sleeping pills didn't really help. They made me groggy and moody the next day. I pretty quickly decided that was worse that feeling sleep deprived. It's likely that sleeping pills are better these days. But they are still designed as short term help, not a long term solution.

At this point it was easy to feel angry at my GP for not having a solution. But A GP is a generalist, and insomnia isn't a generalist's kind of problem. The tools they have are a prescription they'd rather not repeat and a referral onward - in my case a psychologist. But I never really felt like I needed a psychologist. As it turns out, there are other options, but it seems they are fragmented, not well-known and not easy to access.

So I kept looking.

Books, podcasts, YouTube, forums. The occasional deep research  into whatever study had just made the rounds online. There was always something new. Some YouTuber who had found 'the thing', the mechanism, the explanation that was going to finally make sense of it all.

I would get genuinely excited. This one feels different. This one might actually be it.

It never was.

Some of the problem was volume. The good books didn't give you one thing to try, they gave you twenty. Sometimes more. Each with variations, caveats, individual differences to account for. For someone already exhausted and overwhelmed, the prospect of working through all of it was paralysing. So I'd pick one or two, and try for a couple of weeks. They wouldn't work, or I couldn't tell if they were working. So I'd stop them and move on to the next thing, wondering if I was ever going to find a fix.

With hindsight, the right information was there. And, I was trying the right things. I just didn't have the framework to make sense of it.

The moment things started to click, I was driving.

That year, our whole family was into mountain biking - proper downhill, chairlift, helmet-and-pads stuff. As a side note, I later realised my lifetime of chasing action sports, was a craving for adrenline as an antidote to constant fatigue.

The mountain biking trails we loved were about six hours away, which meant long car trips, most holidays. On one of those drives, the family either on their phones or asleep, I was listening to audiobooks about sleep and the nervous system.

I'd been kicking something around in my mind for a while. I'd had days on the mountain that were the most physically intense of my life. Hours of full adrenaline, every sense switched on, pushing harder than I ever had in a gym. And yet I wasn't sleeping better on those nights. In fact, I consistently slept worse. That just didn't make sense.

But somewhere on that drive, it started to.

Cortisol, adrenaline, the nervous system, stress and how the body reacts to it. I'd read about all of it in isolation, and now it started connecting. I started to see a pattern.

I already knew that a stressful day at work led to a bad night's sleep. But if daytime stress causes a nighttime problem, what about everything else that I did during the day?

Without realising it, I'd been thinking about sleep as a night-time problem with a magic switch. All I had to do was find it. But the night was never the problem. It was just where I noticed it.

I was beginning to see how much more the daytime mattered.

It reframed everything. Every tip I'd tried, every supplement, every bedtime routine. None of it was wrong exactly. Some of it was even well-supported by science.

Some people can try 'one thing' and swear by it. Most likely, they had a solid foundation. The tip worked because most of the system was already working. For me, the whole system was {~~out of alignment~>working against itself~~}. I spent all day doing the wrong things, so trying their 'one thing' was never going to work.

And if the day was where the problem existed, that's where the work had to be.

That was when it became obvious. I needed to build a 'good day'. The problem was, I didn't know what a good day looked like. And I couldn't find anything that showed me.

I didn't need another book of tips or a rigid sleep protocol. I had a demanding job, a family, a commute, and the kind of tiredness that makes even simple things a chore. I needed something that held my hand, which worked within a modern busy schedule.

It had to be holistic. It had to be incremental. My willpower was running on empty. It had to be flexible enough that one bad week didn't unravel the whole thing.

Since a program like this was nowhere I could find, I decided I had to build it.

The starting point was biology.

If I was going to build something that actually worked, I needed to understand the system I was trying to support. Not at a research level, just enough to make sense of what had already been found and figure out what I could realistically do about it.

It began with re-reading and self-experimenting. Trying things, measuring the response, adjusting. It wasn't a clean clinical trial. I couldn't always tell what was working and what wasn't. I paid close attention to how I felt during the day: energy levels, mood, the quality of my mornings. My WHOOP later help me to refine it too, though how that came about is a story for later. Because there were so many variables, it was never quite a process of: do this, get that.

The whole process took years. Years of not knowing what I was looking for, trying the wrong things, measuring (and guessing), discarding and starting again. I was building the program from scratch.

And after all those years, I finally found what worked, and more importantly, what I could realistically sustain.

Fortunately, you don't have to go through all of that. The program now exists. Laid out in this book.

This program changed my life. And, I believe that if you follow it as it's laid out, it will change yours too. Far faster than the years it took me. In the coming chapters I'll give you a sense of what to expect, and roughly when, before you begin.

I'm sharing this, with all the figuring out already done. You just need to put in the effort to follow along.

If you are still hoping for 'one thing I can do for a good night's sleep', a program might sound like a lot. But compared to months, years or decades of searching without any permanent success, it's nothing.

You won't have to wait until the end to know it's working. Somewhere partway through the program, you'll see a noticeable improvement in your sleep, and it will break a story you've been telling yourself for a long time. The one that goes: I will never be a good sleeper.

Once that story breaks, the motivation to finish the program takes care of itself. 

My version of that took longer. I was doing and testing the program both at the same time. But it came. And eventually, the insomnia was gone.

And sleep was just the beginning. 

Things I'd carried for years began to lift.

The anxiety went. Not dramatically, not overnight, but one day that background weight was lighter. The minor bouts of depression, gone. The irritability (this-shouldn't-annoy-me-but-it-does), much less. My ability to handle stress improved. I hadn't realised how much of my stress response was caused by exhaustion.

The recurring dreams stopped too, with no therapy and no digging into what they meant. I just started sleeping properly, and one day I realised they were gone.

Energy started coming back. And then something I hadn't felt in a very long time. Excitement. Genuine anticipation about things I was going to do on the weekend. I hadn't experienced that emotion in more than a decade.

But the one that meant the most was even simpler.

I started showing up. For my wife, for my kids, for the people around me. Not just physically present. Actually there. The gap I'd been managing for years, between who I was and who I wanted to be, had closed. Those ordinary moments that should have made me happy finally did.

I still have bad weeks. I'm not a perfect sleeper. Some weeks the habits slip and I feel it. But I now understand why, and I know what to do about it.

I've gone from years of broken, poor-quality sleep to 7+ hours of good quality sleep most nights.

It has changed my life.

I'm not sharing this because my story is unique. The specifics are mine: the mountain biking, the trial and error, the shape of my sleep. But the pattern will be familiar. The searching, the failed fixes, the exhaustion that makes everyday things feel like way too much. If any of that resonated, you're in the right place.

What I hope you take from this chapter is the possibility.

Maybe you don't have to accept: I'm just a bad sleeper. Maybe this isn't the rest of your life.

The moment where you stop hoping it might be true and actually feel it. That's closer than it probably feels right now.

And the biology is where it starts. I'm going to explain it in a way that you might not have seen before. The simplicity might make neuroscientists cringe, but we're not neuroscientists. We just need to understand sleep well enough to get a meaningful improvement.

Take in as much as you can, it's worth it.
