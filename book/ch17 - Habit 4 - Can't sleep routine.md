# Phase 3 – Train your body clock
# Chapter 17 - Habit 4: Can't sleep routine

There's a question that most people with sleep problems ask first, before they think about hormones or habits or any of it: what do I do when I can't sleep?

Tonight, when I'm lying in the dark, mind already running, the clock ticking toward an hour that means tomorrow is going to be very difficult. What do I actually do with that?

When you notice your mind racing, or frustration starting to rise, and you just know that staying in bed won't work anytime soon, get up.

Move to a dimly lit space. Nothing that tells the brain the day has restarted. And do something mildly amusing and undemanding. Low thinking TV, listen to music or bedtime stories, anything that is (and this is the key) only just engaging enough to distract you from your thoughts. But not engaging enough to activate you.

The goal is to get out of your own head, but not to call the Wake Executives (dopamine and cortisol) back into the office. Nothing so interesting that you can't stop watching, or reading. No dramas that have you contemplating life, no reality TV that gets you annoyed by certain characters. Definitely no suspense or thrillers. Not even interesting documentaries.

Wait until your eyes are feeling really heavy (however long that might be) then return to bed. Or just sleep on the couch. For me, once all the other habits kicked in, I rarely needed to get out of bed in the middle of the night, so any association formed with the couch wasn't a problem. Returning to bed is better, but sleeping is what matters most. If you wake again, repeat. It works the same way at 11pm as it does at 4am.

There's a fear worth discussing: if I get up, I'll lose even more sleep. In my experience, it rarely plays out that way. The frustration loop steals more hours of sleep than getting up ever does. If the dim room and low engagement do their job (which, more often than not, they do) sleep comes back sooner than it would have lying in bed frustrated. If not, you've spent those hours resting instead of fighting. Lying in bed struggling offers neither.

For a long time, my answer was the opposite: stay in bed. Keep the lights off. Don't get up. That will only wake you further. Just lie there, stay still, and eventually sleep will come back. It seemed like the only logical position. Getting up felt like admitting defeat, like telling the body it was acceptable to be awake at 2am. So I stayed, lay there in the dark and waited. Dead tired. Wide awake.

The problem was that waiting wasn't resting. It was thinking. 

**Why staying in bed makes it worse**

Two things happen when you lie awake in bed frustrated, and both of them work against you.

The first is hormonal. By now you know cortisol doesn't care what the stressor is, and lying awake annoyed at not sleeping is a stressor like any other. The more frustrated you get, the more cortisol rises, the more alert you stay, the less possible sleep becomes. The thinking doesn't stop; at 2am it finds the thing you're most worried about, which feeds the frustration, and round it goes.

The second is about association. The brain learns by repetition. It builds connections between environments and expected states, between contexts and what the body should be doing in them. We discussed this briefly in Chapter 7. Your bedroom, ideally, is a place the brain associates strongly with sleep: you get in, the lights go off, the system begins to wind down.

Every minute spent lying awake and anxious in bed adds a data point to a different association. Bed-equals-racing-mind, instead of bed-equals-sleep. Do that enough nights in a row and the bed itself becomes a cue for wakefulness, the brain activates the moment you get into it, because that's the pattern. This is why some people with chronic insomnia find they can fall asleep easily on the couch but the moment they move to bed their mind switches on.

Every activity in bed (phone, TV, worrying) teaches the brain that bed is a place where those things happen. Sleep is the thing that gets crowded out.

The goal, then, is not to force sleep back. It's to protect what the bed means, and that means getting out of it when sleep isn't happening.

Getting up does something lying there can't: it removes the pressure to sleep. In bed, not sleeping, you are, consciously or not, struggling.

Two established therapies land on the same instruction. CBT-I (cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia) makes "don't lie in bed awake; get up" a central recommendation. ACT-I, its acceptance-based cousin, adds the why: acceptance of wakefulness, because the struggle generates more arousal than the wakefulness itself. (I only worked out the match after learning it the hard way.)

The routine is a practical version of that idea. When I can't sleep, I do this. A protocol instead of a struggle. In the language of the program, you're refusing to feed the activating side (no frustration, no dopamine) so the calming side can come back on its own.

**Getting the engagement level right**

Before I understood any of that, my first instinct was simple: get up and do something enjoyable. So I got up and swapped one mistake for another. I turned on the television.

My default was something to binge watch. Something that sounded interesting. Problem was, this held my attention for hours. I would binge episode after episode, interested enough to keep watching. Feeling drowsy but not feeling able to sleep. On the good nights sleep would eventually take hold and I'd get a couple of hours before it was time to get up. On the worst nights, I went all the way through to morning.

It wasn't until I understood what dopamine does to the system that I worked out where I'd gone wrong. Interesting content triggers dopamine, the opposite of the state you need for sleep. And anything gripping enough drives cortisol too: suspense, a cliffhanger, an irritating character. So it wasn't the swap it felt like. I'd called dopamine in without sending cortisol home, both Wake Executives, back at their desks. Engagement feels better than frustration. But neither gets you back to sleep.

In practice, that meant shows that were mildly amusing, nothing with suspense, nothing with a plot I needed to concentrate on. I started by experimenting with things like Seinfeld, Scrubs, Big Bang Theory, Brooklyn 99. These worked well. Then I found cartoons, which gave one more level of disconnection: The Simpsons, Family Guy, Archer, South Park. The first lot I would probably watch during the day, but cartoons I wouldn't. Which was precisely the point. If I really liked them, they would have kept me up.

You're possibly far more cultured than I and cartoons might sound ridiculous. The principle isn't really about television or those particular shows. It's about finding something with just enough stimulation to pull you out of your own head, but not so much that it spikes dopamine or cortisol. Think of the children's book I struggled to stay awake for in Chapter 1, different medium, same principle.

For others it might be reading, music, bedtime stories, gentle stretching. The medium doesn't matter. It just happened that television was the easiest for me (and if you're concerned about blue light, see the footnote below).

In the early days of building this habit, I would watch for hours, four, sometimes five episodes before I felt drowsy enough to go back to bed. As the other habits in the program strengthened, that number came down on its own.

I still use it, though these days I rarely need it. And when I do, I rarely make it through a single episode.

The routine works on two timescales: tonight it breaks the spiral, and over weeks it rewrites the association: bed equals sleep. The first few nights may feel worse, not better; the new pattern hasn't had enough repetitions yet. That's expected.

So the next time you're lying awake at 2am, you'll have something to do, and even when it doesn't get you straight back to sleep, it's still the better option.

Footnote: 
On blue light: I'm going to go into some detail here, because the mainstream belief is not completely accurate.

The unwanted effects of blue light depend entirely on the intensity reaching your eyes. And, there are studies which show people differ enormously in how sensitive they are. For the average person, melatonin suppression (the unwanted effect) is half its maximum at around 25 lux, though an earlier study put that nearer 100 lux. For the most sensitive people, the halfway point is as low as 6 lux, so some suppression starts below that. Light diffuses over distance, so a standard TV at three metres across the room registers around 5 lux by the time it reaches your eye. That's well under where most people are affected, though the most sensitive may still notice it.

A phone or tablet held 30cm from your face is a different story, at 25–50 lux. 

But even then (and this is the part that blue light articles tend to skip) the practical effect is small. Controlled studies showed screen light delayed sleep onset by only about 10 minutes, and what's on the screen matters as much as the light. In fact, in 2024 in the US, when the National Sleep Foundation's expert panel reviewed the evidence, they couldn't agree that pre-bed light disrupts sleep, but they could agree that pre-bed content does. This panel focused on children and teenagers.
