# Phase 2 – Remove the first three disruptors
# Chapter 14 - Habit 1: Caffeine

I was not a low-intake, well-timed coffee drinker. I was seven cups a day, last one at a bit after 3pm, and completely convinced it wasn't affecting my sleep.

Phase 1 was about getting set up: a tracker running, a supplement routine bedded in, a bedroom that works for sleep rather than against it. None of that required you to give anything up. Phase 2 does. And caffeine is first, not because it's the easiest, but because for anyone who drinks it in the afternoon it is likely the habit doing the most damage to their sleep. It's also the clearest activating input there is, which makes cutting it back, or simply moving it earlier, your first real move toward calm.

Throughout this chapter, treat energy drinks the same as coffee: the same caffeine, the same half-life, the same rules apply.

If coffee is not really your thing, either you don't drink it, or one cup in the morning and you're done, this chapter is still worth reading. The second half talks about how to actually change a habit. The mechanics. What worked when everything else didn't, and why. And if you're anything like I was, you'll assume cutting back has to be miserable. It doesn't.

For me, coffee, and lots of it, was the best way I knew how to cope with the fatigue. And for a very long time I thought that was totally fine. I usually fell asleep the moment my head hit the pillow. Most nights, in record time. Whatever the books said about caffeine clearly didn't apply to someone whose body shut down as fast as mine did. The evidence was right there. Head down, out in minutes. Coffee. Not the problem.

What I wasn't connecting was everything that came after. The wake-ups. Two hours in, sometimes three, wide awake. Lying there for an hour, two hours, occasionally the rest of the night.

And when I wasn't out like a light, I looked for different explanations: stress, a busy mind, something that had happened that day. It never occurred to me that the thing connecting all of them was sitting in a mug on my desk in the middle of the afternoon.

**What caffeine is actually doing**

The largest effect of caffeine isn't actually to give you energy. It blocks the receptors in your brain that read adenosine, the chemical that builds through the day and pushes you toward sleep. The adenosine is still accumulating; your brain just can't feel it. You're tired, but the warning light is switched off.

When the caffeine wears off and the adenosine that's been building behind it floods back, that is the afternoon slump. It exists for everyone, but caffeine can make it worse over time, turning a manageable dip into a wall. Which sends most people reaching for another coffee.

This was the loop. Tired in the afternoon. Another coffee. Wired at night. Couldn't understand why. Groggy in the morning. Straight to the machine.

Caffeine also stays in the system much longer than I realised. I read that it had a roughly five-hour half-life. I thought that meant it was gone in ten hours. Last coffee at 10am, clear by 8pm.

That's not how it works. Every five hours or so, the amount in your system halves. Two double-shot espressos before 10am sounds reasonable. Halve that: it's the equivalent of a large coffee at 3pm. Halve it again: a single shot espresso at 8pm, still circulating well into the evening, still blocking the signal your brain needs to sleep.

I'm still too scared to do the maths on my seven-cup days.

There's also the morning. Andrew Huberman argues that caffeine in the first sixty to ninety minutes after waking blocks adenosine that hasn't cleared yet. His advice is to delay the first coffee by that long. The idea hasn't been tested. Let the body do its own work first.

This is the one I'm still working on. I spent years reaching for the cup the second I woke, and a habit that old doesn't let go quickly. The evidence is thinner than I'd like, but the reasoning makes sense to me, and that's part of what keeps me at it. I can't point to a sign on my tracker yet. I did feel I had more energy in the morning without it, though that could be placebo. It's still early. Twenty years of interfering with that process takes time to surface. But I feel enough to keep pushing the first cup back.

**Five minutes earlier**

Once I understood what caffeine was actually doing, I knew something had to change. The maths was not in my favour.

The obvious move was to quit. And I tried. Every time I discovered something I thought would help my sleep I'd draw a line and commit to it. Cold turkey. I did it with real conviction, more than once. But it never lasted. A few good weeks, and then life got busy, and I was back to my old habits before I knew it.

It wasn't a willpower problem, or at least, not in the way I'd always assumed. Sleep deprivation measurably reduces your capacity for self-control. That's not a character flaw. That's biology. The problem was that cold turkey required willpower from a body too depleted to generate it. I was asking the least-resourced version of myself to make the hardest possible choices. It was never going to work.

So I tried something different.

Instead of cutting coffee out, I moved my last coffee of the day five to ten minutes earlier. That was it. And once that felt normal, I moved it another five to ten minutes earlier. Then every few days I stopped to give myself credit: *wow, I was now 30 minutes earlier than when I started.*

It took weeks. And if work got hard, or life got busy, I would pause. Holding where I was. I'd got there slowly, so the new time wasn't hard to hold. When things calmed down, I'd continue.

As my late-afternoon coffee got closer to my mid-afternoon coffee, I no longer felt like I needed it. Dropping it entirely pushed my last coffee of the day forward by close to an hour in one step. It was a little harder, but not for long. I paused until it got easy.

This was a huge win for motivation. The cup that had felt non-negotiable for a decade. I simply stopped wanting it. No hard work resisting cravings. Instead, they were just gone.

The same pattern repeated as I moved everything earlier.

The psychological link between waking up and walking straight to the machine was the last thing to go, and harder than any of the afternoon coffees. But the method was the same.

At the time of writing I'm at two cups, both before ten in the morning. I got there slowly, unglamorously, and without a single moment that felt like a breakthrough. Which is, I've come to think, exactly what sustainable change looks like. Cold turkey only ever gave me two good weeks and a story about trying. The slow version is the only thing that stuck.

Cutting back on caffeine was the first change I made that moved the needle. Noticeably. I just wish I'd been using a sleep tracker from the start. I would have loved some before and after data on that.

That's why tracking begins on day one in this program. You'll have the data I didn't.

Caffeine was never the whole problem. Cutting back removed one real obstacle. The rest (the cortisol, the timing, the habits I hadn't touched yet) were still working against me. Some nights I slept better. Others, worse. Still not where I needed to be.

If caffeine is a problem for you, the move is simple. Pick the last coffee you drink each day. And tomorrow, drink it five to ten minutes earlier than you did today.

That's it. Not a new routine and not cold turkey: one cup, moved slightly. When it feels normal, move again.

When it feels hard, hold the line where it is. Don't move again until it's easy.

If you're not ready to touch the morning coffee yet, don't. Start with the last one. The morning will come.

If caffeine isn't a problem for you, strike it off the list. Enjoy the easy win.

Either way, the same slow method applies to everything that comes next.
