# Chapter 16 - Habit 3: Food Timing

Evenings are when life happens. Sometimes a race to get dinner on time, kids through homework and bed, and a few chores. You finally get some time to relax. A snack creeps in. For a long time I didn't think any of it was relevant to what happened an hour later when I closed my eyes. If anything, I thought the opposite. 

You know that warm, heavy feeling that sometimes follows a big meal: what some people call a food coma? I loved that feeling. It felt like the body making a case for sleep. I thought I could use it.

It's a bit like alcohol though, while it might help us get to sleep, the quality suffers.

And here's why.

You already know the conditions sleep requires: temperature dropping, the calming system taking its turn, the activating system standing down.

When you eat, your body shifts into an active state: exactly what it needs to do to digest the meal. Metabolism rises, core temperature follows, insulin is released, and your gut gets to work.

The problem is simply one of timing. It's the same conditions that prevent sleep from starting properly, arriving at the worst possible hour. You're asking the body to cool down while it's generating heat: the activating system running when it should be standing down.

You'll often still drop off if the food-coma feeling is strong. But the sleep you get is lower quality and more fragmented. You might not feel the difference during the night, but you're paying a cost that shows up in how you feel the next day. The body is still processing when it should be restoring, and restoration is the whole point.

The research mostly points one way: the closer to bed you eat, the more broken the night. I use 3 hours as the target. It sits at the top of the two to three hours sleep health bodies recommend, and it's realistic to maintain.

In practice, three hours is reasonably straightforward. I aim to finish dinner by 7pm, for a 10pm bedtime. Sometimes dinner runs late and I eat late. I accept that. But I do what I can to keep those exceptions occasional.

The other situation is hunger. If it feels like it's been a long gap since dinner and you're genuinely hungry at bedtime, going to bed on an empty stomach isn't the answer. Hunger is its own cortisol trigger. Cortisol at bedtime is exactly what the rest of this program has been working to avoid. A small snack is better than lying awake, but if it becomes a regular habit, use the incremental approach to gradually move the snack earlier until the gap closes on its own.

If you do snack, composition matters less than people think. A low-carb, protein-rich snack is gentler on blood sugar than a carb snack; carbohydrates help tryptophan reach the brain to support melatonin. Both have a case.

I'll eat either. If you want a recommendation, protein wins: the safer choice if you have an underlying glucose regulation problem.

What matters more than what you eat is the size: just enough to take the edge off and get you to sleep. Not a meal, only a small bridge. Neither too full nor too hungry.

Meal timing fits where you'd expect. Light sets the clock. Sleep timing reinforces it. Food timing keeps everything on schedule.

Your starting point is simple: finish your last substantial meal three hours before bed, most nights. Snack only if you need to. Keep it small.
