# Phase 4 – Build your calming system

The focus of this phase is to train your nervous system: strengthening the calming system and tempering the activating one. Three habits, different in form, all working the same underlying system.

# Chapter 20 - Habit 7: Meditation

You may have noticed that we are nineteen chapters in before I have asked you to meditate.

That was deliberate.

Meditation is probably the single most eye-roll-inducing recommendation in sleep advice. I know that. I felt it myself. And I know that if I had opened this book with it, a number of you would have closed the book. So I didn't. I wanted you to have the supplements in place, the environment sorted, the caffeine and alcohol addressed, the rhythm established. I wanted you to have momentum, and results, and enough trust in the program that when I finally asked you to do the thing that looks the most like wellness cliché, you might actually try it.

When I was lying awake at two in the morning, mind going at full speed, frustrated and exhausted in equal measure, the last thing I wanted to hear was: you just need to calm down and breathe. It felt dismissive. It felt too simple. And it felt completely disconnected from what I was actually experiencing.

Because when people told me that breathing exercises had helped their racing mind, I assumed their racing mind was like a car doing ten over on a quiet country road. One tap on the brakes and it's fixed.

Mine felt like a monster truck tearing through a city street, demolishing everything in its path.

A gentle breath was not going to fix that. And back then, I was right. It wouldn't have. But I was wrong about why.

I had been thinking about meditation as a calming tool. Something you reach for in the moment, like paracetamol for a headache. Racing mind arrives, apply breathing, racing mind subsides.

That's not what it is.

There's a more careful version of the same idea, and it's one of the most common explanations you'll hear: that you calm down by signalling to your body, in the moment, that it's not under threat. Reassure the system, and it stands down. For some people, on some nights, I'm sure that's exactly right. It just never covered all of mine.

Plenty of nights I wasn't registering a threat. I was wide awake and busy: planning, solving, improving something. You'll remember from Chapter 7 that the nervous system doesn't separate threat from productivity; both feel urgent, and both get cortisol. Telling myself I was safe did nothing for those nights, because there was no threat to talk myself out of.

What both kinds of night shared wasn't a threat. It was a calming system too weak to bring me down. Not broken, just not well trained. So a racing mind isn't something to soothe in the moment. It's a strength problem: never the content of the thoughts, but the capacity to come down from them. And you can't build that capacity in the moment. A few quick push-ups right before you lift something heavy won't get it off the ground.

But do those push-ups every day? That's different. That's strength training.

Breathing (done consistently, even if done badly) is nervous system strength training.

At first, I was terrible at it. My mind wandered constantly. I'd lose focus after 30 seconds and spend the rest of the session aware that I was failing to meditate rather than actually meditating. Some sessions I gave up halfway through.

I kept going anyway. And slowly, something shifted.

One night, at the typical wake-up time, I did a meditation… and it helped. Just a little. Typically, a night-time wake-up meant at least an hour or two awake. But this time it calmed me. My mind still wandered, but I was able to return it more easily. I didn't quite get back to sleep. But I was lying there a lot calmer than I ever had before. And that was enough for me to feel like it was a turning point. I had proof the system was responding. And this was just a couple of weeks in.

If you asked me to rank all the things I did to rebuild my sleep (the supplements, the environment, the caffeine reduction, the morning routine, the evening routine, all of it) I would have to think carefully about most of the list. Not about this. Meditation would take first place instantly.

It was also the hardest habit to build, and the results took a couple of weeks before I noticed much. But the changes that followed (the middle-of-the-night recoveries, the stress that stopped running for hours, the nervous system that gradually learned to stand down) are why.

Your nervous system runs in two broad modes. You already know them: we've been calling them the activating and calming systems since the start. Here's the biology underneath them.

The first: a sympathetic dominant state, your activating system. It's often called fight or flight. High alert, adrenaline up and cortisol close behind, the body primed and ready. This is go-mode. Work stress, life pressure, keeping you safe, getting you through a busy day.

The second: a parasympathetic dominant state, your calming system. It's often referred to as rest and digest. Cortisol dropping, the body doing the maintenance work. Shifting you out of go-mode. It might be doing nothing, or it might be doing things just not at 100 miles an hour. This is the relaxed state. If you can remember what that feels like.

Most people with persistent sleep problems spend too much time stuck in go-mode, always on alert. The calming system rarely gets a turn, so it weakens from lack of use until it can't bring them back. The two are supposed to take turns: one speaks, the other listens, then they swap. Instead, the activating system has grown so loud it never stops talking. And the calming system can barely get a word in.

Meditation, done consistently, builds that muscle. By gradually strengthening the calming system, you are teaching it to hold its ground, and the activating system to back off. Once it's strong enough to take its turn again, you will have it when you need it, and reaching for it in the middle of the night will start to work.

This might sound a bit abstract, but the calming system has an anatomy. A physical pathway it actually runs through.

Its primary channel is the vagus nerve. A long nerve that runs from your brainstem down through your throat, your heart, your lungs, your gut. When you slow your breathing down, when you extend the exhale, when you bring your attention to the sensations in your body rather than the thoughts in your head, you are directly stimulating that nerve. It's not a metaphor. The slow exhale mechanically activates the vagus nerve through the diaphragm and the lungs.

But the vagus runs both ways (it doesn't only carry calming signals down to the body, it carries information up from it) so simply attending to those sensations works the nerve from the other side.

Of all the organs the vagus nerve connects to, the lungs are unique: the only organ you can take off autopilot. When the body is genuinely calm, breath slows on its own. Reverse engineer that: slow the breath deliberately, and the rest of the system responds, the vagus nerve sending a safety signal to the activating system.

This connects back to Chapter 7, and to cortisol. Cortisol won't stop just because you ask it to; there's no direct switch. But the vagus nerve is a back channel to the system that governs it. A trained, well-exercised one helps turn down the cortisol response. When that happens at night (as the result of consistent daily training) the mind slows down because the Wake Executive, cortisol, has finally left the building.

Which means the breathing isn't asking your mind to be quiet. It's changing the chemistry that makes quiet possible.

So how much of it do you need?

From my own experience: 5 minutes is too short. The session ends before the system has had time to shift. 10–15 minutes is the sweet spot: long enough to feel something, short enough to repeat often. Beyond that, the returns diminish and the barrier to do it goes up.

For meditation, consistency beats duration.

The person who meditates for 10 minutes every day will build a stronger calming system than the person who does 30 minutes once every few days.

Don't aim for perfect sessions. Aim for sessions. The next question is which kind.

There are many types of meditation. I tried several. What I'm going to describe is the one I found most effective and easiest to stick with, and I recommend it for one reason: the direction the signal travels.

When the activating system is running the show, signals are flowing one way: brain to body, keeping everything on alert.¹ To shift out of that state, you need to reverse the direction. Body to brain. Feeding the brain information from the body (sensation, breath, physical presence) rather than letting the brain continue to drive. This is what pulls the calming system back online.

This matters for choosing a method. Meditation that keeps you in your head (visualisation, affirmations, thinking your way to calm) keeps the signal flowing brain to body. Both have value. But body-based meditation runs the other way, body to brain.

Which is why the body scan is where I start.

The idea is simple. Move your attention slowly through your body, one area at a time. As you inhale, imagine that area filling, not dramatically, just a quiet awareness of it. As you exhale, feel it soften. In the pause after the exhale, notice whatever is there: tension, warmth, a feeling you can't describe. Doesn't matter, just feel it. Stick with this body part until it feels heavier than when you began. Then move to the next area.

You are not trying to relax each part of the body by force. You are attending to it. The attention itself is the signal, and which direction it travels is the whole point. Telling yourself *I am here, I am safe, the threat has passed* is brain to body: a message in words, pushed down from the top. The body scan has no words at all: you feel what is there, acknowledge it, let it be, and the sensation itself carries the message up.

This approach is similar to Yoga Nidra, if you have come across that. But it doesn't really need a name or a tradition behind it. Move through the body. Breathe and feel. That's it.

For the breathing itself, keep it simple. There is a lot of discussion around specific breathing patterns: counted ratios, complex sequences, techniques with names. I tried several and found most of them too rigid. And they put my attention on getting the pattern right rather than on the body, which is where the attention should be.

So this pattern is intentionally simple: inhale slowly through the nose, exhale slowly through the nose, add a gentle pause before you start again.

Nasal breathing helps in two ways. First, it helps you slow down. It's much harder to over-breathe through your nose than through your mouth, and a slow, deep breath simply takes longer to draw in. The pace regulates itself. Second, the nose itself seems to calm the body. In small studies, breathing through the nose rather than the mouth, at the same pace, nudged blood pressure down and shifted the body further towards rest. And meditators came out of slow nose breathing calmer than from the same breathing through the mouth.

When you inhale, your heart rate speeds up. When you exhale and pause, it slows down. With the exhale and pause longer than your inhale, there's more time slowing down than speeding up. That's exactly what we are trying to do.

A rough guide: 5 seconds in, 5 seconds out, 5-second pause. But don't force it, especially the pause. If it feels strained, you have gone too far. Find the slowest rhythm that feels natural and stay there.

The environment matters too, specifically, what you listen to. I strongly recommend background audio. Ideally with noise-cancelling earbuds or headphones. If not, a phone playing quietly near your head will work.

The first reason is the more obvious: it helps to block out background noise. A barking dog, a car outside, people talking, can pull you out of a session. Consistent background audio reduces that variable.

The second is conditioning. Use the same audio every time, something you'd never normally listen to, so you don't carry any other association into the session. Personally, I search for '528 Hz'. Some claim the frequency itself aids sleep, but the more important point is this: I've made it my meditation music. Over time my brain has come to recognise it as a signal for calm, even before the session has properly begun.

On position: what matters most is that your position is different enough from your normal waking state that your brain registers a shift. For me: flat on my back, no pillow, one hand on my chest, one on my stomach, earbuds in. That's it. No special posture, no particular setting. The same position every time. Which is part of the point.

As for when: I use three triggers. Getting into bed at night. Waking in the morning, before I get up. And if I wake during the night and can't get back to sleep. The same triggers every time, the same simple lying position: that's what makes it stick.

Once a day is the minimum. Less than that, and you probably won't see any results. Twice a day will meaningfully speed up the progress. Three times is a bonus.

The morning session is training. You are not trying to feel calm. You are doing the work.

The evening and middle-of-the-night sessions are different. You want the calm, and you are reaching for it. But if it doesn't arrive (if sleep doesn't follow) that is still a training session.

If you're working through the middle-of-the-night routine from Chapter 17, meditation now belongs at the front of it. Before you get up (before anything else) try the body scan meditation first. If it works, you've stayed in bed and avoided the disruption altogether. If it doesn't, nothing is lost. Get up and follow the rest of the Chapter 17 routine from there. Meditation doesn't replace it. It sits in front of it as a first attempt at getting back to sleep.

That is the thing I most want you to take from this chapter. You are not trying to win at meditation. There is no correct experience. You are training a system you cannot see directly, and the evidence won't show up in the sessions themselves.

It will show up at two in the morning. You'll reach for it, and it will work. A few weeks in, you'll get back to sleep without needing it at all. Keep going (morning and night) and it starts doing its work during the day too. You'll arrive at bedtime with the Wake Executive, cortisol, already clocked off, and the nights start taking care of themselves.

Meditation didn't fix my insomnia on its own. But it definitely moved the needle. The greatest benefit wasn't calming me down in the moment. The greatest benefit came from making the calming system strong enough to do its job.

That's the muscle. Keep building it.
