# Chapter 13 - Your Sleep Environment

If you've spent any time reading about sleep online, you'll have come across bedroom environment. It's one of the most reliably hyped topics in sleep hygiene: blackout blinds, white noise machines, cooling mattress pads, temperature-controlled everything. The promise that optimising your bedroom will transform your sleep.

I want to offer a more honest version of that argument.

I didn't approach my bedroom environment the way the internet suggested, a 'sleep sanctuary' complete with incense, candles and sounds of the rainforest. I just looked at the obvious things that were most likely disturbing my sleep and made some adjustments. I did it simply because it made sense to remove friction: the effort was minimal, and once done, I didn't have to think about it again.

That's the only argument I'm making in this chapter. Your bedroom environment won't fix your sleep by itself. But it definitely contributes. So go ahead and remove the things that are working against you.

**Light is the easiest thing to fix**

The science on light and sleep is straightforward. When light enters the bedroom during the night, your body reads it as a signal, almost morning, time to start waking up. It doesn't take much. Even low-level light can put your system into a state of mild alertness that runs quietly in the background all night, reducing the quality of sleep without you ever fully waking. You don't need to read deeply into the research to act on this one. Less light is better. It's worth stopping what you can.

In our previous house we had blackout blinds. They worked reasonably well, but they sat slightly off the wall, with gaps around the edges, and on bright mornings or in rooms facing streetlights, those gaps let in more light than you'd expect. Good, but not great.

In our current house I had recessed blackout shutters installed directly into the window frame. About as close to zero outside light as you can get. Whatever your solution might be, the target is a faint silhouette of your hand when you hold it up.

Achieving that target means no small light sources either: clocks, chargers, standby LEDs, anything with a glow. A small piece of tape directly over the LED is all you need. It's not glamorous but it works perfectly.

**Temperature is about control, not perfection**

Temperature is wired into the biology of sleep itself (your body has to cool down to fall asleep and to stay there), and the core science behind it is well established.

Most popular sources quote an optimal range of somewhere between 18 and 20 degrees Celsius (roughly 65 to 68 degrees Fahrenheit), with around 18 degrees (about 65 Fahrenheit) most often singled out as the target. I think that advice could be better, for two reasons.

First, it says nothing about what you're wearing or sleeping under. It really depends on your clothes and your bedding. But a number on the thermostat can't tell you whether your duvet is too thick, or whether tonight calls for long pyjamas or just a singlet and shorts.

Second, and more important: if you don't have air conditioning (and I didn't), hitting that 18-degree target is often simply impossible. Telling someone to set a room they can't control to a precise temperature isn't helpful advice. It's one more obstacle stacked against them.

So here's the version that actually helped me, the bit beyond the headlines.

The room itself is only one factor, and not the one to fixate on. What matters is that the whole setup (room temperature, what you're wearing, your bed coverings) lets your body drop and maintain a comfortable skin temperature throughout the night.

The temperature that actually counts isn't the air in the room. It's the warm pocket trapped between your skin and your covers: your bed climate.

Before you fall asleep, your core body temperature begins to drop by around half a degree to a degree Celsius (about one to two degrees Fahrenheit). It's part of how sleep is initiated. Remember the convergence of signals in that narrow window, the one that creates the strongest pull toward sleep? A falling core temperature is one of them. Your body needs to shed heat outward through your hands and feet, and conditions that are too warm prevent that from happening efficiently, making it harder to fall asleep.

And the job isn't done once you're out. Your body has to hold that lower temperature all night. Too warm and it struggles, fragmenting sleep and pushing you toward awakenings, especially during REM sleep, when your body largely stops regulating its own temperature and is left at the mercy of the room. It's perhaps why there's a common belief that a hot, stuffy night brings vivid, restless dreams.

So how warm is too warm? It depends almost entirely on your covers. The clearest illustration comes from studies of people sleeping near-naked, just shorts, no covers at all. For them, the ideal room temperature turned out to be around 29 degrees Celsius. Strip away the bedding and the room can be far warmer than the headline numbers suggest.

Which leads to a conclusion that sounds wrong but isn't: a "perfect" 18-degree bedroom can give you worse sleep than a warm 29-degree one, if at 18 degrees you're buried under a duvet that's too thick.

So the practical target is simple: get your bedroom to roughly 29 degrees or below, which most rooms manage most of the year. From there, you tune the bed climate with the two things you can actually control: your bedding and what you wear.

If you're lucky enough to have air conditioning in the bedroom, this might be easier to manage. Same bedding each night, same thermostat temperature.

If you don't have air conditioning, and I didn't, the good news is that I rebuilt my sleep without it.

Where I landed was several thin layers of cotton blankets rather than a single heavy duvet. In practice it means I can adjust easily depending on the room temperature each night. Push a layer off, pull one back on, until the temperature feels right.

On the hot nights, when there are no more layers to take off, I use a large fan positioned to push cool air from another room toward the bedroom. Not perfect, but enough of a difference that my body temperature can do what it needs to.

**Noise is more individual**

Of these environmental factors, noise depends most on your personal circumstances. What matters is whether sound is interrupting your sleep. Sudden sounds in particular can either pull you out of sleep entirely, or pull you out of deep sleep without fully waking you, reducing your sleep quality.

The first thing worth doing is identifying anything actually disturbing you. For some people it's traffic. For others it's a partner snoring, a neighbour's air conditioner, people up later than you slamming doors. Ideally you want to eliminate the source. If you can't, earplugs are the go-to.

In our case, it was the dog. We had a neighbour who often came home at 10.30pm. Just at the time the house was quiet, and I was drifting off to sleep. Barking rang through the house. Boom. Wide awake. Remember what I mentioned earlier about missing my sleep window?

I researched barking collars and landed on a citronella collar, a small spray when the dog barks, interrupting the behaviour without harming the dog. It worked. The barking dropped off and my sleep stopped being interrupted. Well worth the cost... except when we forget to put it on.

I'm not suggesting everyone needs a citronella collar. You might not even have a dog. The point is more general: whatever is disturbing your sleep, find a way to deal with it. Put things over your windows to block outside noise. Teach your teenagers how to close doors quietly. Sleep is hard enough already. Don't put up with things you can fix.

**And finally: the TV**

A television in the bedroom works against you in two ways.

Firstly, it introduces stimulation: news, drama, interesting series, content designed to hold your attention at the moment your nervous system should be winding down.

Secondly, your brain learns by association. TV teaches it that the bed is also for being awake and entertained, not just sleeping. The light output isn't really the main concern here. The stimulation and the association are doing the damage.

If you've got a TV in the bedroom, take it out. The bed is for sleep; remove anything that tells your brain otherwise. There's a fuller version of this in Chapter 17, where it matters most, but for this chapter, the simple version is enough.
