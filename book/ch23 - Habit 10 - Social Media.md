# Phase 5 – Remove the final three disruptors

Phase 2 cleared the biggest disruptors. Phase 5 clears the rest.

These habits are subtler: each generating its own quiet cortisol signal, interfering with the body's ability to cool and settle, or feeding the Wake Executives in ways that compound through the night. They come last not because they matter less, but because once you've made progress on your sleep and restored a little willpower, they're easier, and the effect, though quieter, is one you'll notice.

# Chapter 23 - Habit 10: Social Media

Social media is the first habit in Phase 5, and probably the one you've heard the most about already.

Hydration and sugar, the rest of this phase, are about what you put into your body. Social media is about what you put into your mind.

I knew it wasn't good for my brain. Everyone says so. For a long time I thought the problem was blue light, and I assumed the filter on my phone was solving it. It was only when I started reading past the headlines and into the studies themselves that I found there was more to it than that.

"Blue light is the villain" was the early headline, and it wasn't quite right. The light is real: it can suppress melatonin and nudge the body clock later. But it's not the whole story. More recent studies suggest that what's on the screen may play the bigger role: stimulating or emotionally engaging content may delay sleep onset more than light exposure alone, though the clearest evidence is in children and teenagers. So you can't blue-light-filter your way out of it.

The catch is, it never feels like a problem. You pick up the phone and within thirty seconds you're laughing, or outraged, or interested in something. You feel alert. Engaged. Usually better than you did before you picked it up. That's the whole point. The problem isn't that it feels good. The problem is when, how much, and what it's doing to the two Wake Executives running in the background while you scroll.

When I actually looked at what I was watching, the picture got uncomfortable.

My feed was the same outrage machine from Chapter 8: politics, this-side-versus-that-side commentary, the watch-this-guy-get-what-he-deserves clips. On some level I knew it was cheap trash (I'm smarter than that), but I kept getting dragged in.

A spare two minutes in the kitchen, a wait at the school pickup line, the gap between dinner and bed, the moment I'd put the kids down: phone out, thumb moving. I wasn't watching it because I was bored. Some part of my system just wanted the next hit, and the next, and the next.

This is the dopamine-and-cortisol trap from Chapter 8, in its purest form: every piece of content lands a small hit (curiosity from the things that interest you, cortisol from the things that provoke you) and most feed content delivers both at once. Late at night, that's activating: both Wake Executives running on full, priming you for novelty and threat exactly when you need to be winding down.

The algorithm learns what you engage with: what you click, what you watch to the end, what you react to. It has no opinion about your sleep or your mental health. It just serves you more of what you've already responded to. And we respond most reliably to content that makes us feel something: outrage, shock, indignation. It's human nature. The platform didn't create it. It just found it and tapped in.

The sleep window doesn't wait. The longer you stay in the feed, the more of it you spend in the wrong state. You scroll straight past it, the way Chapter 8 warned. You're tired but wired. By the time you put the phone down, the conditions that were lining up to carry you into sleep have already shifted.

The worst version of this is the middle of the night. Waking briefly is normal. Almost everyone does it. But the phone is right there, and you tell yourself you'll just check the time. Within seconds you've opened something. Within a minute you're activated. What should have been a moment's stir back into sleep becomes a long stretch of full wakefulness, or worse, the night's sleep is gone.

That one behaviour (picking up the phone in the middle of the night) is one of the most disruptive things someone who sleeps badly can do.

The first step is non-negotiable. If you wake in the middle of the night, do not check your phone. Leave it face down. That single behaviour change, before anything else, is the most important move in this chapter. Even a glance at a notification is enough. If sleep doesn't come back, the can't-sleep routine from Chapter 17 is what takes over: the phone is never part of it. Everything else can be worked on gradually. This one can't wait.

Steps two and three are daytime habits. The good news is that the feed you have is largely a reflection of what your nervous system has been responding to, which means it can change.

The second step is awareness before reduction. Before you try to use your phone less, change what you're using it for. Start noticing which content activates you, winds you up, leaves you agitated, and stop clicking on it. This doesn't take discipline. You now know what it's doing to your cortisol while you watch it, and that makes the decision to scroll past it a little easier.

Then start clicking toward things that interest you instead. Content that engages you without working you up. The difference matters, not just the amount of time you spend on the phone. And the encouraging part is that the feed responds quickly. You're not fighting the platform. You're redirecting it. Once the feed changes, the pull toward spiking content weakens on its own.

The third step is reduction. Use the phone less, but give it time. What you watch matters as well as how much, so start with step two today. Let step three wait until the new feed has settled, which should reduce the pull at least a little. Then cut the time down too. Your evenings are already handled: after Chapter 19, the phone is away once the wind-down begins. So this step is about the daytime scrolling: the spare minutes, the queues, the gaps between things. Hold strong and keep resisting the urge to click the old content. The moment you start engaging with it again, the feed notices.

For me the shift looked like this.

I stopped clicking on politics. Stopped engaging with the debate videos, the outrage compilations, all of it. When it came up (and it still came up, because the algorithm doesn't change overnight), I scrolled past without stopping. Just with the knowledge of what clicking it was actually costing me.

And I started clicking on things I genuinely loved. Mountain biking. Surfing. Brewing beer. The algorithm rarely served me these things, but the more deliberately I clicked toward them, the more they came.

I stayed away from near-miss and crash compilations in mountain biking: the content matters, not just the topic. I was after things that calmed me, not wound me up.

Within a few days the feed started to change. Within a week it looked different. The outrage content got crowded out. The stuff I knew was better for me came forward. I hadn't deleted anything. I'd just changed what I was telling the algorithm I wanted.

So tonight: leave the phone face down. Tomorrow: start noticing what you're clicking on and why. The awareness comes first.
