# Chapter 22 - Habit 9: Exercise

Exercise is one of the most consistently recommended sleep interventions there is. It is also one of the most misunderstood.

The mountain biking days from Chapter 2 were the clearest example. Full adrenaline from first run to last, and at the end of every one, someone would say it: "We're going to sleep well tonight." I'd smile and say nothing. Because I knew I wouldn't. Sleeping worse, not better, dragging myself through the week that followed. I thought it was the price of doing something I loved. It wasn't. It was a mechanism I didn't yet understand.

Exercise spikes cortisol. That's not a flaw in the system. That's the point. Cortisol is what drives the effort. It sharpens focus, mobilises energy, keeps you moving when the body wants to stop. On a full day of downhill riding, I was running on it from the first run to the last. Cortisol, the Wake Executive, working flat out, doing exactly what it was designed to do.

The problem isn't the spike. The problem is what happens after.

In a well-rested body with a trained calming system, cortisol clears within a few hours of the effort ending. The system shifts. The Wake Executive clocks off. The Sleep Executives take over. That's the recovery the other dads were experiencing when they talked about sleeping well that night.

My calming system wasn't strong enough to do that. I was carrying the cortisol from a full day of maximum effort straight through the evening and into the night. The Wake Executive wasn't clocking off. It was burning the candle at both ends. And alongside the cortisol, my core body temperature was staying elevated far longer than it should. Intense exercise raises body temperature significantly, and it can take anywhere from thirty minutes to two hours to return to baseline, exactly the window when the body needs to be cooling down to initiate sleep. I was arriving at my sleep window still running hot in every sense.

Three or four days of that, and the cumulative effect was written all over the following week.

Most people don't have days like that. But the principle applies well beyond mountain biking.

The fitness industry has spent years building a culture around intensity. Bootcamp classes, HIIT sessions, no-pain-no-gain messaging, social media feeds full of people pushing harder and going longer. For generally healthy, well-rested people, a lot of that is fine. The body can handle it. The calming system is strong enough to bring cortisol back down and reset before the sleep window opens.

For someone who is already sleep-deprived, it's a different story. The calming system is already weak. Recovery is already slow. And the people most likely to be chasing intense exercise as a sleep solution are often the ones least equipped to recover from it, particularly when that exercise is happening in the evening, which for many people is the only window they have.

You're exhausted, you've read that exercise improves sleep, you drag yourself to a seven pm bootcamp class, and you go hard because that's what the culture demands. Cortisol spikes. Body temperature rises. You get home at nine, shower, try to wind down, and wonder why you're lying awake at eleven with your heart still racing.

What went wrong was the intensity and the timing, and nobody told you that those two things matter as much as they do.

Understanding that was the turning point. But understanding it and knowing what to do with it are different things. This is where my WHOOP changed how I thought about exercise.

WHOOP tracks both strain and recovery. Strain is a measure of cardiovascular load across the day: every activity contributing to a cumulative score. Recovery is calculated each morning based on how well you slept, your resting heart rate, heart rate variability, and a few other markers. The two talk to each other. Your daily strain target is set in response to your recovery score. High recovery, higher target. Low recovery, lower target.

I expected to have to work hard to hit my targets. I didn't.

My recovery scores were low enough (my baseline depleted enough from months of poor sleep) that moderate exercise was registering as significant strain. A brisk walk. A casual bike ride. A swim at a pace I could hold a conversation through. These were moving the needle in ways I hadn't expected. I didn't need to destroy myself to hit the number. I just needed to move.

It varies day to day, but often the morning walk plus a short, light gym session or a yoga class would hit my daily target. Just a modest raise in heart rate for roughly an hour in total.

That surprised me. I'm far from a fitness model, but at fifty I like to think I'm on the fitter side of average. In my depleted state, that was all it took. If that was enough for me, it's likely enough for a lot of people.

A great unexpected side effect was how this changed my relationship with exercise motivation. The internal argument I'd been having for years (just do it, you know it's good for you, stop making excuses) got quieter. It's easier to talk yourself into a moderate session than a brutal one. The barrier to getting there drops. The session itself is more enjoyable. And the sleep that follows is better, which means the next day's recovery score is higher, which means you can do a little more. Eventually, you want to do more. The feedback loop runs in the right direction for once.

So what does this look like in practice?

The morning walk is already your baseline: it's been there since Chapter 18. But if you want to build on that, if you want intensity, the morning is the best time.

Make the walk a run. Take the bike out. Hit the gym before work. Whatever form intensity takes for you, front-load it. But front-loading doesn't mean transplanting the bootcamp to seven in the morning. For someone already sleep-deprived, all-out effort is likely still too much whatever the hour. Think one level below your maximum: a run rather than sprint intervals, a gym session you push through but could repeat tomorrow.

The WHOOP logic applies: let your recovery score set the ceiling, not the class. Morning exercise works with your cortisol curve rather than against it: the cortisol awakening response is already rising when you wake up, and exercise channels that into effort rather than creating a fresh spike later in the day that has to clear before you can sleep. Studies also link regular exercise to lower cortisol levels and better sleep quality over time.

Once the afternoon arrives, the calculus changes. The research recommends finishing vigorous exercise at least four hours before sleep onset, but most of those studies were done on healthy adults without sleep disorders. People whose calming systems were already working. If you're reading this book, you're probably not that person.

My own experience, and it's self-experimentation rather than science, points to at least six hours as the safer margin. For most people, that means finishing before 4pm (assuming a 10pm bedtime). This is a good rule of thumb, but earlier in the day is still better, especially when your recovery baseline is already low.

Anything after 4pm should be low-intensity. A walk. A gentle swim. Stretching. Movement that asks something of the body without demanding a stress response in return.

On low intensity, yoga deserves a closer look, because it actively trains the nervous system while you move. It falls under what's sometimes called somatic exercise: movement practised with deliberate awareness of the body and breath. Paying attention to the physical experience of moving. How the breath changes. Where tension lives. What releases when you stop pushing.

That's the same principle as the body scan. The same attention, just moving rather than still. Your body is active, but your nervous system is calm. Where meditation trains the calming system at rest, somatic movement trains it in motion. More signals for the same bottom-up channel: more ways to stimulate the vagus nerve and build your calming system.

Once I understood that, something I'd half-noticed since I first tried yoga made sense: the nights I did it, I generally slept better.

Tai chi works on the same principle. So does qigong. The specific practice matters less than the quality of attention: slow movement, breath connection, awareness of what the body is doing rather than just asking it to perform.

I replaced two intense evening sessions a week with two yoga sessions. The difference was meaningful, as if I could feel the calming system getting a workout it hadn't been getting before.

I recommend starting with one session a week any time that works. Build to two. That's enough to feel the difference.

Sometimes an intense evening session can't be avoided. Evening team training. A late class you committed to. Life doesn't always arrange itself around the ideal exercise window, and the answer isn't to stop moving: it's to manage the aftermath as deliberately as you can.

After those mountain-biking days my protocol was: a plunge in a cold stream if one was nearby, a cool shower if not (anything to bring the body temperature down), then a long stretch in front of something easy on TV, then the full body scan before bed. The goal across all of it was the same: give cortisol every chance to clear, give body temperature every chance to drop. Build whatever version of the comedown you can.

The rule is simple: intensity as early as you can manage, nothing vigorous after 4pm. One or two yoga sessions a week. If an intense evening session is unavoidable, take the comedown seriously.

If you have a tracker like WHOOP, let the daily strain recommendation do the thinking for you. It knows how well your body recovered last night.
