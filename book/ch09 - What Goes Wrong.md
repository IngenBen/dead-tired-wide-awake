# Chapter 9 - What Goes Wrong

You've now met all four executives, one at a time. Let's get them in the room all at the same time.

Back in Chapter 4 I showed you this. By now it should make a lot more sense.

![[image-when-it-all-lines-up.gif]]

Through the day, the Wake Executives run the shift. Through the evening, they stand down. The Sleep Executives take over. At bedtime, sleep hormones high, wake hormones low. 

If everything lands where it should, that's all four executives working together. You feel awake at the right times, tired at the right times, and sleep when you're supposed to.

## The bad night

The other version is the executives working against each other, and it can go very wrong. Cortisol won't fall, still activated deep into the night. Dopamine keeps spiking, another hit with every phone check. Melatonin is rising, but it's weakened and outnumbered. Adenosine keeps building, and maybe sometime after midnight its strong enough that the others finally take notice. Sleep comes, but it has run out of time to do its job before morning hits.

![[image-when-it-goes-wrong.gif]]

Looking at the graph, it's overwhelming how complicated this is. Understanding that complication alone was a light-bulb moment. I finally realised that's why there is no magic fix. How did I ever expect, with so much going wrong, that just before bed I could take a pill or do one simple trick, and everything would fall back into line for a solid eight-hour sleep?

Don't worry if that's a lot to take in all at once. It will make more sense as we move through the most common symptoms of insomnia.
## Struggle getting to sleep

**Cortisol**

Through the day, your nervous system should keep returning you to baseline, so you arrive in the evening low and ready for sleep. 

Two things can stop that. Either you're activated all day (hypervigilant, anxious, stuck in go-mode), with a calming system so overwhelmed it can't bring you back down. Or the bed itself has become a trigger. Enough nights lying awake and you brain forms a subconscious association that bed is a place of thinking. Or, you develop sleep anxiety - the worry you won't be able to sleep raising your stress level. In both cases your system starts activating the moment you get in.

Whatever the case, you carry elevated cortisol through the night, fighting to keep you awake. And with high cortisol your mind won't settle.

![[image-cortisol-struggling-to-sleep.gif]]

**Dopamine**

Cortisol alone is enough to fight melatonin and adenosine. But add a quick scroll, or an engaging TV show right before bed, and now both Wake Executives are keeping your eyes open. 

Same thing in the middle of the night. A quick phone check and dopamine is right back at work, sending activating signals that fight against the Sleep Executives, making it very difficult to get back to sleep. 

![[image-struggling-to-sleep-dopamine.gif]]

**Adenosine**

And since you still haven't got to sleep, adenosine keeps building. If you do finally manage a few hours, it's only because the pressure got so strong the other hormones had no choice but to listen and stand down for a while.

![[image-struggling-to-sleep-adenosine.gif]]

## Melatonin and the sleep window

The narrow sleep window from Chapter 6 (the convergence of melatonin rising fast and adenosine high) is roughly the stretch when the pull to sleep is strongest.

If cortisol is higher than it should be, but not high enough to override this window, here is the one moment sleep can still win.

Miss it and you're lying there with time on your hands. Your mind finds something to work on, the Wake Executives get back on top, and cortisol is winning the fight again. You're awake.

And the most common way to miss it is self-inflicted: a scroll or a screen at exactly the wrong moment, which supplies enough dopamine, cortisol or both, to carry you past the window.

![[image-sleep-window.gif]]

## Waking at 2am

If you do get to sleep in your window, the next most common complaint is waking in the early hours, somewhere around 2 to 3am, depending on when you went to bed.

Around then, your circadian system starts turning the night around, preparing you for morning.

Melatonin has peaked and its production tails off. For someone who sleeps well there's still plenty in the tank to carry through to morning. For someone who sleeps badly (the weak overnight hold from Chapter 6) there's less to carry through, and the step down from the peak can be enough to weaken the signal.

Also, deep sleep, and the sleep pressure it discharges, is typically front-loaded into the first half of the night. So by the early hours of the morning much of that pressure has already eased off. Adenosine is still working, but with less force than earlier, no longer supporting  melatonin as strongly.

For someone who sleeps well, both Sleep Executives easing off at around the same time is not usually problem. Cortisol is low and only just beginning to climb. They sleep through, or surface briefly, a quick trip to the bathroom, and back to sleep. 

But for someone who sleeps badly, with cortisol already running high than it should, that same moment is the opening. The cortisol Wake Executive is suddenly strong enough to overpower the fading Sleep Executives and activate your racing-mind. Wide awake, sometimes with little chance of getting back to sleep.

## Waking up tired

Sometimes you might have a reasonable night. No fight to get down, no wake-up, or at least only a brief wake up and straight back to sleep. You had a goodnight's sleep, but you still wake up flat. Often, this is adenosine that never cleared. In earlier chapters I mentioned how it empties fastest in deep sleep. Unfortunately deep sleep is a part of sleep that is easily stolen. Anything that sends activating signals in to the night - caffeine too late, a warm room, late night screens - effects the quality of your sleep. 

The hours might have been there; but the depth wasn't. So you wake in the morning with some of yesterday's pressure still on board. This creates (or adds to) sleep debt that many people carry around. 

Sometimes a good night is actually a good night, but if it wasn't enough to clear accumulated sleep debt, you still wake up tired. 

Occasionally I’d get a stranger version of this: a much better night than usual, but the next morning I'd feel far more tired than usual. I have a theory about why, and it is only a theory. For a long time I was getting 4-5 hours a night. On a normal morning I woke up tired but with elevated cortisol. Which was probably the reason I managed to get out of bed at all. On the rare nights that I did sleep well, my guess is that cortisol stepped down that night. So I woke up feeling the full weight of my accumulated sleep debt, without the elevated cortisol to counter it.

Whatever the case, a good night or a shallow night might not be enough to clear it. You carry the leftover pressure through the next day and into the following evening. Starting the next day already behind.  Adding to the accumulated sleep debt.

![[image-wake-up-tired-adenosine.gif]]

## Same problem, every time

Different nights. One cause.

Not a flaw in you. Not a bad sleeper. Four executives working against each other, arriving at bedtime in the wrong places.

Which is the good news. The systems that get them working together can be trained. And you mostly train them during the day, not at night.

That's the program.
