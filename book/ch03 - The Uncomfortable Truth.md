# Chapter 3 - The Uncomfortable Truth

Some people swear by hemp extracts for sleep. I started reading about CBN, a compound formed when THC breaks down, supposedly even more targeted for sleep than CBD.

The research was thin. The availability was patchy. But I'd already tried the common things. This felt like the next level. Maybe I'd finally found something that could fix me. It just hadn't made it to the mainstream yet.

Because alongside the sparse science, forums posts were saying: of course you haven't heard of it. Drug companies can't patent a plant. When you're exhausted and desperate, the conspiracy starts to sound a lot like hope.

So I bought some with the quiet optimism of someone who had been disappointed before.

It definitely did something. Some nights, it helped. I fell asleep more easily. Woke up slightly less. Enough to keep me using it.

But other nights, felt like it did nothing at all. Same oil, same dose. Nothing.

That inconsistency was frustrating. Was it working? Was I imagining it? Should I take more? Try a different brand? Take it at a different time? What was different about the nights it did work?

Whatever it was doing, it wasn't a reliable fix, it was a nudge. Only on some nights, for reasons I couldn't identify or reproduce.

I'd been here before. Many times. A new study, a new supplement, a new technique. Hopeful at first, then back exactly where I started.

The problem wasn't the CBN. The problem was the question underneath.

What's a quick fix for my sleep?

It was the wrong question. That's not how sleep works. It isn't found in a product you buy or a simple hack. It's something your body does, once the conditions are right. And like recovering from an illness or an injury, it doesn't happen overnight. For sleep as broken as mine, there's no quick fix. And searching for one is exactly what that kept me stuck. 

That's the uncomfortable truth.

Not something I was happy to learn. But once I faced it, the question changed. Instead of "what's the best thing I can do to fix my sleep?" it became "what are all the things required to create a good nights sleep".

And, as I continued my research from this new frame of reference, the theory I started to form was: the key to good sleep is not finding something that will finally flip a switch. Instead its much more like getting fit. There's no shortcut. You build it gradually, from a foundation, with lots of small daily habits done consistently, over weeks.

It was like I'd been drinking, smoking, eating badly, barely moving, and I was desperately trying to find "one thing" I could do to get fit. Then I'd try "one thing" for a week or two. Not see any results, go back to my old habits and think: everyone is different... that "one thing" doesn't work for me. Then resume my quest for the next "one thing".

What kept me searching long after I should have stopped were the stories: a friend of a friend, a stranger online, someone who'd made one simple change and got a huge win.

And as we saw in the last chapter, a single change can work, but only on a foundation that's already there. If your sleep is already in reasonable shape, one well-chosen adjustment might genuinely be enough. Fix the timing of your last coffee. Cool the bedroom down. Cut the screen off earlier.

However, if you've been running on poor quality, fragmented sleep for years, if you're tired but wired most nights, if you've tried a dozen things and none of them stuck, one adjustment won't be enough. You need to train for it, the same way you'd train to get fit.

Here's the challenge though. We generally know what it takes to get fit, but nobody taught us about sleep. From a young age the basic concepts are taught in school. Move your body. Eat reasonably well. Rest and recover. Do it consistently.  

Most adults, even those not as fit as they could be, have a reasonable idea: 'I know... I should probably do a bit more of this, and a bit less of that'.

Not so with sleep. You arrive at adulthood with a sleep system you've never been shown how to train. No framework or fundamentals. Just a vague sense that eight hours is probably good and that you should probably go to bed at a reasonable time. And when the system starts to fail, the quality drops, when you're constantly waking at 2 am, the only toolkit you've been given is: Try this tip. Try that supplement. Try sleeping pills.

I don't believe it's some great conspiracy. It's simply that the majority people never have a problem. They don't understand insomnia. Go to bed, turn out the light, close your eyes, go to sleep. What's so hard? What's there to teach?

Modern culture doesn't help either. If anything, it pushes us the other way. We've built an odd kind of pride around not sleeping: the early riser who powers through on five hours, the high performer who treats sleep as a weakness, the devoted worker who wears exhaustion like a badge of honour. As if rest were a character flaw and not a biological need.

For a long time, I was caught up with that way of thinking. My response to exhaustion was to be that high performer, push through it. Sleep is optional.

However, the reality is, only a very small percent of the population can genuinely function that way.  For most of us, biology just doesn’t work like that.

And the consequences of getting it wrong aren't trivial. Poor sleep is linked to serious, long-term consequences: heart disease, diabetes, depression, cognitive decline. And driving tired can impair you as badly as driving drunk.

The stakes are high.

But I don't need to convince you to improve your sleep. You're reading this because you've already decided its a good idea. So lets get back on track... If quick fixes don't work without a foundation, what is the foundation, and how do we build it?

Through my research I had noted a crazy number of things, with and without scientific backing, that people claimed improve sleep. But I didn't have a framework that tied them all together.

I didn't know which things made the most difference. Which things need to be combined, how much, how often, or which things were just complete nonsense.  I wanted to understand the mechanisms of sleep, so that I could evaluate each of these things the mechanism they were supposed to affect.

Eventually I came to realise that once you boil it all down, underneath all of it, the whole process of creating a good nights sleep depends almost entirely on hormones.

Hormones that need to rise and fall in a predictable rhythm throughout 24 hours. That was the mechanism.

When those hormones {~~are aligned~>work together~~} (right amounts, at the right times), sleep happens naturally. When they{~~'re not~> don't~~}, no "one thing" will reliably fix it.

As I dug deeper, I discovered there are dozens of hormones involved in sleep. A long, complicated list. But I wasn't looking for a complete biological picture. I was looking for the mechanisms I could leverage. I wanted to focus only on the ones that would move the needle, the hormones that did most of the work, and the ones I could actually do something about.

That's when the pattern emerged. The list narrowed from dozens to just four.

Four hormones that sit at the top of the system. {~~When aligned~>When they're working as they should~~}, they bring the rest into order. When they're not, everything else is just treating symptoms.

That was the mechanism, which creates the foundation. Four hormones.

What they do, how they work and what disrupts them, is where we start. And most of the habits that keep them {~~aligned~>working together~~} don't require anything extraordinary. Just small adjustments in an ordinary day, done consistently.

That foundation can be built. And that's what the rest of this book is about.
