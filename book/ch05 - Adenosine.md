# Chapter 5 - Adenosine

Every book said the same thing: don't drink coffee after a certain time.

At the time, I was ignoring it. My reasoning felt solid. Some nights I fell asleep the moment my head hit the pillow. Coffee: not the problem.

It changed on a boat.

I was away with a group of friends, a dads' trip, a few days out on the water. The boat ran a tab system: every coffee and every beer was marked down against your name to settle at the end. When the bill came I noticed my coffee column was almost as long as everyone else's combined.

I did the maths. Seven cups a day.

I knew I drank a lot of coffee. I didn't know it was that much. Seeing it stacked up against everyone else was a huge wakeup call. I'd spent years telling myself coffee wasn't the problem. But surely this wasn't good.

I really needed to know what all this caffeine was doing to my body.

That's when I first came across adenosine.

From the moment you wake up, your brain starts producing adenosine, a natural byproduct of being awake. It accumulates through the day, building what is often called sleep pressure: a slow, biological push toward sleep that gets stronger with every hour you're awake.

By evening, if the system is working as it should, adenosine levels are high enough that the pressure is real and physical. Eyes heavy. Thinking slower. Bed starts calling your name.

During sleep the brain clears adenosine, fastest during deep (slow wave) sleep, which happens more in the first half of the night.

By morning, if that clearing has happened properly, that pressure has largely lifted. You wake up alert, with the cycle reset and ready to start again.

The problem is almost never a shortage of adenosine. Your body generates it continuously, all day, regardless of how the previous night went. If you've been awake all day, even sedentary, unproductive, running on fumes, adenosine has been building since the moment you opened your eyes. But you already know this. It's why you feel tired all the time.

The problem is usually one of two things, or often both. Either something is overriding adenosine and you can't get to sleep, or the pressure isn't clearing properly overnight and you wake up tired.

Overriding adenosine is an alignment problem. Chapter 4 introduces the other executives, and when they're not moving in the same direction, the signal gets drowned out. The pressure is real. Adenosine is still built up. Its just not winning the battle. You're tired, but you lie awake anyway. More on that in Chapter 7.

Adenosine not clearing overnight usually comes down to one thing above all: not enough deep sleep. When deep sleep is fragmented or suppressed, adenosine clears more slowly, not leaving enough time for the brain to finish the overnight reset. You sleep, but don't fully empty the tank.

Several things disrupt your deep sleep:

**Caffeine** was my main interference. It suppressed my deep sleep more than it stopped me falling asleep, which is why I could drop off instantly some nights, but still wake exhausted. That's Chapter 14.

**Stress** holds the brain in a lighter, vigilant state, the opposite of what deep sleep needs. The night feels thin and restless. That's Chapter 20.

**Warmth** works against you more than most realise: deep sleep needs your core temperature to drop, and a warm sleep environment makes that harder. That's Chapter 13.

**An irregular schedule** can cost you deep sleep. It's front-loaded into the first part of the night. A drifting body clock and daytime sleep can leave you short. That's Chapters 18 and 19.

**Screens** don't need to be blaring. The light, notifications, and habit of checking fragment the lighter stages and cut in to your sleep. That's Chapter 23.

Whatever your disruptor, the pattern is the same: it costs you the deep sleep that clears a lot of the tiredness, often without you noticing. Morning grogginess doesn't always mean you're not a morning person. It sometimes means clearing ran short.

For me, the loop looked like this: tired, reach for coffee, suppresses deep sleep, adenosine doesn't clear, wake up tired, reach for coffee. It felt logical but every part was making it worse.

The way out of that loop isn't forcing it. It starts with understanding what the thing you're reaching for is actually doing.

Caffeine is the clearest example. Every cup is sends an activating signal. A signal that lasts for hours. A cup late in the day activates you well into the evening, exactly when your body needs to be calming.

I did eventually reduce my caffeine. How that actually worked, what stuck when everything else hadn't, is Chapter 14. But understanding why it mattered, came first.

Did it fix my sleep? Not entirely. It wasn't the only thing fragmenting my deep sleep, but it was the largest, and removing it was where the visible progress started.

Understanding more about adenosine and deep sleep changed the question I was asking, from "why can't I sleep?" to "what else am I doing that's getting in the way?".

Adenosine is sleep pressure: the force that makes you want to sleep. What it doesn't explain by itself is why, with all that pressure built up, you can still lie awake at midnight, wired and frustrated, wondering why your body won't cooperate.

That's not an adenosine problem. That's a timing problem, and it takes more than one executive to solve it. Caffeine has a role to play there too, but that's for Chapter 7.
