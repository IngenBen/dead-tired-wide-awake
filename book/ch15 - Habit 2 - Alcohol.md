# Chapter 15 - Habit 2: Alcohol

Caffeine was my first crutch. Alcohol was my second.

They're related in a way that took me a long time to see. Both felt like they were helping, one to get through the day, one to wind down at the end of it. But both were making the underlying problem worse. And both required a compromise rather than a clean solution, because neither was something I was willing to give up entirely.

If you don't drink, this chapter asks nothing of you. It's still worth reading, to understand why that choice is quietly helping your sleep. Either way, you can cross it off as one thing you're already getting right.

For everyone else (and I'm very much in this group) the science here is uncomfortable. For your overall health, the latest position is blunt: no amount of alcohol is good for you, and the recommended level is now zero. For sleep, the direction is no different.

And once I learned that, I had a real problem. One of my hobbies is brewing beer.

Fortunately, there's a more realistic position, one that accepts alcohol is deeply entwined in how we live, and offers next best advice: drink as infrequently as possible, as little as possible, as far from bedtime as you can manage.

This I could work with.

What I'd been telling myself, up to that point, was: alcohol helps me sleep. It calms my nerves. Takes the edge off. Slows my mind. Helps me fall asleep faster. The evidence seemed obvious: a drink or two in the evening, and I felt more sleepy. Things that usually bothered me didn't matter as much. All positives. Up until I fell asleep, that is.

**What alcohol actually does**

It does help you fall asleep faster. That much is true. The problem is what it does to the rest of the night.

Alcohol sedates rather than induces natural sleep. 

The two stages you met in Chapter 11 (deep sleep for physical repair, REM for memory and emotional processing) are your restorative sleep.

In the first part of the night, alcohol does something sneaky. It doesn't rob you of deep sleep. If anything, you get more of it, pulled early, which is part of why the sleep feels so convincing. What it suppresses is REM, the dreaming sleep that handles memory and emotion. And it doesn't cost you evenly: even a drink or two measurably eats into that restorative sleep, and the more you drink, the more it takes.

So you wake having ticked the deep-sleep box but missing the things that actually leave you feeling rested: unbroken sleep, a full share of REM, and a body that properly powered down overnight. That's a big part of the tiredness behind a hangover: not sleep you never got, but recovery the night never finished.

There's a problem in the second part of the night too. After a heavier night of drinking, I would often crash out for the first three or four hours. Then wide awake. Three or four in the morning, heart going, no prospect of getting back to sleep. 

This is metabolic rebound: heart rate up, blood pressure up, the body shifting from suppressed to activated at the point in the night when it should be doing its recovery work. For someone who sleeps well, the rebound might mean a patch of light sleep before drifting off again. For someone already not sleeping well, it can mean that night's sleep is over.

If you do sleep through the metabolic rebound, you hit REM rebound. The REM suppressed earlier springs back, often as more intense, vivid, sometimes disturbing dreams in the early hours. If you've ever woken from an unusually vivid dream after a night of drinking, that's what you were experiencing.

How much all this costs you depends on your reserve. With no sleep debt, one disrupted night may not matter much. Running on empty, it's a substantial blow. It's why some people wake up fine after a few drinks while others (and I'm reliably in this camp) wake up feeling terrible.

**What I actually do**

Until I understood what alcohol was doing to my sleep, most weeks I would drink at least one beer every night to unwind from the day, and two or three on weekend nights. I knew it wasn't recommended for peak athletes. But I didn't think it was a problem for the average person wanting a decent night's sleep.

The first thing that had to change was the frequency.

A dry working week was the decision I landed on. Nothing on weeknights. This was hard at first because it was so ingrained in my daily routine. So I applied the same approach as caffeine. I stopped one night a week to begin with, then expanded on that until Sunday to Thursday were clear.

That left the weekends. Ideally, I would have stopped them too. But beer is part of my social life, and a hobby: you can't ask a baker not to taste his pastries. I wasn't prepared to give it up entirely. So here I landed on one to two drinks, finishing at dinner time. That typically means at least 3 hours before bed. And having it with food slows absorption and softens the peak.

It's not the optimal position. The science is clear on that. But it's an honest one, a compromise made with full awareness of the cost.

Here's what to take from this chapter.

If you're drinking most evenings, even one drink, start with frequency. A dry working week is the simplest, highest-impact change. It takes the decision off the table on the nights you most need to sleep well.

If you're already drinking infrequently, just shrink the impact: earlier, with food, as little as the occasion allows, ideally two drinks max, finished with dinner.

And if you're not ready to change anything yet (if the evening drink feels genuinely non-negotiable right now) I really recommend: do something, not nothing. Find the smallest possible move. One fewer drink. One night a week without.

A small shift you hold onto is worth more than a big commitment you drop.
