# Chapter 19 - Habit 6: Your Evening Routine

The evening doesn't need to be elaborate. A couple of the right things, done consistently: a clear signal that the Wake Executives are ready to hand over to the Sleep Executives. That's all the system needs.

What's already happening by the time dinner is done: cortisol (your primary Wake Executive) is naturally declining. Melatonin is beginning to rise. The sleep window is approaching on its own.

What the evening requires, then, is that you stop interfering with it.

Bright overhead light tells the Sleep Executives you're not ready for them yet. So does stimulating content, and so does any conversation that goes somewhere emotionally activating. Each of those inputs is a signal that the day isn't over yet, so the window slides later, and you arrive at bed exhausted but wide awake.

My evenings have a shape that isn't exactly negotiable. Dinner, homework, then getting three kids to bed. By the time the last one is down, the gap between that moment and my own reasonable bedtime is not particularly long. Whatever the evening habit was going to look like, it had to fit inside that window, not replace what came before it.

This is probably closer to most people's reality than the sleep-sanctuary evening the internet sells you. The question isn't how to design the perfect evening from scratch. It's what to do with the time that's left once the day has happened.

For most people, that gap exists. It might be 30 minutes. It might be an hour. It doesn't need to be more than that.

By the time the kids are down, the lights are already down. Homework and reading happen at desk lamps: lower, directional light that isn't flooding the room. It's not a deliberate sleep strategy at that point. It's just practical. But it means the handover to the Sleep Executives has already begun before the evening is technically over.

From there, no phones: nothing in my hand, nothing I can scroll. I watch TV for half an hour to an hour: something undemanding, nothing that's going to spike the Wake Executives. A sitcom, a mild comedy. It's the same low-engagement watching as the can't-sleep routine, but you can afford to dial it up slightly more than you would at 2am.

When the TV goes off, the evening is over. Teeth, PJs, bed. No social media. No checking messages. No conversation that might go too deep. Nothing that will spike dopamine or cortisol. Anything like that can wait until morning.

The bed is for sleep. Whatever didn't get resolved before I got into it doesn't get resolved in it. If I really need a conversation (this is real life), I do it sitting up. Lying down means sleep. The only exception is intimacy.

In practice, the day ends in the same place every time, so the body can begin to recognise it as a signal for sleep.

The last part of this habit is the actual time itself.

Start by working backwards from your wake-up time. Allow eight hours. That gives you roughly thirty minutes to fall asleep and seven and a half hours of actual sleep: a solid baseline. Of course, you may not be getting that at the beginning, but that is what you are working toward. That's your bedtime and it should be consistent, within reason.

A common recommendation is to keep your bedtime within 30 minutes of the same time each night. There is reasonable evidence behind this: even modest deviations later than your usual bedtime show measurable effects on heart rate that carry into the following day, and the effect compounds the further you stretch it.

In practice, this is a judgement call. If you genuinely aren't sleepy when your set bedtime arrives, going to bed anyway tends to mean lying there frustrated, which is the opposite of what we are trying to achieve. Stay up a little longer (keep the evening quiet, nothing activating) and let sleep find you.

Going to bed earlier is a little more flexible. Earlier than your set time, provided you're feeling sleepy, is generally fine. In fact, as my sleep improved, I found that bed-a-bit-earlier was the only sustainable way to catch up on a poor night without resorting to napping. Varying your bedtime carries a cost, but going earlier it's less of a problem.

So: aim for consistency, allow for tiredness in either direction, and treat the thirty-minute window as your target-most-nights rather than a hard rule.

The one thing that doesn't flex is the morning. The wake-up time is the anchor. Protect it, and the occasional late night stays recoverable instead of becoming drift.

That's the evening. Lights down soon after dinner. Something undemanding for an hour. A clean line to bed. Bed is for sleep only. And aim for a consistent bedtime, occasionally earlier or later depending on sleepiness. 

Phase 3 is now in place. You have a morning anchor and an evening one. The day has a shape your body can recognise, and the system has the inputs it needs to do its job.

What sits between those two anchors is your nervous system, and how well it's trained to come back down. That's what we work on next.
