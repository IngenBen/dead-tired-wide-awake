# Chapter 7 - Cortisol

Most problems with sleep come down to two things. In fact it's the definition of insomnia - trouble getting to sleep and trouble staying asleep. For a long time, I struggled with both. Many people do.

If I had to pick the bigger of the two, mine was staying asleep. But I'm not sure that distinction matters much. If I missed my window, and by now you know how narrow that was, I was lying awake. If I managed to get to sleep, I'd typically wake somewhere between one and three in the morning. Either way, it ended up in the same place. Alert in a way that nobody wants at that time of night. Tossing and turning, mind racing.

Sometimes it was reliving difficulties from the past. Sometimes catastrophising about things in the future. But often, it had a positive focus, ways to move forward in life: Getting fitter. Business ideas. Home improvements. Fun things to do with the family. 

Because I wasn't always awake with anxious thoughts, for years I assumed insomnia advice didn't apply. I just had a busy mind.

Being awake with anxiety and being awake with ideas felt like different problems. And most of the sleep advice is aimed at anxiety. Mine didn't quite fit. A busy mind full of possibilities and problem solving didn't have a solution. Lots of them were feel-good ideas, which made it harder to name as a problem.

However, as I learned more about cortisol, I came to understand that whether the mind is racing with anxiety or its racing with positive thoughts, as long as it feels urgent, cortisol gets involved. And the worst part is, the more cortisol you have floating around in your system the more that things feel urgent, at that time of night.

However, I have to point out that even though cortisol gets a bad reputation as the stress hormone, something to be managed and reduced, its essential to our daily functioning. 

Cortisol is a Wake Executive, the one running the day shift. It's the body's alertness system, waking you up, sharpening focus and preparing you for whatever the day brings. Before you open your eyes, it's already rising, peaking in the first hour or so after waking, then beginning its long downward curve through the day, declining through the afternoon and into the evening until, by the time sleep approaches, it's low enough for the Sleep Executives to take over. Well, at least that's what should happen.

That curve is your baseline. Demand lifts you above it. A difficult meeting, a hard workout, a deadline, a disagreement, each one triggers a temporary spike, giving the system what it needs to respond. Then, when the demand passes, a well-trained system brings it back down to the line. Spike and return.

That's the system working. But what counts as demand is broader than it looks.

Cortisol often gets lumped in with fight or flight. But that first jolt is mostly adrenaline, and the two work at different speeds.

Adrenaline is the flash. A door slams in the wind at night and you're instantly awake, heart thumping. Within seconds you've worked out what it was, and within minutes you're drifting off again. The flash fades.

Now the garbage truck rumbles past, and you remember the bins are still up by the house. Same jolt. But this time there's a problem, one you can't fix from bed, and your mind picks it up and starts working on it. That's when the slower system comes in behind the flash. And it doesn't fade in minutes. It lingers.

What decides it isn't the size of the jolt. It's what it means to you.

Strictly speaking, cortisol is the last link in that slower system, the hormone at the end of a chain of signals that starts in the brain. Scientists haven't settled whether cortisol itself, or the signals before it, does most of the work of keeping you awake. For what you need to do about it, it makes no difference. So when I say cortisol in this book, I mean that whole slower system: the kind of activation that lingers.

It's also the system that people who sleep badly have been found, again and again, to run higher, especially in the evening.

And it isn't only about threat. Cortisol actually mobilises you for anything that feels like it needs immediate action.

It gives your body energy and gets your mind alert and focused. This makes sense in the popular definition. If you really were in danger, your mind races through every option for safety. It's solving problems at hyper speed.

It also makes sense when you have a deadline. Or when you're in go-mode, super activated, ploughing through your day, crossing off as much of your list as possible, jumping from one priority to the next.

That's the racing mind.

The nervous system doesn't distinguish between a threat and productivity. Both can feel urgent. And if they feel urgent, they get cortisol.

That's what was happening for me at 2am. Sometimes the thoughts were dark and anxious and they fit the popular definition. But often they weren't: the next holiday, activities with the family. A better version of something. It felt purposeful and productive. Better than lying awake worrying. But at 2am even the good ideas felt like they needed acting on, and that urgency was enough to keep cortisol running.

But the problem wasn't that cortisol was running. It was when.

Cortisol and melatonin should work in opposition, in the evening as cortisol falls, melatonin rises. When that handover happens cleanly, sleep follows.

This is activating and calming at their clearest. Cortisol is the activating chemistry. While it stays high, calming can't take over for the night. Much of the program is, one way or another, about getting cortisol to stand down on time.

This is a very common version of the mind that won't switch off at night, though not the only one. Not a flaw in you: cortisol, still high when it should be falling. Bring it down on time, and the mind comes down with it.

There's a subtler version of this, too. The nervous system learns by repetition, and after enough nights lying awake and frustrated in the same bed, the bed itself becomes a trigger, cortisol rising the moment you get in, before a single thought arrives. (Retraining that is a job for later in the program).

Either way, when the Cortisol executive refuses to pack up and go home, you lie in bed with your eyes open, exhausted and alert, wondering what's wrong with you. And if you do get to sleep, it can still find you later, when melatonin eases back, whatever cortisol was still running around fills the gap.

That's the problem staying asleep. And, there's a reason it tends to be between 1 and 3am. Melatonin's retreat, as the last chapter described, creates an opening. For someone who sleeps well, cortisol stays low enough that it doesn't matter. But when it's riding higher than it should, that step back is all that cortisol needs to jolt you awake.

Caffeine makes this even harder. Every cup taken too late is a mild cortisol stimulus, a small kick to a system that should be gradually falling toward evening. Caffeine keeps nudging it back up. By evening, if you already sleep badly, instead of a cortisol level low enough for sleep to take over, the baseline can be higher than it should be.

In my worst period, lying awake at 2am, I would typically do one of three things, all of which felt entirely reasonable at the time. I'd get up, open the laptop, and start working. Get something done. Turn a bad night into a productive one. Or I'd put on the television, something binge-worthy that had me hooked. Or I'd reach for the phone: Youtube, social media, emails.

This is the pattern modern life has perfected. The technology doesn't set out to ruin sleep. It's designed to capture attention, and attention often costs cortisol (dopamine too, but that has its own chapter).

Before the phone, if you arranged to meet someone and they were late, there was nothing to do but wait. Watch people walk past. Let your mind go where it wanted. The nervous system got downtime, which was exactly what it needed.

That experience is almost gone. The phone fills every gap. Social media feeds are built to surface the most provocative content first, because provocation triggers a response, and a response means engagement. Outrage, excitement, genuine interest, it doesn't matter. The nervous system treats them all the same. Every piece of stimulation can be another small bump. If you keep replaying them, those bumps can accumulate. Cortisol can stay up longer. The evening decline can get shallower. The Sleep Executives are trying to advance, but the Wake Executive (cortisol) never fully leaves the building.

This makes returning to sleep harder. Which increases the frustration. Which spikes cortisol. Which makes sleep harder still. And unfortunately it gets worse.

Sleep deprivation is stress. The body responds to it just like a threat, by releasing cortisol. Which means a bad night sends cortisol into the next day already running hot. The baseline higher, the drop shallower. The Sleep Executives facing steeper resistance the next night.

The habits I'd adopted to cope with the wakefulness were the same habits making the wakefulness worse. Getting up to work felt productive. It was the opposite. Binge watching television seemed to block the busy mind. The next day, coffee got me through. Every one of them a signal to the Wake Executive that the shift wasn't over.

The first thing that actually changed wasn't something I found in a research paper.

It was a podcast. One of dozens I was working through at the time, trying to piece together what I could do to fix my sleep. The guest said something simple, almost throwaway: don't do things at night that you'd do during the day. It sends completely the wrong signal.

I didn't fully understand it at the time, but it made sense. I stopped getting up to work. I stopped looking at my phone. I didn't yet know why. I just knew, from that line, that I shouldn't. (We'll come back to the television).

It wasn't until years later, when I understood the biology, that the logic clicked. The system knows what to do. What's complicated is protecting that rhythm in a modern day environment which seems to be designed, at every turn, to override it.

Every spike that didn't return to baseline. Every habit that kept the Wake Executive active at the wrong times. Every bad night that sent extra cortisol into the next day. All those things made the cycle harder to break.

Cortisol itself was never the problem. It was too much at the wrong times.

But cortisol at least announces itself. The next one doesn't.
