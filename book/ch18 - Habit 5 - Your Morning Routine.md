# Chapter 18 - Habit 5: Your Morning Routine

For a long time, my solution to a bad night was to sleep in as long as possible.

It seemed obvious. I'd been awake for hours in the middle of the night, lying there, finally drifting back off sometime between 4 and 5am, alarm going off at 6. My job had flexibility on start time so I used it. I'd roll in late, make up the hours, it wasn't a problem. I thought I was being smart. Banking every hour of sleep I could get.

I was completely unaware of the chaos I was causing for my Sleep and Wake Executives: adenosine and melatonin, cortisol and dopamine, the four we met earlier. The problem is what happens to your body clock when the start of your day keeps moving around.

Your body is not a bank where you deposit what you missed and withdraw it later. It is a timing system. And the single most important input into that system is the time you wake up and get light into your eyes.

When that time shifts, even by an hour or two, everything shifts with it. The cortisol awakening response, your body's natural alerting mechanism, fires later. The countdown to evening melatonin release starts later. By the time you want to sleep that night, your Sleep Executives haven't had enough time to build to the level they need. The window hasn't arrived yet. You're lying there, tired but wired, frustrated that you are losing all the extra sleep you gained that morning.

The morning signal is the most powerful cue your body has to set its internal clock.

Light entering your eyes in the first thirty to sixty minutes after waking tells your brain: it's daytime. The Wake Executives fire. The sleep countdown begins. Done consistently, your body knows what to expect. The melatonin that helps you sleep that night will be ready when you need it, because your system has been tracking the hours since morning light arrived.

Without that signal (or with it arriving at a different time each day) the countdown starts from wherever your body happened to surface. And a system that can't predict when the day begins struggles to predict when sleep should begin.

The habit itself is simple. Get outside within thirty minutes of waking. Natural light, even on an overcast day, is dramatically brighter than indoor lighting: bright enough to trigger the response that indoor lamps, phone screens, and ceiling lights cannot replicate. Five to fifteen minutes in direct sunlight is a good starting point. On an overcast day, aim for twenty minutes. The sky, even grey, delivers far more light than the inside of a house.

Light alone will do the job. But movement alongside it helps too: exercise is its own gentle cue to the body clock. A morning walk gives you both at once. That's the habit worth building. It doesn't need to be long. It just needs to happen.

The walk was typical of how insomnia played out for me. A daily habit I wanted to build, and, at the same time, one that fatigue had me really not wanting to do at all. That constant tension extended to so many activities in my life, and it was a punishment in itself. For quite a while I had to force myself out the door.

It helped that my wife and our dog were already in the routine. But I joined them quiet, a bit grumpy, reluctant to be there, and not the most pleasant version of myself. I did the best I could to mask it. Some days I managed better than others.

As the sleep improved, the walks changed. Slowly, reluctant became less reluctant. Less reluctant became keen. Keen became: I don't want to miss it. I started talking more. Noticing the light, the air, whatever was happening around me. At some point, the walk stopped being a thing I was forcing and started being a thing I wanted.

Along the way, I made one simple addition. I stopped looking at the ground. Head up, shoulders back, eyes up and out: toward the horizon, the mountains, the trees, the clouds, rather than my feet or my phone. Taking in whatever nature was in front of me. It cost no extra time and almost no effort, I was already out there. And walking like that, I felt calmer, more confident, and more in control. For something that costs nothing, the return was surprisingly large.

I'm not sure the sleep caused the walks to improve, or the walks helped the sleep improve, or both. But the grumpy, quiet version of me mostly disappeared.

The movement doesn't have to be a walk: a run, a cycle, anything that gets you outside works just as well. If movement genuinely isn't possible, getting outside for fifteen minutes still delivers most of the signal. Sit and read, stand in the garden, whatever works. And if your schedule or situation keeps you inside entirely, a bright light therapy lamp at breakfast can be a workable substitute.

But what if you had a really late night and you are desperate for more sleep?

This is the rule I find hardest to follow. But the answer is always the same: wake time does not move. Get up, and stay up for at least a couple of hours. Let the Wake Executives turn up to work at the time they expect. Give the system its signal. Then, if you are absolutely desperate, the shortest nap you can manage. Twenty minutes at most. Somewhere between late morning and 3pm. Just enough to take the edge off without pushing the evening window. Better still, turn to the napping chapter when you get there. There is another way to do it that doesn't cost you the following night.

What you are trying to avoid is the full sleep-in. Rolling over and going back to sleep for a couple of hours sends an inconsistent signal to the executives. I've broken this rule: lain in on a Sunday after a rough Saturday, told myself it didn't matter, and spent the following week paying for it. I know what it costs. It's better to tough it out to ensure a quality sleep that evening, because the morning routine is not really about the morning.

It is about the following evening.

The signal you send at six or seven in the morning (consistent, bright light at the same time each day) sets the timer for when the Sleep Executives need to be ready to take over. Everything that follows (the cortisol clearing, the adenosine building, the melatonin rising) runs from that fixed starting point.

The morning habit you're building is setting your clock. This is the one time you want the activating system switched on hard, on purpose, early. The same light that wakes you decides when the calming system takes over that night.

When the clock is set well, the rhythm holds. When the rhythm holds, sleep becomes something that arrives rather than something you chase.

The habit is this: wake at a consistent time, get outside within thirty minutes, walk if you can, look up when you do.

That is all it needs to be for now.
