When all four executives are in their correct states simultaneously — adenosine high, melatonin rising steeply, cortisol low, body temperature falling — sleep comes easily. That alignment is brief: it tracks the steepest part of melatonin's *rise* (centred on bedtime), not melatonin's peak, which comes later (the middle of the night, usually 2–4 h after sleep onset). After the window the pull to sleep does not weaken: the sleep gate stays open and adenosine keeps building. What changes is the other side. Lying awake gives a thought room to land; working on it recruits the slower stress system (cortisol in the book's sense, see [[Cortisol]]), which then keeps the person awake, and in someone whose Wake Executives never fully stood down it takes little. That chain is the book's reasoning, built from established findings: worry and rumination delay sleep (Harvey 2002; Zoccola 2009; Tang 2007), people with insomnia carry raised evening arousal (Riemann 2010; Dressle 2022), and stimulus control advises not lying in bed awake.

Timeline relative to habitual bedtime (clock times for a 10:30pm bedtime): wake maintenance zone, when sleep is hardest, about 4 to 2 h before (~6:30–8:30pm) → melatonin onset about 2 h before (~8:30pm) → sleep gate opens around onset (~8:30–9:30pm; exact lag unconfirmed) → steepest climb in the hour or two around bedtime (~9:30–11:30pm; the window) → about 80% of peak by around bedtime → peak 2–4 h after sleep onset (~12:30–2:30am) → gradual decline through the early morning. Sources: Sletten 2010, Shochat 1997, Lavie 1997, Rajaratnam 2004, Zeitzer 2007 (curves read by eye).

For someone with a well-trained sleep system the window matters little — the Wake Executives are quiet enough that the Sleep Executives win regardless. For someone who has slept badly for years, the window is the only period when the pull to sleep is strong enough to win, making protecting it critical.
