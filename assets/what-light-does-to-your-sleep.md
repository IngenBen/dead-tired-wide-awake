# What Evening Light Does to Your Sleep — A Walk Down the Dose–Response Curve

*A working synthesis of the evidence reviewed in discussion. Prepared as a structured narrative review, built around the lux dose–response curve and the very large person-to-person variation around it.*

---

## Abstract

The popular story about light and sleep has two halves, and both are slightly the wrong shape. The first is the "blue-light panic" — screens emit blue light, blue light wrecks sleep, so filter it. The second is the reassuring threshold — "below about X lux you're fine." This paper replaces both with the actual structure of the evidence: melatonin's response to evening light is a **steep dose–response curve with no clean safe cutoff**, and the position of that curve **varies more than fifty-fold between people**. At the group level, ordinary dim indoor light already does real work — roughly 25–100 lux produces about half-maximal melatonin suppression, and as little as ~10 lux measurably delays the melatonin rise. But the same evening room is registered very differently by different nervous systems: the most light-sensitive person suppresses melatonin at ~6 lux, the least at ~350. The throughline correction is this: **a melatonin signal is not the same thing as a ruined night.** Light suppressing some melatonin shifts and dampens a *clock signal*; the measured effect of pre-bed screen light on how fast you actually fall asleep is, by contrast, modest — on the order of ten minutes — and the *content* on the screen matters at least as much as the light. So the useful levers are dimness and timing (for the clock) and what you're doing on the device (for sleep onset), not a magic lux number. Throughout, what each study measured is kept separate from what is reasonable inference.

## 1. Framing the question

Light is the single most powerful input to the human circadian clock — the signal that tells the body what time it is. That fact is not in dispute, and it is why a footnote that hangs a recommendation on one tidy number ("below 17 lux, melatonin suppression is minimal") is worth examining: no study supports that specific threshold, and more importantly the *idea* of a threshold misrepresents the biology. The relationship is a curve, not a cliff.

Two reframings make the rest of the paper readable. First, the question is not "is this light below the safe line?" but "where on a steep, individual-specific dose–response curve does this light put me?" Second, "light affects sleep" actually bundles **two separable effects** — a slow, clock-shifting effect carried by melatonin, and a fast, wakefulness-raising *alerting* effect — which have different time-courses and different practical consequences (§4).

As in the companion files, two cautions recur. Much of the *mechanism* (which retinal cells, which wavelength) is established in controlled lab settings rather than measured in your living room; and almost everything is a *correlate* — a melatonin level, an EEG trace — so the step from "light changed this marker" to "light wrecked your sleep" is frequently an inference, flagged as such.

## 2. The dose–response curve — how little light it takes

**What happens.** Melatonin suppression by evening light follows a logistic dose–response curve, and the curve sits at surprisingly low intensities. The classic human study exposed volunteers to a single 6.5-hour episode of evening light across a wide range and found that *half* of the maximal response to bright light (~9000 lux) was reached "with just over 1% of this light (dim room light of approximately 100 lux)" — and that this held for the acute suppression of plasma melatonin as well as for clock phase-shifting (Zeitzer 2000). A later within-subjects study pushed the estimate lower still: a group-level half-suppression dose (ED50) of **24.6 lux**, with 10, 30, and 50 lux delaying the apparent melatonin onset by **22, 77, and 109 minutes** respectively (Phillips 2019).

**Why it matters.** Both numbers are *below or at* the light level of an ordinary lamp-lit lounge room in the evening. The takeaway is not a scarier threshold; it is that there is no comfortable threshold at all. Phillips frames the historical shift well: before electric lighting humans met mostly "intense (>300 lux) or dim (<30 lux)" light, whereas we now "spend hours per day exposed to intermediate light intensities (30–300 lux), particularly in the evening" — i.e. modern life parks us right on the steep part of the curve (Phillips 2019).

**The mechanism.** These non-visual effects are driven mainly by a specialised class of retinal cells — the intrinsically photosensitive, melanopsin-expressing retinal ganglion cells — which signal ambient light level to the body clock and are most sensitive to short-wavelength ("blue") light (Brown 2022). This is the kernel of truth inside the blue-light story: the clock's light sensor is genuinely blue-biased.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: ~50% melatonin suppression at ~100 lux (Zeitzer 2000) and at ~25 lux group-level (Phillips 2019); melanopsin-expressing retinal neurons mediate these effects (Brown 2022). Inferred: that the precise lux in a given person's bedroom maps cleanly onto these curves — it does only loosely, because of §3.

## 3. The fifty-fold spread — why a single number misleads

**What happens.** The same evening light is not the same dose to two different people. In the within-subjects study, individual half-suppression doses "ranged by over an order of magnitude (6 lux in the most sensitive individual, 350 lux in the least sensitive individual)" — a greater than fifty-fold spread (Phillips 2019).

**Why it matters.** This is the finding that quietly kills any universal threshold. A light level that barely touches one person's melatonin can halve another's. It means honest advice has to be conditional ("dimmer is better, and you may be more sensitive than average") rather than absolute ("under X lux is safe"). It also explains why people's lived experience of screens-before-bed genuinely differs — some are reporting accurately that it wrecks them, others that it doesn't, and both can be right.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: the 6–350 lux individual range (Phillips 2019). Inferred: that this variability is stable and trait-like enough to act on personally — plausible (the study reports a modest within-person coefficient of variation) but not something a reader can measure at home.

## 4. Two effects, not one — the clock signal and the alerting kick

**What happens.** Evening light does two separable things. The slow one is the circadian/melatonin effect above. The fast one is **acute alerting**: light at night directly raises wakefulness. A companion dose–response study found that "half of the maximum alerting response to bright light of 9100 lux was obtained with room light of approximately 100 lux," with the alerting response (fewer slow eye-movements, less theta-alpha EEG, lower subjective sleepiness) tracking the degree of melatonin suppression (Cajochen 2000).

**Why it matters.** These two effects have different practical meanings. The clock effect is about *timing* — push melatonin later night after night and you drift your whole schedule later. The alerting effect is about *right now* — bright light makes you feel switched-on in the moment, working against the wind-down regardless of what it does to tomorrow's clock. A blue-light filter, which targets the wavelength the clock sensor cares about, does little about the alerting/arousal route.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: a room-light-level alerting response that correlates with melatonin suppression (Cajochen 2000). Inferred: the relative weight of "clock" vs "alerting" in any one bad night — both are present; the split isn't measured per person.

## 5. From lux to the screen in your hand

**What happens.** What reaches the clock's sensor is the light **at the eye**, not the light leaving the device — and that falls off steeply with distance and depends on screen brightness and size. So the relevant comparison is never "screens" in the abstract; it is a close, bright phone versus a dim, distant TV. A television several metres away delivers far less illuminance to the eye than a phone held a hand-span from the face.

**A caution on the specific numbers.** Convenient figures circulate — e.g. "a TV at three metres is ~5 lux at the eye, a phone at 30 cm is ~25–50 lux." Those particular values are reasonable as a rough illustration of the distance principle but are not, in this review, traceable to a measured source; they should be presented as illustration, not data. The robust point is the physics (inverse-square falloff with distance) and the dose–response curve of §2, not the exact lux of any one device.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: melatonin/alerting effects scale with illuminance at the eye (Zeitzer 2000; Phillips 2019; Cajochen 2000), which by simple optics falls with distance. Inferred/illustrative: device-specific lux figures.

## 6. What screens actually do to sleep onset — the modest reality

**What happens.** Here the panic over-reaches the data. When researchers test the hypotheses head-on in controlled experiments — bright screen vs dim, or stimulating content vs neutral — the effect on how quickly people fall asleep is small. The most recent synthesis concludes the technology–sleep link "is more complex than originally thought," reviews the bright-light and arousal mechanisms, and finds each delays sleep onset by, at most, on the order of ten minutes (Bauducco 2024). A National Sleep Foundation expert panel reached formal consensus that **pre-bed content** impairs sleep but did **not** reach consensus that pre-bed **light** does (Hartstein 2024).

**Why it matters.** This is the corrective the whole topic needs: **melatonin suppression is not the same as insomnia.** Light can measurably dent a clock signal (§2) while barely moving the practical outcome people care about — minutes to fall asleep. And to the extent screens *do* delay sleep, the engaging/arousing content is at least as important as the photons. That is exactly why "you can't blue-light-filter your way out of it" is the right conclusion: the filter addresses the wavelength the clock cares about, not the arousal from what you're watching.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: experimental delays of roughly ten minutes for light and for arousal (Bauducco 2024, evidence synthesis); consensus on content but not light (Hartstein 2024). Note for accuracy: the specific minute figures sit in Bauducco's body/summary rather than the abstract, and in those experiments light's measured delay was marginally *larger* than arousal's — so the honest framing is "both effects are small," with content the more *robustly demonstrated* factor rather than necessarily the larger one.

## 7. A necessary distinction: dimming the clock signal vs losing sleep

It is tempting to collapse everything into "light is bad for sleep." More precisely, evening light does three distinguishable things, and only some of them are what people fear:

1. It **shifts and dampens the clock signal** (melatonin), and it does so at low intensities with big individual variation (§2–3). Repeated nightly, this is a *timing* problem — a later body clock — more than a single-night problem.
2. It **acutely raises arousal** in the moment (§4), working against wind-down.
3. It has only a **modest direct effect on sleep-onset latency** in experiments (§6).

So "bright evening light pushes your body clock later, especially if you're light-sensitive" is well supported. "Screens rob you of sleep through blue light" overstates it: the measured sleep-onset cost is small, individual, and shares the stage with content. The practical levers follow from the distinction — dimness and timing (and morning light to anchor the clock) for the circadian side; managing *what* you do on the screen for the arousal/onset side.

## 8. Synthesis

Walking down the curve rather than hunting for a threshold: evening melatonin responds to light along a steep logistic curve that already bites at dim-room levels (~25–100 lux for half-suppression; ~10 lux delays onset; §2), the curve's position varies more than fifty-fold between people (§3), and light is doing two jobs at once — slowly shifting the clock and quickly raising alertness (§4). What reaches the eye is what counts, so distance and brightness matter more than the device label (§5) — but the *practical* effect of pre-bed screen light on falling asleep is modest, and content matters at least as much (§6). The cleanest one-line framing that stays inside the evidence: **evening light is a powerful signal to your body clock but a weak cause of any single bad night** — so dim the room, mind your own sensitivity, and worry about what's on the screen as much as the light coming off it.

The most defensible caveats, kept honest: the melanopsin/short-wavelength mechanism is lab-characterised, not measured in your bedroom; device-specific lux figures are illustrative, not sourced; individual sensitivity is real and large but not something a reader can quantify at home; and effect sizes for screens on sleep onset are small and dose-/content-dependent, best left qualitative.
