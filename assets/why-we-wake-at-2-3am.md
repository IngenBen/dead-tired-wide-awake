# Why Do People Wake at 2–3 a.m.? Candidate Mechanisms in the Light of the Sleep Endocrinology and Homeostasis Literature

*A working synthesis of the evidence reviewed in discussion. Prepared as a structured narrative review. Revised to incorporate SCN morning program framing and wake-time-dependent melatonin timing.*

---

## Abstract

Waking in the small hours — classically around 2–3 a.m. — and struggling to return to sleep is one of the most common complaints in insomnia. This paper asks a narrow physiological question: what plausibly *drives* that mid-night awakening? "Middle of the night" here means approximately four hours before habitual rise time; for a 6 a.m. waker, that is the 2 a.m. hour. It works through four candidate mechanisms — (A) a blunted nocturnal melatonin amplitude; (B) a circadian phase advance; (C) cortisol / HPA-axis hyperarousal; and (D) the front-loading of homeostatic sleep pressure. It also examines, and substantially revises, the intuitive idea that a *fall* in melatonin triggers the wake. The early version of this article rejected that idea; the revised view is more nuanced: melatonin timing depends on habitual wake time, not bedtime, and for a 6 a.m. waker the SCN's synthesis-offset signal falls squarely in the 2–3 a.m. window. More importantly, a new framing section introduces the SCN morning program as the unified upstream cause: the synthesis offset (SynOff) marks the moment the SCN simultaneously terminates melatonin synthesis, activates the HPA axis, and begins restarting arousal circuits. Melatonin decline and cortisol rise are parallel downstream signatures of that single event, not a causal chain. The best-supported account of the 2–3 a.m. wake is a convergence of all four mechanisms: the SCN morning program begins (the circadian event that selects the hour) at the precise point in the night when homeostatic sleep pressure has decayed to its overnight floor (D — not merely permissive, but jointly necessary for timing specificity). This event acts on a sleeper whose sleep-promoting signal is already weakened by blunted melatonin amplitude (A) and whose flip-flop switch is already destabilised by elevated baseline cortisol (C). Neither the SCN morning program nor discharged sleep pressure alone would produce the wake; their convergence at 2–3 a.m. is what makes that hour the vulnerable window, and A and C determine who crosses the threshold. Throughout, the honest position remains that these are convergent, mostly correlational lines of evidence; causal roles are inferred, not demonstrated.

---

## 1. Framing the question

"Middle of the night" is relative to the clock the body is keeping, not the wall clock. This paper frames 2–3 a.m. as approximately four hours before habitual rise time — the biological midpoint of a night that ends at 6 a.m. That framing matters because melatonin timing is anchored to wake time, not bedtime. Burgess and Fogg (2008), in 170 healthy adults aged 18–45, found that an earlier assigned wake time was associated with an earlier dim-light melatonin onset (DLMO) at a coefficient of B = 0.77 (p < 0.001): each hour of earlier wake time advanced the DLMO by 0.77 hours. Crucially, bedtime showed no significant association with any melatonin parameter. The implication for this question is direct: when the study population's mean wake time shifts from roughly 8 a.m. to 6 a.m., the entire melatonin curve shifts roughly 1.5 hours earlier — moving SynOff, the true synthesis-offset signal, from around 5 a.m. into the 3 a.m. hour.

Two framing points recur through the paper. First, several of the relevant studies are small, and some are decades old; they are most safely read as convergent pointers rather than individually decisive. Second, almost every study measures a *correlate* (a hormone level, an EEG marker) rather than the awakening itself, so the step from "this is altered in insomnia" to "this causes the 2–3 a.m. wake" is frequently an inference, and that distinction is flagged explicitly throughout.

## 2. Reframing the melatonin timing question

### 2a. The original rejection and why it was population-specific

The earliest version of this article rejected a melatonin fall as a mechanism on straightforward grounds: the melatonin acrophase sits in the 2–4 a.m. window, the decline is a later-night phenomenon, and so the hormone was at or near its peak at the time of the awakening. That rejection was sound for its reference population — healthy sleepers or research subjects studied on roughly 8 a.m. wake schedules.

The PLOS ONE data (Burgess & Fogg 2008; n = 170, mean prestudy wake time 08:11 ± 01:01) give a mean DLMO of 20:50 (±1:12) and a mean DLMO-to-DLMOff interval of 11.52 hours, placing the dim-light offset at approximately 08:21. The synthesis offset — the moment the SCN actively cuts melatonin production — precedes the plasma offset. Lewy, Cutler and Sack (1999) measured this interval directly, calling it Duros (DLMO₂ to SynOff), and found a mean of 8.18 ± 1.31 hours, a figure that was not confounded by melatonin amplitude and was independently cross-validated by Brown et al. (1997) in a separate sample. Applied to the Burgess & Fogg DLMO of 20:50, SynOff for an 8 a.m. waker falls around 05:00 — well after the 2–3 a.m. window. The original rejection holds for this population.

### 2b. What changes for a 6 a.m. waker

For a person who wakes consistently at 6 a.m., two hours earlier than the Burgess & Fogg mean, the B = 0.77 coefficient places the DLMO approximately 1.54 hours earlier, at roughly 19:16 (7:16 p.m.). Applying the same Duros of 8.18 hours puts SynOff at approximately 03:27 — directly in the 2–3 a.m. window. So for this chronotype, the SCN synthesis-offset signal, and whatever biological cascade it initiates, occurs at exactly the hour in question.

The Burgess & Fogg sample was healthy adults aged 18–45 and the Lewy 1999 Duros was measured in an elderly insomnia population (mean age 70), so neither study was designed to test the 6 a.m. extrapolation directly. The calculation assumes the Duros interval transfers across populations (Lewy's cross-validation with Brown et al. makes this reasonable) and that the B = 0.77 wake-time–DLMO relationship scales linearly beyond the studied range (a standard chronobiology assumption, consistent with how phase-shifting via jet lag and shift work is modelled, but not tested at this specific magnitude). That caveat is noted; the estimate is treated as a reasonable inference, not a confirmed figure.

A lighter framing for non-technical contexts: these studies were done with subjects waking around 8 a.m. For those people, melatonin synthesis ends around 5 a.m. — after the 2–3 a.m. window. If your habitual wake time is 6 a.m., the whole curve plausibly shifts roughly 1.5 hours earlier, moving the synthesis-offset to around 3 a.m. The existing rejection doesn't apply to your schedule.

## 3. The SCN morning program as unified upstream cause

### 3a. What SynOff actually represents

The SCN does not passively allow melatonin to "wear off." It terminates synthesis actively, via a GABA-ergic signal through the paraventricular nucleus and superior cervical ganglion to the pineal gland. Lewy's argument for preferring SynOff over the plasma DLMOff as a phase marker rests precisely on this: SynOff is temporally closer to the actual SCN event; the DLMOff (when plasma levels cross a threshold) is a lagging, amplitude-confounded downstream measurement.

This means SynOff marks something specific: the SCN beginning its morning program. At that moment, the SCN simultaneously terminates melatonin synthesis, activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis to initiate the cortisol rise, and begins tipping the flip-flop switch (VLPO sleep-active neurons vs. wake-active LC/TMN/DR neurons) back toward the wake state. These are not sequential events where one causes the next; they are parallel downstream outputs of the same upstream pacemaker signal.

### 3b. Melatonin and cortisol as co-regulated signals

Zisapel, Tarrasch and Laudon (2005) reviewed and extended evidence for the inverse temporal relationship between melatonin and cortisol in humans. The key demonstrations were: (1) in adults, melatonin onset typically occurs during a period of low cortisol secretion; (2) administering exogenous melatonin around dusk advances not only the melatonin rhythm but also the cortisol rhythm as a block — when you move one, the other follows; (3) with aging, melatonin production declines and shifts later while cortisol production increases and its peak shifts earlier in the night, such that the two rhythms converge and the gap between them — the protected overnight sleep window — narrows.

The phase-shift finding is the key one for the model here. If melatonin and cortisol move together when an external signal entrains the clock, they are coordinated by the same upstream pacemaker rather than one causing the other. The SCN morning program accounts for both: it ends the melatonin signal and starts the cortisol signal simultaneously. This is why Born et al. (1989) found that experimentally raising cortisol deepened rather than fragmented sleep — because exogenous cortisol in the absence of the SCN morning program is not the same as the full complement of sleep-to-wake transition signals; it is one downstream variable uncoupled from the causal network.

### 3c. What this means for the 2–3 a.m. awakening model

For the good sleeper, the SCN morning program beginning at ~3 a.m. (for a 6 a.m. waker) or ~5 a.m. (for an 8 a.m. waker) produces gradual sleep lightening and increasing REM pressure — normal morning sleep architecture. The flip-flop switch is stable enough that the program's incremental change in the balance of sleep-promoting vs. arousal-promoting signals does not tip the system.

For the poor sleeper with elevated baseline cortisol and blunted melatonin amplitude, the flip-flop switch is less stable throughout the night. When the SCN morning program begins — simultaneously withdrawing the melatonin signal and starting the cortisol ramp — that same biological event crosses the threshold into a full awakening rather than merely lightening sleep. The melatonin decline and the cortisol rise are not the mechanism; they are the observable signatures of the mechanism: the SCN transitioning the pacemaker from nighttime to morning mode.

This reframes the A+C composite account from the earlier version of this paper. The picture is no longer "reduced melatonin signal (A) plus a cortisol trigger (C)." It is: the SCN morning program (unified upstream cause) acts on a sleeper whose overnight sleep-hold is already weakened — either because melatonin amplitude is blunted (A) providing less sleep-promoting signal to begin with, or because baseline HPA activity is elevated (C) destabilising the flip-flop switch — or both.

*Stated vs inferred.* Directly stated: melatonin and cortisol are co-regulated and phase-shift as a block (Zisapel 2005); experimentally raising cortisol deepened rather than caused waking (Born 1989); SynOff marks the SCN synthesis-cessation event (Lewy 1999). The unified-upstream-cause framing is the mechanistic inference that connects these findings and is well-grounded in the flip-flop-switch model of sleep-wake transitions, but it has not been tested in the direct context of the 2–3 a.m. awakening specifically.

## 4. Mechanism A — Blunted nocturnal melatonin amplitude

**The mechanism.** Rather than melatonin being mistimed, the idea here is that its overnight *amplitude* is reduced — the curve sits lower through the core of the night, so the sleep-promoting signal strength at 2–3 a.m. is weaker. A weaker signal makes a mid-night arousal easier to have and harder to reverse, and lowers the threshold at which the SCN morning program tips the sleeper into wake.

**Evidence for.** The most directly relevant study is Hajak et al. (1995), which measured overnight plasma melatonin in chronic primary insomniacs who complained *predominantly of difficulty maintaining sleep* — precisely the mid-night-waking phenotype. Melatonin in these patients tended to begin rising earlier in the evening but was significantly lower during the middle of the night, with a blunted peak (about 82 pg/ml versus about 117 pg/ml in controls), and the deficit was greatest in those whose insomnia had lasted more than five years. Riemann et al. (2002) found, in primary insomnia, that nocturnal melatonin production was significantly diminished relative to controls. Léger, Laudon and Zisapel (2004) reported, in a large sample of older insomnia patients, lower nocturnal melatonin output than in age-matched controls, with roughly a third classified as "low excretors" who responded better to melatonin replacement. At the conceptual level, the Lack, Micic and Lovato (2023) review assigns *sleep-maintenance* insomnia specifically to a "blunted amplitude of circadian rhythm," and the Scott/Lovato/Lack (2025) study nominates amplitude as the maintenance-relevant marker.

**Evidence against / limits.** The older-adults melatonin-peak study is a caution: because people can wake with melatonin still relatively high, reduced amplitude is best read as *contributory rather than strictly necessary*. Riemann himself called the pathophysiological significance of the melatonin reduction "a matter of debate." The supporting studies are small (Hajak and Riemann each around ten patients), partly age-mismatched, and the literature is mixed rather than uniform. And no study pins the reduced amplitude to a 2–3 a.m. awakening *event*; the link to that specific hour is reconstructed from the maintenance phenotype.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: mid-night melatonin is lower in maintenance insomniacs (Hajak); nocturnal melatonin is reduced in primary insomnia (Riemann) and in older insomnia (Léger); the maintenance phenotype is associated with blunted amplitude (2023 review). Inferred: that this reduced mid-night signal causes the 2–3 a.m. wake by lowering the threshold at which the SCN morning program crosses into a full awakening. The inference is well-motivated and lands in the right phenotype, but it is an inference.

## 5. Mechanism B — Circadian phase advance

**The mechanism.** Here the whole circadian rhythm — melatonin and core body temperature together — is shifted earlier. If the entire "biological night" is advanced, it also *ends* early, so the sleeper surfaces before the intended wake time.

**Evidence for.** Lack, Mercer and Wright (1996) compared early-morning-awakening insomniacs with good sleepers and found their temperature and melatonin rhythms phase-advanced by 2–4 hours, concluding that the awakening arises from advanced circadian rhythms evoking early arousal. The Lack/Micic/Lovato (2023) review restates this cleanly: early-morning-awakening insomnia is "associated with an advanced circadian phase (early timing)" and responds to evening bright light, which delays the clock. Hajak et al. (1995) is partially consistent, in that the melatonin rise tended to begin earlier in the evening.

**Evidence against / limits.** The decisive limitation is one of *timing*: a phase advance maps onto *early-morning* awakening, in the region of 4–5 a.m., rather than the mid-night 2–3 a.m. wake. Lack's own framing (and the companion treatment work) concerns waking "too early in the morning," not mid-night arousals. The Scott/Lovato/Lack (2025) study found that melatonin *timing* (DLMO) was associated with when people slept but **not** with sleep continuity or wake-after-sleep-onset — i.e. phase relates to the *schedule* of sleep, not to mid-night awakenings. This mechanism also overlaps with the wake-time DLMO shift from Section 2: a person on a 6 a.m. schedule is not phase-advanced in a pathological sense — they have a normally entrained rhythm appropriate to their schedule. The distinction between a pathological phase advance and a schedule-appropriate DLMO shift matters for interpretation.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: early-morning-awakening insomnia is phase-advanced (Lack 1996; 2023 review); melatonin timing predicts sleep timing but not continuity (Scott 2025). Inferred — and not supported by the evidence — that phase advance is the explanation for the *2–3 a.m.* wake specifically. The evidence points to a related but distinct phenotype.

## 6. Mechanism C — Cortisol / HPA-axis hyperarousal

**The mechanism.** Insomnia has long been framed as a disorder of 24-hour hyperarousal, with raised activity of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis. The proposal is that an overnight rise or pulse of cortisol drives the awakening directly.

**Evidence for.** The hyperarousal literature situates insomnia as round-the-clock hyperarousal with elevated cortisol. The nocturnal stress-hormone pilot in chronic insomnia (2024) found that stress-related hormone levels track sleep–wake states across the night, reaching their highest during especially long awakenings and lowest in deep sleep. Follenius et al. (1992), sampling cortisol every ten minutes overnight in healthy men, found that cortisol increases "generally accompanied prolonged waking periods," while slow-wave sleep coincided with *declining* cortisol — a genuine temporal coupling. Vargas, Vgontzas et al. (2018) develop this into a specific hypothesis: that aberrant *ultradian* cortisol pulses (the roughly 60–120-minute pulses, as distinct from the circadian rhythm) may predispose to nocturnal awakenings, possibly as a conditioned phenomenon.

**The SCN reframing and the Born paradox resolution.** The most important re-reading of Mechanism C is that it is not a standalone cause but a co-regulated downstream signature of the SCN morning program. Zisapel et al. (2005) showed that melatonin and cortisol move as a block under phase-shifting conditions, consistent with common upstream SCN control. Born et al. (1989) infused cortisol, ACTH or CRH overnight in healthy men and found that raising cortisol *reduced* REM sleep and significantly *increased* slow-wave sleep — it deepened sleep rather than fragmenting it. This long appeared paradoxical: if cortisol is co-occurring with awakenings, why does raising it not cause them? The SCN framing resolves this: the cortisol rise in natural sleep is one component of the coordinated SCN morning program. Cortisol alone — without the simultaneous melatonin withdrawal, flip-flop switch tipping, and arousal-circuit restarting — does not replicate the full transition. The Born experiment was testing the cortisol component in isolation; the natural 2–3 a.m. cortisol signal is part of an orchestrated pacemaker event. Riemann et al. (2002) reinforced the limits of the standalone account: cortisol did not differ between primary insomnia patients and controls, while melatonin was reduced.

**Synthesis-compatible role.** Elevated baseline cortisol — from chronic stress, anxiety, or HPA dysregulation — destabilises the flip-flop switch throughout the night, making the sleeper more susceptible to the SCN morning program's transition signal. This is the appropriate role for cortisol in the A+C composite: not a trigger on its own, but a vulnerability factor that determines whether the SCN morning program tips into a full wake or merely lightens sleep.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: cortisol is elevated round-the-clock in insomnia and accompanies nocturnal awakenings (hyperarousal literature; pilot study; Follenius); experimentally raising cortisol deepens sleep rather than causing awakenings (Born 1989); cortisol did not differ in primary insomnia (Riemann 2002); melatonin and cortisol are co-regulated by a common pacemaker signal (Zisapel 2005). Inferred: that elevated baseline HPA activity destabilises the flip-flop switch and lowers the threshold at which the SCN morning program produces a full awakening. Cortisol alone as a standalone trigger is the weakest and most actively contradicted reading.

## 7. Mechanism D — Homeostatic sleep-pressure front-loading (slow-wave activity / adenosine)

**The mechanism.** Across the day, sleep pressure builds; across the night, it discharges. Because deep slow-wave sleep is concentrated in the first half of the night, most of that pressure is spent early — leaving the second half lighter, with less homeostatic drive holding the sleeper down and therefore a greater susceptibility to arousal.

**Evidence for.** Porkka-Heiskanen et al. (1997) showed, by basal-forebrain microdialysis in cats, that extracellular adenosine is higher in wakefulness than in slow-wave sleep, rises progressively during sustained wakefulness, and declines during recovery sleep. Porkka-Heiskanen, Strecker and McCarley (2000) localised this largely to the cholinergic basal forebrain. The human evidence for front-loading comes from the two-process model (Achermann, Dijk, Brunner and Borbély 1993; Borbély and Achermann 1999): NREM slow-wave activity (SWA), the EEG marker of homeostatic sleep pressure, peaks at sleep onset and declines exponentially across the night.

**Evidence against / limits.** Two limits matter. First, the measured adenosine decline is slow: in the 2000 microdialysis study, basal-forebrain adenosine remained significantly elevated throughout three-hour recovery sleep — so the molecular signal does not collapse rapidly in the first half. Second, and more importantly, none of these studies measured insomnia, awakenings, or a 2–3 a.m. wake; they describe the *normal* time-course of sleep pressure in healthy subjects or animals. The link from "less pressure later" to "a specific mid-night awakening" is not measured.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: adenosine accumulates with wakefulness and dissipates with sleep (Porkka-Heiskanen 1997, 2000); SWA is front-loaded and declines exponentially (Borbély/Achermann). Inferred: that the lower second-half pressure *permits* the 2–3 a.m. wake. This is mainstream two-process reasoning and physiologically reasonable, but it is inference — and the cleanly evidenced part concerns SWA rather than adenosine concentration.

---

## 8. A necessary distinction: homeostatic pressure vs adenosine, and what SWA actually measures

Slow-wave activity is an *electrophysiological measurement* — EEG spectral power in the slow-wave band during NREM sleep. It is the marker that has been shown, in humans, to peak at sleep onset and decay exponentially across the night. When one says sleep pressure is "front-loaded," it is SWA that has actually been measured.

Adenosine is the *putative molecular substrate* presumed to sit behind that marker. The microdialysis work establishes that adenosine tracks time-awake and falls during sleep, but it does so in animals, in a specific brain region, and — crucially — with a *slow* overnight decline. The identity "SWA = adenosine" is a hypothesis-linked correlation, not a demonstrated equivalence.

The practical consequence for any popular-level account is precision of wording. It is well supported to say "deep sleep and homeostatic sleep pressure are front-loaded into the first half of the night." It overstates the data to say "adenosine has mostly cleared by 2–3 a.m." — the measured adenosine decline is gradual, and the front-loading that is robustly evidenced is the SWA curve.

## 9. Synthesis and ranking

Pulling the strands together, the mechanisms differ both in how well they are evidenced and in which awakening they best explain. The SCN morning program framing from Section 3 provides a unified upstream account that partially reorganises how the four mechanisms relate to each other.

**The unified upstream cause** is the SCN transitioning from nighttime to morning mode — marked by SynOff, simultaneously withdrawing the melatonin signal and initiating the cortisol and arousal-circuit cascade. For a 6 a.m. waker, this occurs around 3 a.m. For an 8 a.m. waker, around 5 a.m. Whether this biological event produces full waking or merely lightens sleep depends on how stable the flip-flop switch is throughout the night.

**A (blunted melatonin amplitude)** remains the best-supported vulnerability factor for mid-night wake. Lower overnight melatonin amplitude means the sleep-promoting signal that should be holding the flip-flop switch in the sleep position is weaker throughout — so when the SCN morning program begins, less of a shift is needed to tip the system. The evidence is directly in the right phenotype (Hajak 1995; Riemann 2002; Léger 2004; Lack 2023 review), though the causal step from blunted amplitude to the specific awakening hour remains an inference.

**C (HPA dysregulation as vulnerability)** is best understood as a flip-flop switch destabiliser rather than a standalone trigger. Elevated baseline cortisol keeps the sleep-wake system in a less stable state throughout the night, meaning the normal SCN morning program crosses the threshold into full wake rather than merely lightening sleep. This is why the A+C composite outperforms either alone: they address different parts of the same vulnerability — A weakens the sleep-promoting side, C weakens the system's resistance to the transition.

**D (homeostatic front-loading)** is better understood as a *timing co-determinant* than as merely permissive background. The two-process model (Borbély/Achermann; confirmed by forced desynchrony, Dijk & Czeisler 1994/1995) is precise on this: you remain asleep when Process S (homeostatic pressure) exceeds Process C (circadian alerting); you wake when C tips above S. The SCN morning program beginning at ~3 a.m. is the circadian event — but at midnight, even in a poor sleeper, Process S is still high enough that the same SCN signal would be absorbed without producing a wake. The reason the 2–3 a.m. hour is specifically the vulnerable one is that it is when the two processes converge: the SCN morning program starts *and* sleep pressure has decayed to its overnight floor after four or more hours of sleep. Remove D — imagine a sleeper who somehow still has full sleep pressure at 3 a.m. — and the SCN morning program at 3 a.m. would merely lighten sleep. Remove the SynOff event — imagine a sleeper whose circadian alerting never rises — and low sleep pressure alone does not select 3 a.m. over any other hour. D does not distinguish insomniacs from good sleepers (both experience the same SWA decay), but it jointly determines *why this hour* rather than earlier or later. A and C determine who, within the population that reaches 3 a.m. with depleted sleep pressure and a beginning SCN morning program, actually crosses into a full wake.

**B (circadian phase advance)** is well evidenced but for a *different* complaint — the ~4–5 a.m. early-morning awakening — and the 2025 data show timing relates to sleep schedule rather than mid-night continuity. It is off-target for a 2–3 a.m. wake, and should not be conflated with the wake-time-appropriate DLMO shift described in Section 2.

**C-only (cortisol as a standalone, melatonin-independent cause)** remains the weakest reading. Born 1989 showed raised cortisol deepened sleep; Riemann 2002 found no cortisol difference in primary insomnia; Zisapel 2005 placed cortisol as co-regulated alongside melatonin by the same upstream clock signal. Cortisol earns its place as a vulnerability modifier within A+C, not as a sufficient cause on its own.

---

## 10. Conclusion

On the evidence reviewed, the 2–3 a.m. awakening is best understood through a unified upstream framing: the SCN morning program begins (marked by SynOff), simultaneously withdrawing the melatonin signal, activating the HPA axis, and beginning the transition of arousal circuits back toward wake. Melatonin decline and cortisol rise are parallel signatures of this single event, not a causal chain between them. Whether this program produces a full awakening or merely normal morning sleep lightening depends on how stable the flip-flop switch is — which is where **blunted melatonin amplitude** (A) and **elevated baseline cortisol** (C) do their work, as vulnerability factors rather than direct triggers.

The wake-time framing matters: the original rejection of melatonin decline as a mechanism was accurate for a population waking around 8 a.m., for whom SynOff falls around 5 a.m. For a consistent 6 a.m. waker, the estimated SynOff falls near 3 a.m., placing the SCN morning program squarely in the 2–3 a.m. window.

**Homeostatic front-loading** (D) is a timing co-determinant: the SCN morning program at ~3 a.m. would not produce a wake if sleep pressure were still at its sleep-onset level. It is the convergence of the circadian event (SynOff) with the homeostatic floor (substantially discharged Process S) that makes 2–3 a.m. the specifically vulnerable hour. **Circadian phase advance** (B) is a well-evidenced mechanism but for the earlier, ~4–5 a.m. early-morning awakening — a related but distinct phenotype.

Throughout, the honest position is that these are convergent, mostly correlational lines of evidence. The mechanisms are well grounded as *features* of disturbed sleep; their specific causal role in any individual 2–3 a.m. wake remains, to varying degrees, an inference rather than a demonstrated fact.

Findings are reported at the level of what each study was about and what it concluded. Where a causal claim about the 2–3 a.m. awakening goes beyond what a study directly measured, the text flags it as an inference. The SCN morning program framing in Section 3 is a mechanistic synthesis across multiple study streams and should be read as a well-grounded interpretive framework rather than a directly tested hypothesis.*
