# How Deep Sleep Clears Sleep Pressure — Pressure, Adenosine, and the Slow-Wave Signal

*A working synthesis of the evidence reviewed in discussion. Prepared as a structured narrative review, built around one question: does deep sleep do more of the overnight "clearing," and how cleanly can that be said?*

---

## Abstract

The popular telling — and the current wording of Ch05 — treats deep sleep as *the* time the brain clears adenosine and discharges the tiredness of the day. This paper tightens that picture. It separates three words that get used as if they were one thing — *homeostatic sleep pressure*, *adenosine*, and *slow-wave activity (SWA)* — and shows they are a functional construct, its proposed chemical substrate, and its electrophysiological readout, respectively: one process described in three vocabularies, not three quantities. From there it asks the question directly. It **is** reasonable to say more sleep pressure is discharged during deep sleep and less in other stages — but the claim has to be stated as *fastest and most*, not *only*, and the load-bearing evidence is not the slow-wave decay curve (which is partly circular) but the *stage-dissociation* experiments that suppress deep sleep specifically and watch the consequences land. The throughline correction: pressure clearance is concentrated in deep sleep but not gated by it — adenosine keeps falling through the second half of the night, in lighter sleep and REM, because the wake-driven production that builds it has stopped. Throughout, what each study directly measured is kept separate from what is reasonable inference — and the adenosine strand in particular is flagged as mechanistically well-grounded but, in a whole human night, largely unmeasured.

## 1. Framing the question

Ask "where in the night does the tiredness actually get cleared?" and the intuitive answer is "deep sleep." The intuition is broadly right, but it hides a trap: one of the standard ways of supporting it is almost circular, and the genuinely persuasive evidence comes from a different kind of study. This paper walks that distinction. It first fixes the vocabulary (§2), then states the claim and weighs the two classes of evidence for it — the slow-wave decay curve (§3) and the stage-dissociation experiments (§4) — before turning to the part the simple story gets wrong: that clearance continues through the second half of the night in stages that are *not* deep sleep (§5). It closes with what deliberately removing deep sleep does to a person (§6) and the precise wording the data will actually support (§7).

Two cautions recur, as in the companion files. First, much of the molecular mechanism — the adenosine strand especially — is established in animals or in vitro, not by measuring a sleeping human's brain across a night. Second, almost everything here is a *correlate* — an EEG band, a glucose curve, a sleepiness score — so the step from "this changes when deep sleep is removed" to "this is the clearing that didn't happen" is an inference, and is flagged as such.

## 2. Three vocabularies for one process

The single most useful move is to stop treating pressure, adenosine, and SWA as separate variables.

**Homeostatic sleep pressure (Process S)** is the abstract one: the *drive* for sleep that rises the longer you are awake and falls while you sleep. It is a functional construct, defined by behaviour — how sleepy you are, how fast you fall asleep, how hard SWA rebounds after deprivation — not by any one substance. It is the thing the two-process model models (Borbély 2022).

**Adenosine** is the leading candidate for the *physical substrate* of that pressure — the molecule whose accumulation during waking embodies the need, and whose action at inhibitory A1 receptors facilitates the slow waves (Porkka-Heiskanen 1997; Bjorness & Greene 2009). Pressure is not so much *caused by* adenosine as *realised in* it.

**SWA** is the *readout* — the delta-band EEG signal that indexes how much pressure has accumulated. High SWA at sleep onset means high accumulated pressure; the exponential decay of SWA across the night is that pressure dissipating (Borbély 2022). SWA does not *do* the clearing; it is the needle on the gauge.

A gauge analogy: pressure is "how much the tank needs emptying," adenosine is the fluid in the tank, SWA is the dial. The mappings are tight but not perfect — Process S is an inferred model variable, the adenosine link rests largely on animal data, and SWA has local, use-dependent components that do not reduce cleanly to a single whole-brain number.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: SWA declines exponentially across sleep and indexes Process S (Borbély 2022); adenosine rises in waking and acts via A1 receptors to facilitate SWA (Porkka-Heiskanen 1997; Bjorness & Greene 2009). Inferred: that the three map onto one another tightly enough to be treated as one process in a human night — a well-motivated synthesis, not a single measurement.

## 3. The claim, and the evidence that is partly circular

**The claim.** More sleep pressure is discharged during deep sleep than during other stages.

**The half of the evidence that is nearly definitional.** SWA *is* the marker of pressure discharging, and SWA *is* what defines deep sleep. So "most pressure clears in deep sleep" partly just restates "SWA is concentrated in deep sleep." On two-process logic the discharge is proportional to SWA, and SWA is front-loaded into the first NREM cycles, decaying exponentially with a time constant on the order of many hours (Borbély 2022). That is real and quantified — but it cannot be the *main* support for the claim, because it uses the marker to prove a statement about the marker. Lean on it to *frame* the claim, not to settle it.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: SWA is high early and decays exponentially across the night (Borbély 2022). Inferred (and partly circular): that this proves deep sleep "does the clearing," rather than simply *being* the EEG signature of whenever clearing is fastest.

## 4. The non-circular evidence: stage dissociation

The persuasive support for the claim comes from experiments that remove deep sleep *specifically* — usually with a tone, loud enough to push the sleeper out of slow-wave sleep without fully waking them — and compare the consequences against removing REM instead. If deep sleep carries the homeostatic load, suppressing it should produce a rebound and a cost that suppressing REM does not.

That is what is found. Herzog et al. (2013) ran healthy men through three crossover nights — selective SWS suppression, REM disturbance, and normal sleep — using a graded acoustic tone. Selective SWS suppression impaired morning glucose tolerance and cut insulin sensitivity by up to ~20%; disturbing REM did not (Herzog 2013). This is the cleanest available demonstration that the *specificity* sits with deep sleep: same night length, same fragmentation logic, different stage targeted, and only the deep-sleep arm carried the consequence. Tasali et al. (2008) is the companion piece — three nights of SWS suppression in young adults dropped insulin sensitivity by an amount comparable to gaining 20–30 lb, dose-dependently with the degree of SWA reduction (Tasali 2008).

The matching homeostatic signature is the SWA *rebound*: suppress deep sleep and the brain pushes extra slow waves into the next undisturbed night, direct evidence the need was registered and not met (Borbély 2022, on rebound; mechanism via A1 receptors in Bjorness & Greene 2009).

**An honest counterweight.** The evidence is not all one way. Gillberg & Åkerstedt (1994) suppressed SWS and concluded that *sleep duration* may matter more for daytime alertness than SWS content, and that SWS lost on one night is largely recovered on the next. That does two things to the claim: it warns against treating deep sleep as the *only* thing that matters for next-day alertness, and it shows the system is resilient — a single night's deep-sleep loss is made good later. It does not overturn "more pressure clears in deep sleep," but it keeps the claim modest.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: selective SWS suppression (not REM) impairs glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity (Herzog 2013; Tasali 2008); SWS loss provokes a next-night SWA rebound and is recoverable (Borbély 2022; Gillberg & Åkerstedt 1994). Inferred: that the metabolic and rebound consequences are *the pressure-clearing that did not happen*. Coherent on two-process logic, but the studies measured glucose and EEG, not a residual adenosine load.

## 5. Why clearance continues through the second half — concentrated, not gated

If SWA is front-loaded into the first half, what discharges pressure in the second half, when deep sleep is sparse and REM is plentiful? Two complementary answers, and you need both.

**It is one exponential curve, not an on/off switch.** Process S decays exponentially across the *whole* night. Exponential decay is fastest when the load is highest, so it merely *looks* front-loaded: big slow waves early, a long shallow tail later (Borbély 2022). SWA amplitude tracks the instantaneous level of pressure, so as adenosine falls the slow waves shrink with it — the front-loading of SWA and the slow continued drop late in the night are the same equation, not two phenomena. Low-but-nonzero SWA in late cycles *is* the tail of the discharge.

**Clearance is not gated by deep sleep.** This is the part the simple story understates. Adenosine is produced by *wake-active* neurons burning energy; the moment you are asleep — in any stage — that production largely stops, while glial metabolism keeps breaking adenosine down. Microdialysis work shows extracellular adenosine in the basal forebrain is low during *both* NREM and REM, normalising across recovery sleep generally rather than only during slow-wave-rich periods (Porkka-Heiskanen 1997; basal-forebrain homeostasis work, 2006). So in the second half — even with little deep sleep and abundant REM — adenosine keeps falling, because production stays suppressed and metabolism keeps running. SWA marks the *rate* of discharge; it is not a valve that has to be open for adenosine to fall.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: SWA decays exponentially across the night (Borbély 2022); extracellular adenosine is low in both NREM and REM and falls across sleep generally (Porkka-Heiskanen 1997). Inferred: that the second-half adenosine fall in a particular human, across particular late-night REM, proceeds as the animal microdialysis implies. Mechanistically coherent, not directly measured in a human night.

## 6. What removing deep sleep actually does to a person

Selective deep-sleep deprivation has been run for outcomes beyond glucose, and the results say the missing stage matters in ways the sleeper feels.

**Metabolic.** Covered in §4 — insulin sensitivity falls measurably after a few nights of SWS suppression, dose-dependently with SWA lost (Tasali 2008; Herzog 2013).

**Pain and fatigue.** Moldofsky & Scarisbrick (1976) disrupted stage-4 (deep) NREM in healthy sedentary volunteers with tones and produced unrefreshing sleep, diffuse musculoskeletal aching, fatigue and tender points resembling fibromyalgia — which resolved with recovery sleep (Moldofsky 1976). The important caveat: samples were tiny and later replications were mixed (increased pain sensitivity in some, not others), so this is suggestive of deep sleep's restorative role, not settled.

**Sleepiness and alertness.** Suppressing deep sleep tends to raise next-day sleep propensity and lower subjective sleep quality — though, per Gillberg & Åkerstedt (1994), *duration* is a strong competing factor and a single night's SWS loss is recoverable. The alertness cost of deep-sleep loss is real but easy to overstate relative to simply sleeping too few hours.

**Stated vs inferred.** Directly stated: SWS suppression impairs glucose handling (Tasali 2008; Herzog 2013) and, in early small studies, induced musculoskeletal pain and fatigue (Moldofsky 1976). Inferred: that these are unified by "deep sleep is when restorative work is concentrated." A reasonable synthesis across different outcome measures, not one experiment.

## 7. A necessary distinction: fastest and most, not only

It is tempting to write that deep sleep is *the* time the brain clears its tiredness. More precisely, deep sleep is when pressure is discharged **fastest and most**, because that is when both the adenosine load and the slow-wave discharge are largest — but clearance is not exclusive to it. The first half does the heavy lifting; the second half continues the slow drop through lighter sleep and REM via suppressed production plus ongoing metabolism that do not depend on deep sleep at all (§5).

The practical consequence for a popular-level account is precision of wording. It is well supported to say "deep sleep is when your sleep pressure clears fastest, and losing it has measurable costs." It overstates the data to say "adenosine only clears during deep sleep" or "without deep sleep your sleep pressure does not come down" — adenosine keeps falling whenever you are asleep, and a single night's deep-sleep loss is largely recovered the next night (Porkka-Heiskanen 1997; Gillberg & Åkerstedt 1994). The defensible claim is one of *degree and rate*, not exclusivity.

## 8. Synthesis

Pressure, adenosine, and SWA are one process in three vocabularies (§2). Yes — more of that pressure is discharged in deep sleep than in other stages (§3–4), but the clean evidence for it is the stage-dissociation work that removes deep sleep specifically and watches metabolic and rebound costs land (Herzog 2013; Tasali 2008), not the slow-wave decay curve, which is partly circular (§3). And the claim must stay modest: clearance is *concentrated* in deep sleep, not *gated* by it — adenosine keeps falling through the second half in lighter sleep and REM because the wake-driven production that builds it has stopped (§5), and a night's deep-sleep loss is recoverable (Gillberg & Åkerstedt 1994). The cleanest one-line framing that stays inside the evidence: **deep sleep is where sleep pressure clears fastest and most — not the only place it clears.**

The most defensible caveats, kept honest: the adenosine mechanism is animal/in-vitro and unmeasured across a human night; the SWA-decay argument is partly circular and should frame, not prove, the claim; the early pain studies are small and mixed; and effect *sizes* are dose-dependent and best left qualitative.

---

## Implication for the book (Ch05)

Ch05 currently reads as if adenosine clears *during* deep sleep, full stop — "not enough deep sleep" means "the tank doesn't empty." The tighter, defensible wording is: *adenosine clears throughout sleep; deep sleep is when it clears fastest and most.* This preserves the chapter's point (lose deep sleep and you wake under-cleared and tired) while removing the overstatement that other stages do nothing. Suggested as an `/audit/` proposal, not an edit to `/book/`.
