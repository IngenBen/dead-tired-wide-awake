# Chapter 6 - Melatonin

It felt like I had a window.

Thirty, maybe forty minutes in the evening when sleep felt genuinely possible. Heavy eyelids. The couch getting comfortable.

Miss it, and the night was gone.

It didn't matter how tired I was. If something disrupted that window, I'd lie in bed waiting for it to come back. It usually took hours. Sometimes I'd give up, go downstairs, and watch television until one or two in the morning — sometimes later — running out the clock until adenosine finally took me by force.

The frustration made it worse. Getting annoyed at being awake, then annoyed at being annoyed. Once I hit that loop, I was more awake than when I went to bed.

For years I had one explanation: I was just a bad sleeper. A bit weird - I have a sleep window. At some point I even bought melatonin supplements — the logic seemed sound: it signals sleep, it's natural, more must be better. They didn't do much. I filed it under things that don't work for me and went back to running out the clock. I didn't realise the whole thing had a perfectly understandable hormonal explanation.

Melatonin is not a sleeping pill your body makes. It's a signal.

As the day fades and light drops, your brain begins releasing it. Slowly at first, then more steeply through the evening. It tells the system: night is coming, begin the sequence. Core body temperature drops slightly. Alertness falls. The machinery prepares.

But the signal doesn't force sleep — it enables it. It moves you toward the conditions where sleep becomes possible.

Those conditions converge in a narrow window: melatonin rising steeply, adenosine high from a full day of wakefulness, cortisol low, core body temperature falling — and a separate circadian alertness mechanism, finally standing down.

When they align, you feel the strongest pull toward sleep. That's what I was feeling as my thirty- or forty-minute sleep window. Melatonin continues to rise afterward, but more slowly. So does adenosine if you're still awake. It peaks and holds high through the middle of the night — long after this window. But the other signals have shifted. The convergence has passed. The pull weakens with it. At this point, if the Wake Executives are still at their desks, you've missed the window. You're exhausted but wide awake.

For someone with a well-regulated sleep system, there's flexibility across a longer stretch of the evening. The Wake Executives are calm and ready to hand over — the Sleep Executives don't have to battle them out the door.

For a chronic poor sleeper, it's different. The Wake Executives stay elevated, spike unexpectedly, refuse to go home. That flexibility disappears. Sleep becomes a contest. The pull to sleep is only strong enough to win at the steepest part of the rise, if at all.

For me, this explained a great deal. The frustration. The television until early hours of the morning. I'd been fighting a clock I didn't know existed.

So why do poor sleepers miss the window? Three reasons.

Light. Melatonin production is tightly coupled to the absence of it. Bright light in the evening — particularly the short-wavelength light that screens produce — tells the brain it's still daytime. The signal gets delayed, or somewhat suppressed.

Irregular schedule. Melatonin doesn't operate on demand. It follows a rhythm — and that rhythm is set, gradually, by consistent behaviour. Go to bed and wake up at wildly different times each night and the system loses its reference point. The rise still happens, but it happens at the wrong time, or flattens, or arrives too late.

Stress and hyperarousal. Even when melatonin is present and rising on schedule, a nervous system running too hot can override it. The signal is there. But if the mind is still running — thoughts looping, stuck in go-mode — still activating when it should be calming — melatonin can't win that contest. That's cortisol's problem.

The supplements I'd tried made sense to me back then — but most nights I already found the window. The nights I didn't — those were the problem. Melatonin didn't fix those.

I didn't need more melatonin. My body already wanted to hit the window — it's built to. I just needed to stop working against it.

A consistent bedtime and morning light exposure — both covered in the habit chapters — give the system what it needs to set the rhythm. Do those two reliably and the timing takes care of itself. The window arrives on schedule.

But getting in is only half of it.

The window is about falling asleep. Staying asleep is a different job — and it runs on the same hormone. Remember that melatonin peaks and holds high through the middle of the night? That plateau isn't decoration. It's the signal meant to keep the night quiet — to hold the Sleep Executives in their chairs while the Wake Executives stay home.

For a lot of poor sleepers, that plateau sits lower than it should. The signal still comes, but weaker — less of it through the exact hours when the night is most fragile. And a weak hold is easy to break. A noise, a full bladder, a stray spike of stress — things a strong signal would absorb — are now enough to surface you. You wake at two or three in the morning and can't work out why.

This is the part most of us never see, because we're asleep for it. We notice the window — the frustrating wait to get in. We don't notice the hold quietly failing. But most people who sleep badly aren't one or the other. They're both: slow to get in, quick to come back out.

So melatonin can fall short in two directions. It can arrive too late, or too scattered, to open the window cleanly. And it can run too low through the night to hold it. The good news is the same fix covers both — consistent timing and morning light steady the whole rhythm, and a well-timed signal tends to be a strong one.

You might be thinking: if the signal runs low through the night, why not top it up? Take some melatonin to hold the night, not just to open it.

You're not the first. That's the thinking behind slow-release melatonin — a version built to trickle out over hours instead of arriving in one early pulse, meant to hold a steadier level through the small hours.

It's a sensible idea, and people have chased it — that's what slow-release melatonin is for. But the evidence is thin, and the best it has shown is around thirteen extra minutes of sleep. If you're only getting four hours a night, thirteen minutes is nothing.

So slow-release might earn a place at the edges. It isn't the fix for a night that won't hold.

Melatonin can still be useful — but probably not in the scenario you'd reach for it.

If you've missed your window, the instinct is to take some. The steep rise that opens the window has passed; a small dose might recreate that signal. It's not a bad instinct.

But it works against you. Melatonin is a timing signal — take it late and the body learns to expect sleep later. The morning routine later in the book is the better fix for pulling it back into line.

Jet lag, or adjusting for shift work, are different. Here, shifting the clock is exactly the point. When you cross time zones, or a new roster puts you to bed at a different hour, the melatonin signal isn't disrupted so much as stranded. Your body is still signalling night according to where — or when — it was set. A small dose — 0.5 to 1mg — taken 30 to 60 minutes before your new target bedtime helps nudge the clock toward where it needs to be. This is melatonin doing exactly what it was designed to do: signalling your body with the new sleep time.

One cost worth knowing: dose. Most over-the-counter melatonin runs far higher than what the body naturally produces. Take too much, and residual melatonin can still be circulating by morning, producing grogginess. And because the body responds to the rise rather than the level, a high dose doesn't increase the effect — it just extends it into the morning and gradually blunts your sensitivity to the natural signal. That's why 0.5mg works. It's enough to send the signal. More isn't more.

Looking back, melatonin wasn't my problem.

My body was making melatonin. The signal was being sent — it just wasn't landing when I needed it. For me, that wasn't a deficiency so much as a system working against itself. For others, the overnight signal really does run low, and the night pays for it. Either way, the answer isn't a bottle. A scattered signal and a weak one are, underneath, the same problem — a rhythm that's lost its footing. And you set a rhythm with timing and light, not with a higher dose. Understanding what melatonin actually does — and what it doesn't — at least explained why some nights sleep came the moment my head hit the pillow, and others it didn't come for hours.

Which raises the obvious question. A weak overnight signal makes the night easy to break — but something still has to do the breaking. What keeps the Wake Executives so resistant? What walks back into the building at three in the morning and turns the lights on?

That's cortisol's problem. And cortisol, as it turns out, has a great deal to answer for.
