# Chapter 7 - Cortisol

There are two kinds of sleep problem. Getting to sleep. And staying asleep. For a long time, I had both.

If I had to pick the bigger of the two, mine was staying asleep. But I'm not sure that distinction matters much. If I missed my window — and by now you know how narrow that was — I was lying awake. If I managed to get to sleep, I'd typically wake somewhere between one and three in the morning. Either way, it ended up in the same place. Alert in a way that nobody wants at that hour. Mind running.

Sometimes it was reliving difficulties from the past. Sometimes catastrophising about things that could go wrong. But often it had a positive focus — ways to move forward in life. Getting fitter. House renovations. Things to do with the kids.

Because I wasn't always awake with anxious thoughts, for years I assumed insomnia advice didn't apply. I just had a busy mind.

Being awake with anxiety and being awake with ideas felt like different problems. Most of the sleep advice is aimed at the anxiety version. Mine didn't fit. A busy mind full of half-formed plans didn't have a solution. The feel-good ideas made it harder to name as a problem.

However, what I learned is anxious version or positive version, both involve cortisol.

Cortisol gets a bad reputation. The stress hormone — something to be managed, reduced, kept in check. That's not quite right.

Cortisol is a Wake Executive — the one running the day shift. It's the body's alertness system, sharpening focus and preparing you for whatever the day requires. Before you open your eyes, it's already rising, peaking in the first hour or so after waking, then beginning its long downward curve through the day, declining through the afternoon and into the evening until, by the time sleep approaches, it's low enough for the Sleep Executives to take over. That's the rhythm.

That curve is your baseline. Demand lifts you above it. A difficult meeting, a hard workout, a deadline, a disagreement — each one triggers a temporary spike, giving the system what it needs to respond. Then, when the demand passes, a well-regulated system brings it back down to the line. Spike and return. Spike and return.

That's the system working. But what counts as demand is broader than it looks.

Cortisol has been popularised as the fight or flight hormone. Keeping you alert, ready to act on threat, whether you're in real danger or not. But the popular version is too narrow.

Cortisol actually mobilises you for anything that feels like it needs immediate action.

It gives your body energy and gets your mind alert and focused. This makes sense in the popular definition — if you really were in danger, your mind races through every option for safety. It's solving problems at hyper speed.

It also makes sense when you have a deadline. Or when you're in go-mode, super productive, frantically ploughing through your day, crossing as much as possible off your list.

That's the racing mind.

The nervous system doesn't distinguish between a threat and productivity. Both can feel urgent. And if they feel urgent, they get cortisol.

That's what was happening for me at 2am. Sometimes the thoughts were dark and anxious and they fit the popular definition. But often they weren't — getting fitter, the next holiday, activities with the family. A better version of something. It felt purposeful and productive. Better than lying awake worrying. But cortisol was running either way — keeping the mind racing.

The problem wasn't that cortisol was running. It was when.

Cortisol and melatonin should work in opposition — in the evening as cortisol falls, melatonin rises. When that handover happens cleanly, sleep follows.

This is activating and calming at their clearest. Cortisol is the activating chemistry — while it stays high, calming can't take over for the night. Everything in the program is, one way or another, about getting cortisol to stand down on time. 

This is the mind that won't switch off at night. Not a flaw in you — cortisol, still high when it should be falling. Bring it down on time, and the mind comes down with it.

When the Cortisol executive refuses to pack up and go home, you lie in bed with your eyes open, exhausted and alert, wondering what's wrong with you. And if you do get to sleep, the spike can still find you later. At 2am the cortisol spike collapses whatever the Sleep Executives had in place.

There's a reason it tends to be that hour. Melatonin holds high through the first stretch of the night, then from around 2 am it begins to ease. For a good sleeper that does nothing — they sleep straight through it. But when cortisol is riding higher than it should and  melatonin takes a step back, cortisol jumps right in to fill the gap.

Caffeine makes this harder. Every cup taken too late is a mild cortisol stimulus — a small kick to a system that should be gradually falling toward evening. Caffeine keeps nudging it back up. By evening, instead of a cortisol level low enough for sleep to take over, the baseline is higher than it should be. Every misplaced cup is keeping it from standing down on time.

Here's what I was doing in my worst period.

Lying awake at 2am, I would typically do one of three things — all of which felt entirely reasonable at the time. I'd get up, open the laptop, and start working. Get something done. Turn a bad night into a productive one. Or I'd put on the television — something binge-worthy that had me hooked. Or I'd reach for the phone - Youtube, social media, emails.

This is the pattern modern life has perfected. Not intentionally — the technology doesn't set out to ruin sleep. It's designed to capture attention. And attention costs cortisol.

Before the phone, if you arranged to meet someone and they were late, there was nothing to do but wait. Watch people walk past. Let your mind go where it wanted. The nervous system got downtime, and downtime was exactly what it needed.

That experience is almost gone. The phone fills every gap. Social media feeds are built to surface the most provocative content first — because provocation triggers a response, and a response means engagement. Outrage, excitement, genuine interest — it doesn't matter. The nervous system treats them all the same. Every piece of stimulation is another small bump. Over time, those bumps accumulate. Baseline cortisol creeps up. The evening decline gets shallower. The Sleep Executives are trying to advance, but the Wake Executive never fully leaves the building.

This makes returning to sleep harder. Which increases the frustration. Which spikes cortisol. Which makes sleep harder still. And unfortunately it gets worse.

Sleep deprivation is stress - the body responds just like a threat — by releasing cortisol. Which means a bad night sends cortisol into the next morning already running hot. The baseline higher, the drop shallower. The Sleep Executives facing steeper resistance the next night.

The habits I'd adopted to cope with the wakefulness were the same habits making the wakefulness worse. Getting up to work felt productive. It was the opposite. Binge watching television seemed to block the busy mind. The next day, coffee got me through. Every one of them a signal to the Wake Executive that the shift wasn't over. Every coping mechanism I'd reached for was, in cortisol terms, an accelerant.

The first thing that actually changed wasn't something I found in a research paper.

It was a podcast. One of dozens I was working through at the time, trying to piece together what I could do to fix my sleep. The guest said something simple — almost throwaway: don't do things at night that you'd do during the day. It sends completely the wrong signal.

I didn't fully understand it at the time, but it made sense. I stopped getting up to work. I stopped looking at my phone. I didn't yet know why — I just knew, from that line, that I shouldn't. We'll come back to the television.

It wasn't until years later, when I understood the biology, that the logic clicked into place. The system knows what to do. What's complicated is protecting that rhythm in an environment designed, at every turn, to override it.

Every spike that didn't return to baseline. Every evening habit that kept the Wake Executive at the desk. Every bad night that sent extra cortisol into the next morning, making the whole cycle harder to break.

The signal was never the problem. The rhythm was.

Cortisol at least announces itself. The next one doesn't.
