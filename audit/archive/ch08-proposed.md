# Chapter 8 - Dopamine

Of the four executives, dopamine was the one I understood least. And probably the one doing the quietest damage.

It didn't announce itself. There was no 2am wake-up, no racing mind, no moment where something had obviously gone wrong. The dopamine problem looked completely normal. It looked like everyone else's evening.

On nights when I went to bed before my wife, I'd reach for my phone while I waited. Just to fill the gap. She wasn't there yet, and the phone was on the bedside table. The reach was automatic — barely a decision at all.

What happened next varied. Sometimes ten minutes. Sometimes longer than I'd like to admit. Either way, by the time I put the phone down, something had changed — I wasn't more tired. The quiet that should have been pulling me toward sleep had been replaced by something alert and restless.

I noticed this pattern for a long time before I understood it. Even when I suspected the phone wasn't helping, I didn't know why.

That was dopamine's signature. Quiet. Unremarkable. A system running the wrong way without ever announcing it.

Cortisol pushes you to survive. Dopamine pushes you to thrive.

Dopamine responds to opportunity. The brain releases it to say *this is worth pursuing*. When you're working on something interesting and time flies, that's dopamine. Without it time drags. This is fine during the day. But it requires regulation to bring it down in the evening.

![[Dopamine.png]]

Train it on fast, cheap signals — social media, notification pings, short-form video — and the quieter signals may start to lose out. Including the calm that should be settling you into sleep.

Here's what I was actually doing on those nights with the phone.

The feed I'd built — or more accurately, the feed that had built itself around my behaviour — was heavy on outrage, gender wars, political corruption, woke versus traditional, instant karma clips. The kind of content that triggers a strong reaction — a jolt of stress, cortisol along with it — before you've had time to decide whether you want one.

I knew, on some level, that it wasn't useful. Occasionally I'd catch myself and think: *what are you doing? This is rubbish.* So I built a habit. Whenever I noticed I'd pivot to something else — how-to videos, something educational, or product research on something I wanted to buy. I'm naturally curious, so that kind of content felt better. More productive. More like me.

What I didn't understand was that I'd solved nothing.

Both types of content were doing the same thing to the same system. The outrage feed delivered fast, volatile spikes — the neurological equivalent of a shove, cortisol in the mix. The curiosity content delivered slower, warmer ones — more like a sustained hum, the same dopamine minus the cortisol. Different feeling. Same mechanism. I'd traded what I recognised as a problem for something that felt like a solution. The feeling had changed. The sleep hadn't.

This is how dopamine works against you. The brain doesn't care whether the spike comes from outrage or curiosity, from a conspiracy clip or a product review. What it responds to is novelty and unpredictability — the possibility that the next thing might be interesting. That's what drives the doom scrolling.

When I tuned in to my body, it was most obvious with short-form videos. Interesting. Funny. Outrageous. Boring. Boring. Interesting. The brain stays alert, waiting for the next one.

Over time, the thinking goes, this may recalibrate the reward system: fast, frequent, unpredictable spikes become the baseline, and slower signals — including those coming from the Sleep Executives — start to register as dull by comparison.

That's the sleep problem. The later you stay on the feed, the more it compounds — the dopamine Wake Executive activating, indifferent to the fact that sleep should be happening. And it explains a mistake I kept making.

When I switched to more 'wholesome' content, it felt better, but it wasn't calming. Curiosity still activates you — *this is interesting, I want to know more*. Which is fine during the day, but not when you should be getting off to sleep. 

The contrast that made this clear to me was the television. The right show — low-stakes, predictable, easy to follow — is genuinely passive. The brain isn't anticipating what comes next. It's just following along. That's different to an emotional drama, thriller or suspense. And very different to a feed. I'll come back to what to watch and what to avoid in Chapters 15 and 17. The mechanism is what matters here.

Two things finally pushed me to change.

The first came from that podcast line — the one about not doing daytime things at night.

The second came when I wasn't looking for it.

I was trying to understand why experts were so concerned about screens and gaming for children. The warnings were everywhere — but I wanted to understand the mechanism.

What I found was dopamine.

Gaming, it turns out, is almost perfectly engineered to deliver it. Constant rewards. Immediate feedback. Unpredictable outcomes. Every well-made game is designed to keep the dopamine signal running. The same logic applies to social media. Built entirely to hook you in and keep you there.

At first I thought - surely this didn't apply to me - I wasn't a child. I wasn't gaming. I was an adult. I could watch product research and how-to videos. Surely that was different.

Maybe it is different. Maybe it doesn't affect adults as much as children. I'm not sure.

What I do know is that the mechanism doesn't change. Dopamine at night is working directly against the Sleep Executives. It doesn't matter whether the spike comes from gaming, swiping through short-form video, or a deep-dive into espresso machines. The signal is the signal.

Looking back, that pre-bed scrolling was almost certainly pushing me past my sleep window some nights. Enough dopamine — and the cortisol that came with it — to carry me through to the point where the steep rise had passed. By the time I put the phone down, the window had closed.

I hadn't been unlucky on those nights, I'd been scrolling through my own sleep window.

That was the fourth executive — the last piece of the picture.

Understanding that took years. And here's the thing about understanding — it's necessary, but it isn't sufficient. The biology made sense to me long before my behaviour caught up with it.

Which is where the next part of this book begins. What comes next isn't more explanation. It's the part where it becomes yours to do.
