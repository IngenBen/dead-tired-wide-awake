# Ch06 Audit — Beat Analysis
*Sleep Incorporated — Chapter 6: Melatonin*

---

## Stage 1: Beat-by-beat analysis

The chapter has 12 distinct beats. Main messages: melatonin is a timing signal not a sedative; production is rarely the problem; the sleep window is the brief alignment of conditions that makes sleep possible; supplements are useful only in two specific scenarios.

Only beats with problems are reported below.

---

**Beat 5 — Three blockers (paras 27–35)**
> "Three things, mostly. And they're worth naming clearly before Chapter 7 gets to make its much louder argument about one of them."

⚠️ **Arch meta-reference.** "Chapter 7 gets to make its much louder argument" breaks the chapter's own voice — it's slightly performative and self-aware in a way that calls attention to the book's architecture rather than the reader's experience. The forward tease is the right instinct, but the phrasing is too knowing.

Suggested fix — replace with a forward pull that stays in the chapter's own register:

> ~~"They're worth naming clearly before Chapter 7 gets to make its much louder argument about one of them."~~

> **"They're worth naming clearly — one of them will get a chapter of its own."**

Context check: para 35 ("The signal is there. The body just isn't listening.") follows cleanly from either version. No new transition issue introduced.

---

**Beat 6 — Sleep window mechanics (paras 43–45)**
> "Miss it, and the alignment breaks... Miss it, and the Wake Executives recover. And you lie in bed wondering what's wrong with you."

⚠️ **Triple "Miss it" / under-explained mechanics.** The phrase recurs three times: opening narrative (para 8), mechanics section (para 41), and again at para 45. Three instances tips from rhythm into tic. The passage also tells the reader *that* missing the window matters without fully showing *why*.

Author's rewrite (accepted with one trim):

> ~~"For someone with a well-regulated sleep system, the window barely registers — the Wake Executives aren't fighting hard, so the Sleep Executives don't need to be at their peak. For a chronic poor sleeper, it's different: the Wake Executives stay elevated, spike unexpectedly, refuse to yield. Sleep becomes contested territory."~~
>
> ~~"The window is the brief period when the Sleep Executives gain ground — melatonin climbing, adenosine pressing, the balance tipping. Miss it, and the Wake Executives recover. And you lie in bed wondering what's wrong with you."~~

> **"For someone with a well-regulated sleep system, the window barely registers — the Wake Executives are ready to hand over. The Sleep Executives don't have to battle them out the door.**
>
> **For a chronic poor sleeper, it's different: the Wake Executives stay elevated, spike unexpectedly, refuse to go home. Sleep becomes a contest.**
>
> **The window is the brief period when the Sleep Executives are at their strongest — melatonin climbing, adenosine pressing. Miss that opportunity and the Sleep Executives lose their momentum. The Wake Executives won't budge. And you lie in bed wondering what's wrong with you."**

Context check: para 47 ("Nothing is. The window closed.") lands cleanly. No new transition or repetition issue.

---

**Beat 9 — Supplement experiment (paras 53–57)**
> "At some point during my research phase, I bought melatonin supplements."

⚠️ **Abrupt pivot.** Para 51 closes the window-mechanics section with a strong personal reflection ("For me, this explained a great deal..."). Para 53 then opens a new beat — the supplement anecdote — with a cold start. The jump from "I'd been fighting a clock I didn't know existed" to "I bought melatonin supplements" loses the connective tissue that would make the pivot feel motivated.

Suggested fix — add a single bridging sentence:

> ~~"At some point during my research phase, I bought melatonin supplements."~~

> **"Knowing this, I did what most people do. At some point during my research phase, I bought melatonin supplements."**

---

**Beat 9 continued — Para 57**
> "It didn't do much for me, because most of the time I hit my sleep window anyway."

⚠️ **Slight contradiction with the opening.** The chapter opens with the author struggling badly to hit the window — "Miss it, and the night was gone." Para 57 then says he hit it "most of the time." These aren't irreconcilable — the opening describes a worst-case experience, and "most of the time" is consistent with occasionally missing it — but on first read it creates a small confusion about how severe the problem actually was.

Suggested fix — a small qualifier restores coherence:

> ~~"It didn't do much for me, because most of the time I hit my sleep window anyway."~~

> **"It didn't do much for me, because on most nights I hit my sleep window eventually — just later than I'd have liked."**

Context check: para 59 ("I was trying to use a timing tool to solve a structural problem") follows cleanly from either version. No new issue introduced.

---

**Beat 10 — Two useful scenarios (para 61)**
> "Like you're sending a reminder: its sleep time now."

⚠️ **Typo.** "its" → "it's"

> ~~"its sleep time now"~~

> **"it's sleep time now"**

## Stage 2: Category-by-category review

---

### Category 1 — Contradictions

No cross-chapter contradictions found. Two internal issues.

---

**Contradiction 1 — Missed window dose vs. grogginess risk**

Para 61 recommends a small melatonin dose when you've missed your window — i.e., taken late at night. Para 67 warns that residual melatonin circulating by morning causes grogginess. The chapter treats these as separate scenarios, but they aren't: taking a dose after missing your window *is* taking it late, which is precisely when the grogginess risk is highest. The chapter doesn't acknowledge this tension, leaving the reader with advice that partially contradicts the caveat that follows.

Suggested fix — add a bridging clause in para 67 that acknowledges the overlap:

> ~~"The first is dose. Most over-the-counter melatonin runs far higher than what the body naturally produces. Take too much, and residual melatonin can still be circulating by morning, producing grogginess. But this is a dose problem, not a melatonin problem."~~

> **"The first is dose. Most over-the-counter melatonin runs far higher than what the body naturally produces. Take too much — or take even a small dose too late in the night — and residual melatonin can still be circulating by morning, producing grogginess. But this is a dose and timing problem, not a melatonin problem."**

Context check: the sentence that follows ("Research suggests 0.5mg is sufficient for most people") lands cleanly. No new issue introduced.

---

**Contradiction 2 — Adenosine stays high, but the second wind arrives**

Para 41 states that missing the window leaves adenosine high — sleep pressure is still there. Para 49 then describes the second wind: "suddenly more awake than you were an hour ago." These aren't irreconcilable — the circadian system can override adenosine — but the chapter doesn't explain the mechanism. A reader who has just been told adenosine keeps pressing will find the second wind confusing: if the pressure is still there, why does the body move toward wakefulness?

This is a light flag rather than a hard contradiction. The chapter doesn't need a scientific detour, but one phrase would close the gap:

> ~~"Push further, and something else happens. As the night progresses, melatonin begins to fall."~~

> **"Push further, and something else happens. Even with adenosine still pressing, the circadian system moves on. As the night progresses, melatonin begins to fall."**

Context check: the sentences that follow ("The circadian system starts preparing for the next day regardless...") are now set up rather than unexplained. No new issue introduced.

### Category 2 — Cadence and flow

---

**Cadence 1 — Para 19: four consecutive short sentences saying the same thing**

> "But the signal doesn't force sleep. It enables it. Melatonin doesn't knock you out. It moves you toward the conditions where sleep becomes possible."

Four short declarative sentences in a row, each restating the same idea from a slightly different angle. In the author's style, stacked fragments are a deliberate tool — but here they perform emphasis rather than earn it. The first and fourth are the strongest; the middle two ("It enables it. Melatonin doesn't knock you out.") are redundant with each other.

Suggested fix — collapse to three, with the middle pair merged:

> ~~"But the signal doesn't force sleep. It enables it. Melatonin doesn't knock you out. It moves you toward the conditions where sleep becomes possible."~~

> **"But the signal doesn't force sleep — it enables it. Melatonin doesn't knock you out. It moves you toward the conditions where sleep becomes possible."**

Context check: para 21 ("The wrong assumption most people carry...") follows cleanly. No new issue.

---

**Cadence 2 — Para 59: capitalisation error mid-sentence**

> "I was trying to use a timing tool to solve a structural problem. I now know Melatonin is useful in two specific scenarios."

"Melatonin" is capitalised mid-sentence — inconsistent with usage throughout the rest of the chapter.

> ~~"I now know Melatonin is useful"~~

> **"I now know melatonin is useful"**

---

**Cadence 3 — Paras 61–63: rhythm shift in the two-scenario section**

These are the chapter's two longest paragraphs by some distance. After the short-sentence rhythm of the window mechanics and supplement anecdote, the pace slows noticeably here. This isn't wrong — the content is specific and instructional, and that warrants a slightly different register — but para 61 in particular could be broken into two without losing anything.

Suggested fix — split para 61 at the dose detail:

> ~~"The first is when you've missed your window. If you've pushed past your normal sleep time — a social event, shift work, something that ran long — the clock hasn't stopped. It's moved on without you. Your melatonin signal has peaked and begun to fall. A small dose at this point — 0.5 to 1mg, not the 5mg tablets most shops sell — can help to create a new peak in your body. Like you're sending a reminder: it's sleep time now."~~

> **"The first is when you've missed your window. If you've pushed past your normal sleep time — a social event, shift work, something that ran long — the clock hasn't stopped. It's moved on without you. Your melatonin signal has peaked and begun to fall.**
>
> **A small dose at this point — 0.5 to 1mg, not the 5mg tablets most shops sell — can help to create a new peak in your body. Like you're sending a reminder: it's sleep time now."**

Context check: para 63 (jet lag scenario) follows cleanly from either version. No new issue.

### Category 3 — Weak points

---

**Weak point 1 — Para 25: Sleep Incorporated metaphor feels forced**

> "In Sleep Incorporated terms: this executive almost always shows up for work. The question is whether the message gets through."

This adds nothing to what the previous two paragraphs already established — that production isn't the problem, timing and conditions are. The metaphor restatement feels like a tic rather than a useful frame here, and "almost always shows up for work" is vague where the surrounding prose is specific. The paragraph can be cut without loss.

Suggested fix — delete para 25 entirely.

Context check: para 23 ("For most people, production isn't the problem...") connects directly to para 27 ("So what stops the message getting through?") with no gap. The transition is actually cleaner without it.

---

**Weak point 2 — Para 37: core body temperature introduced without context**

> "The sleep window opens when several signals converge: melatonin rising steeply, adenosine high from a full day of wakefulness, cortisol low, core body temperature falling."

Three of the four signals have been explained in this chapter or earlier ones. Core body temperature dropping has not. It appears here as an equal member of the list with no explanation of what causes it, why it matters, or whether the reader can influence it. For a book built on leverage points, listing an unexplained signal the reader can do nothing with is a weak move.

Two options:

Option A — cut it from the list, keeping only the signals the book has covered:

> ~~"melatonin rising steeply, adenosine high from a full day of wakefulness, cortisol low, core body temperature falling"~~

> **"melatonin rising steeply, adenosine high from a full day of wakefulness, cortisol low"**

Option B — add a single explanatory clause if the author wants to keep it:

> **"melatonin rising steeply, adenosine high from a full day of wakefulness, cortisol low, core body temperature falling as the body prepares for repair"**

Option A is cleaner and consistent with the book's focus on actionable signals.

---

**Weak point 3 — Para 33: irregular schedule mentions bedtime only**

> "Go to bed at wildly different times each night and the system loses its reference point."

The chapter later (para 71) points to a consistent *wake* time as the primary circadian anchor — which is well established in sleep science and covered more fully in the morning routine chapter. Mentioning only bedtime here is slightly incomplete and could give the reader the wrong lever to reach for.

Suggested fix — add wake time:

> ~~"Go to bed at wildly different times each night and the system loses its reference point."~~

> **"Go to bed — and wake up — at wildly different times each night and the system loses its reference point."**

---

**Weak point 4 — Para 63: jet lag advice less specific than missed-window advice**

The missed-window scenario (para 61) gives a specific dose range (0.5–1mg) and notes what not to take (5mg tablets). The jet lag scenario gives no dose guidance at all — just "a small dose." The asymmetry leaves the reader with an incomplete instruction for the second scenario.

Suggested fix — mirror the specificity of para 61:

> ~~"A small dose taken at local bedtime in the new time zone helps nudge the clock toward where it needs to be."~~

> **"A small dose — again, 0.5 to 1mg — taken at local bedtime in the new time zone helps nudge the clock toward where it needs to be."**

### Category 4 — Over-explaining

Cross-chapter check: consulted index for hormone-misalignment (ch04, ch06, ch07, ch08, ch19) and melatonin (ch04, ch06). "Tired but wired" is a ch04 coinage used as shorthand in para 41 — this is a deliberate callback, not over-explaining. No cross-chapter issue.

---

**Over-explaining 1 — Paras 21–23: wrong assumption and correction stated twice**

> "The wrong assumption most people carry — I carried it for years — is that poor sleep must mean low melatonin. That if the signal isn't working, the body isn't making enough of it. It's a reasonable guess. It's also almost always wrong.
>
> For most people, production isn't the problem. The melatonin is there. What goes wrong is the timing, or the conditions that allow the signal to land."

Para 23 opens by restating what para 21 just established ("production isn't the problem / almost always wrong"). The new content in para 23 is "timing, or the conditions that allow the signal to land" — that's the part worth keeping. The restatement before it is redundant.

Suggested fix — cut the restatement, open para 23 on the new content:

> ~~"For most people, production isn't the problem. The melatonin is there. What goes wrong is the timing, or the conditions that allow the signal to land."~~

> **"What goes wrong is the timing, or the conditions that allow the signal to land."**

Context check: para 21 ends "It's also almost always wrong." Para 23 (now a single sentence) follows cleanly and moves the argument forward. Para 27 ("So what stops the message getting through?") still lands well. No new issue.

---

**Over-explaining 2 — Paras 75–77: double closing**

> "But understanding what melatonin actually does — and what it doesn't — at least explained why the window kept closing.
>
> That's the melatonin story — and it's mostly a story about timing."

Both sentences close the melatonin discussion before the handoff to cortisol. The first is the personal landing; the second is a chapter summary. Together they stall the momentum. One should go — and since the personal landing (para 75) is the stronger, more in-voice closer, para 77's summary sentence is the one to cut.

Suggested fix — cut "That's the melatonin story — and it's mostly a story about timing." and run the handoff directly from para 75:

> ~~"That's the melatonin story — and it's mostly a story about timing. Which raises the obvious question: what was keeping the Wake Executives so resistant in the first place? What was stopping the system from standing down?"~~

> **"Which raises the obvious question: what was keeping the Wake Executives so resistant in the first place? What was stopping the system from standing down?"**

Context check: the closing of para 75 ("at least explained why the window kept closing") now flows directly into the question, which gives it more urgency. The final line ("That's cortisol's problem...") lands with more punch. No new issue.

### Category 5 — Repetition

### Category 6 — Abrupt transitions

---

**Transition 1 — Paras 35→37: blockers section to sleep window**

> "The signal is there. The body just isn't listening.
>
> The sleep window opens when several signals converge..."

The blockers section ends on a negative — things that prevent the signal landing. The sleep window section opens on a positive — what happens when conditions align. The connection is implied (if those things block the signal, here's what it looks like when they don't) but the jump is unexplained. A reader following the logic has to do a small amount of work to bridge the two.

Suggested fix — add a single bridging sentence:

> ~~"The signal is there. The body just isn't listening."~~

> **"The signal is there. The body just isn't listening.**
>
> **But when those conditions are met — when nothing is blocking the signal — something brief and reliable happens."**

> "The sleep window opens when several signals converge..."

Context check: the bridge sets up "The sleep window opens" cleanly and the new sentence doesn't introduce any new idea, just connects two existing ones. No new issue.

---

**Transition 2 — Paras 71→73: caveats close to personal reflection**

> "Bright light and a consistent wake time are the most powerful tools for pulling the clock back into line.
>
> Melatonin wasn't my main problem. Not really."

Para 71 closes the caveats section on a practical, forward-looking note about the morning routine. Para 73 pivots abruptly back to personal reflection. The shift in register — from instruction to memoir — is jarring without a bridge.

Suggested fix — a single transitional phrase:

> ~~"Melatonin wasn't my main problem. Not really."~~

> **"Looking back, melatonin wasn't my main problem. Not really."**

"Looking back" signals the register shift and softens the pivot without adding length.
