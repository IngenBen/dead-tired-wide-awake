## Evidence summary (for the decision)

| Goal | Dose | Timing | What to expect | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fall asleep faster (tonight) | Up to ~4 mg; nothing more above that | 2–3 h before usual bedtime; at bedtime only ~3–4 min faster | ~10–20 min faster; a little more sleep, mostly because of the faster start | Moderate (Cruz-Sanabria 2024) |
| Night owl, work night (tonight) | 0.5 mg | 1 h before desired bedtime, bed at that time | Habitual sleep ~1:30 am; bed moved ~2½ h earlier in one jump; asleep ~1 h 50 earlier on melatonin vs ~1 h 20 on placebo (the 34-min result); most still short of goal; clock not significantly shifted | Strong (Sletten 2018) |
| Day sleep after night shift (tonight) | 1–10 mg, no dose effect | Before daytime sleep | Maybe ~20–25 min more sleep, likely by lasting longer; no faster onset | Weak (Liira 2014); mechanism Sack 2007 |
| Fewer night wakings (older adults) | ~0.3 mg; 3 mg did less | 30 min before bed | ~40–45 min less awake over the night | Weak (Zhdanova 2001) |
| Move the clock earlier (for good) | 0.5 mg = 3 mg | Several hours before the body's melatonin rise (early evening), timed to the current clock | ~1.5 h over 3 days alone; ~2.5 h in 3 days with earlier waking and morning light; durability untested | Strong lab (Burgess 2010; Revell 2006) |
| Jet lag (both) | 0.5–5 mg; 5 mg helps sleep onset a bit more; nothing above 5 mg | New local bedtime, first few nights; not early in the local day | Less jet lag, most across 5+ zones, flying east | Moderate–strong (Herxheimer 2002; Suhner 1998) |
| Roster: the clock itself | n/a | Light: bright at work early in the run, dark glasses home, dark bedroom | Under 3% of permanent night workers fully adjust; light gives a workable compromise | Moderate (Folkard 2008; Lowden 2019; Smith 2009; Sack 2007) |
| Next-morning carryover | Keep low | n/a | 3–4 mg keeps melatonin raised ~10 h; 0.3–0.4 mg clears by morning | Moderate for levels (Zhdanova 2001; Gooneratne 2012) |

## Endnote proposals (Chapter 6, reading order)

The endnotes can carry more detail than the prose; these follow the minimal edits. Rebuild into the KB first, then compile.

### N1 (DROPPED 2026-10-28 with edit D): **Does melatonin help less when stress is keeping you awake?**

It appears to. In pooled trials, melatonin shortened the time to fall asleep less in people with insomnia than in healthy volunteers, and the authors suggest the aroused state of insomnia interferes with it, much as light and alerting activity do. This fits the view of insomnia as a state of heightened arousal. The explanation has not been tested directly, and part of the gap may reflect study design, since most healthy-volunteer studies were single nights in a laboratory.

Cruz-Sanabria F et al. 2024, *J Pineal Res* 76(5):e12985. https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.12985 (dose-response meta-analysis of 26 randomised trials; insomnia status predicted a smaller sleep-onset effect, robust to study quality; arousal proposed as the reason)
Riemann D et al. 2010, *Sleep Med Rev* 14:19–31. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2009.04.002 (hyperarousal model of insomnia)
Dressle RJ et al. 2022, *Sleep Med Rev* 62:101588. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2022.101588 (meta-analysis of 20 case-control studies; moderately higher cortisol in insomnia; abstract only)

### N2 (new, for lines 63–65): **Does melatonin help once you've missed the window?**

Taken at bedtime, melatonin shortens the time it takes to fall asleep by only a few minutes on average, and it has not been tested in people already lying awake past their usual bedtime, when the body's own melatonin is well into its evening rise. Pooled trials suggest the effect is several times larger when it is taken two to three hours before bed, while an hour before was not clearly better than at bedtime; these estimates come from a statistical model, and few trials tested earlier times. Taken around bedtime it has little effect on the body clock, and taken towards the end of the night it can push the clock later.

Cruz-Sanabria F et al. 2024, *J Pineal Res* 76(5):e12985. https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.12985 (dose-response meta-analysis of 26 randomised trials, mostly short lab studies; modelled sleep onset about 3–4 min faster than placebo when taken at bedtime and about 13–20 min faster when taken 2–3 h before; 1 h before not significantly better than at bedtime; possible publication bias)
Zeitzer JM et al. 2007, *Sleep* 30(11):1437–1443. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/30.11.1437 (hourly-averaged plasma melatonin in 90 young men: a steep rise across the hours around lights out, peaking in the middle of the night)
Sletten TL et al. 2010, *Front Neurol* 1:137. https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2010.00137 (28 young adults; salivary melatonin onset about 2 hours before sleep onset, with wide individual variation)
Burgess HJ et al. 2010, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 95(7):3325–3331. https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2009-2590 (34 healthy adults in a lab; little clock effect from a dose at bedtime; doses near the end of sleep can delay the clock)

### N3 (replaces the jet-lag part of "When is taking melatonin genuinely useful?"): **How should melatonin be used for jet lag?**

Melatonin taken at the new local bedtime for the first few nights reduces jet lag, with the clearest benefit after crossing five or more time zones, particularly flying east. Doses from about 0.5 to 5 mg work similarly. The higher dose helps people fall asleep somewhat faster, though by how much was not reported, and doses above 5 mg add nothing. Taken early in the local day, it can cause sleepiness and slow adjustment. Shifting the clock before an eastward flight, by getting up earlier each day into bright morning light with a small afternoon dose, has worked in the lab but has not been tested on real flights.

Herxheimer A, Petrie KJ 2002, *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* (2):CD001520. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001520 (Cochrane review of 10 trials; 9 found less jet lag when taken close to target bedtime after crossing five or more time zones; 0.5–5 mg similarly effective, with faster sleep after 5 mg; no gain above 5 mg; taken at the wrong time it can delay adjustment)
Suhner A et al. 1998, *Chronobiol Int* 15(6):655–666. https://doi.org/10.3109/07420529808993201 (320 travellers flying east across 6–8 time zones, 234 completed; 0.5 mg nearly as effective as 5 mg; 5 mg better for sleep onset and self-rated sleep quality, size not given; sleep self-reported; abstract only)
Revell VL et al. 2006, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 91(1):54–59. https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2005-1009 (44 healthy adults; 3 days of earlier waking, morning bright light and afternoon melatonin moved the clock about 2.5 h on 0.5 or 3 mg vs 1.7 h on placebo; lab only; abstract only)
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin (fact sheet; jet lag and delayed or advanced sleep phase; 0.5–5 mg similarly effective)

### N4 (new, split from the same note): **Does melatonin help shift workers adjust?**

Very few permanent night workers fully adjust their body clock to night work, and most adjust too little to benefit, largely because daylight on the way home and on days off keeps pulling the clock back. Sleep in the body's daytime tends to be cut short as the clock's alerting signal rises. Melatonin before daytime sleep may help that sleep last a little longer but does not help people fall asleep faster, and the evidence is weak. Managing light has better support as the main tool, with bright light early in a run of night shifts and light blocked on the commute home and in the bedroom, although most of the trials are small.

Folkard S 2008, *Chronobiol Int* 25(2):215–224. https://doi.org/10.1080/07420520802106835 (review of six studies of permanent night workers; fewer than 3% fully adjusted and fewer than 1 in 4 enough to benefit; abstract only)
Sack RL et al. 2007, *Sleep* 30(11):1460–1483. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/30.11.1460 (American Academy of Sleep Medicine review; daytime sleep shortened by the clock's alerting signal; continued daylight exposure keeps night workers' clocks from shifting; dark goggles on the commute improve adaptation)
Åkerstedt T 2003, *Occup Med (Lond)* 53(2):89–94. https://doi.org/10.1093/occmed/kqg046 (review; shortened sleep and sleepiness at work are the main acute problems of shift work; abstract only)
Liira J et al. 2014, *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* (8):CD009776. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009776.pub2 (Cochrane review; 1–10 mg after a night shift may add about 24 min of daytime sleep; low-quality evidence; no faster sleep onset)
Lowden A et al. 2019, *Ind Health* 57(2):213–227. https://doi.org/10.2486/indhealth.SW-9 (Working Time Society and ICOH consensus statement; bright light in the first three night shifts and orange glasses on the commute home; abstract only)
Smith MR, Fogg LF, Eastman CI 2009, *J Biol Rhythms* 24(2):161–172. https://doi.org/10.1177/0748730409332068 (19 healthy adults on simulated night shifts; bright light at work, dark sunglasses outside and a dark bedroom shifted the clock enough to improve night-shift performance; abstract only)

### N5 (new, split from the same note): **Can melatonin help you move your bedtime earlier?**

In people diagnosed with a body clock that runs late, who usually fell asleep around half past one, a small dose taken about an hour before an earlier bedtime they had chosen, with bed at that time on most nights, brought sleep about half an hour earlier than a dummy pill over four weeks. Most of the shift came from the earlier bedtime itself, most people did not fully reach their goal, and the body clock did not move significantly in that time. Getting up earlier each day into bright morning light moves the clock itself, and in laboratory studies a small evening dose moved it as far as a larger one. Whether the change lasts has not been tested; very late body clocks often run naturally long and tend to drift back, and a sleep specialist can confirm the diagnosis.

Sletten TL et al. 2018, *PLoS Med* 15(6):e1002587. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002587 (Australian randomised trial, 116 people with delayed sleep-wake phase disorder and a confirmed late clock; habitual sleep onset about 1:20–1:30 am, desired bedtime about 10:30 pm; 0.5 mg 1 h before desired bedtime with bed at that time, at least 5 nights a week for 4 weeks; sleep onset moved about 1 h 50 min earlier vs 1 h 20 min on placebo; 53% vs 24% rated much improved; clock shift not significant)
Revell VL et al. 2006, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 91(1):54–59. https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2005-1009 (44 healthy adults; with earlier waking and morning bright light, afternoon 0.5 or 3 mg moved the clock nearly 1 h a day; abstract only)
Burgess HJ et al. 2010, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 95(7):3325–3331. https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2009-2590 (0.5 mg moved the clock about as far as 3 mg when each was taken at its best time)
Micic G et al. 2016, *J Biol Rhythms* 31(4):387–405. https://doi.org/10.1177/0748730416650069 (26 young adults with delayed sleep-wake phase disorder vs 18 controls; clocks ran about 12 min a day longer; abstract only)
Auger RR et al. 2015, *J Clin Sleep Med* 11(10):1199–1236. https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.5100 (American Academy of Sleep Medicine guideline; strategically timed melatonin endorsed at a second-tier level of confidence for delayed sleep-wake phase disorder)

### 1. Ch6, lines 43–45: clock problem vs stress problem (new)

Context (lines 41–43, unchanged):

> **Irregular schedule**. Melatonin doesn't operate on demand. It follows a rhythm, and that rhythm is trained, gradually, by consistent behaviour. Go to bed and wake up at wildly different times each night and the system loses its reference point. The rise still happens, but it happens at the wrong time, or flattens, or arrives too late.
>
> **Stress and hyperarousal**. Even when melatonin is present and rising on schedule, a nervous system running too hot can override it. The signal is there. But if the mind is still running (thoughts looping, stuck in go-mode, still activating when it should be calming), melatonin can't win that contest. That's cortisol's problem.

### Appendix N: mechanism note (moved to KB)

### N3 (new, for the start of lines 71–73): **Why does melatonin help some people fall asleep and others stay asleep?**

Whether sleep comes depends on two forces together: sleep pressure, which builds while awake and falls during sleep, and the body clock, which pushes for wakefulness through the biological day and most strongly in the hours before habitual bedtime. Melatonin's direct effect appears to be quietening that clock-driven push, so it helps where the push is the obstacle and only while it is in the blood. In people trying to sleep earlier than their clock allows, that is the start of the night, and a dose an hour before bed helped them fall asleep sooner without moving the clock much. After a night shift, high sleep pressure gets sleep started, but sleep in the body's daytime is cut short as the clock's alerting signal rises; melatonin before daytime sleep may help it last a little longer but does not speed sleep onset. Taken in the early evening, hours before the body's own melatonin rise, it moves the clock itself. Each step is supported by research; joining them into one explanation is an interpretation rather than a tested finding.

Dijk DJ, Czeisler CA 1994, *Neurosci Lett* 166(1):63–68. https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3940(94)90841-9 (8 men over 33–36 days without time cues; body clock and sleep pressure contributed about equally to staying asleep; their normal timing keeps sleep consolidated at night; abstract only)
