## Ch03.1 — CBN "even more targeted for sleep than CBD" · accept + endnote (summary plus sources)

Attaches at the CBN passage in Ch03 (the forum claim that CBN is "even more targeted for sleep than CBD"). No prose change.

There's some evidence cannabinoids can aid sleep, but it's weak — a 2025 meta-analysis found the benefit came mainly from THC-type compounds, with no significant effect for CBD alone, and the only human CBN trial showed just a small reduction in night-time waking. ## Ch08.14 — passive, predictable TV is "genuinely different" · accept as-is + endnote (directional)

Attaches at the "passive, predictable TV is genuinely different" sentence in Ch08. No prose change —
the endnote just flags that this is a reasoned inference, not a measured finding.

This is me reasoning from the mechanism rather than pointing to a study. We know dopamine
tracks anticipation and reward, and that novelty and unpredictability are what drive it
hardest — so a show that's predictable and low-stakes should give the brain much less to
chase than a feed or a suspenseful drama. That's the direction the science points, but I
haven't found a head-to-head study putting passive television next to interactive media, so
treat it as a sensible extension of the dopamine story, not a proven contrast.

## Ch10.16 — HRV "balancing its two modes" · accept as-is + endnote

Attaches at the HRV sentence in Ch10 (marker ⟦EN:Ch10.16⟧).

It's not quite as tidy as "two modes" makes it sound — the way the activated and calm systems
combine to produce a heart-rate-variability number is complex and not strictly proportional,
so don't read HRV as a clean dial showing the exact balance. But the everyday version holds up

can watch it move as your habits change. That's all you need it for here.

## Ch11.7 — phosphatidylserine, ~400 mg, before bed · edit + endnote

Attaches at the PS dosage sentence in Ch11 (marker ⟦EN:Ch11.7⟧).

The evidence that PS blunts cortisol comes mainly from an older study where 400–800 mg a day
softened the cortisol spike triggered by hard physical exercise — not evening or pre-sleep
cortisol specifically, and using a brain-cortex form that's since been replaced by the soy- and
sunflower-derived versions you'll find today. So 400 mg is a sensible dose to settle on, and
taking it before bed is a practical choice that fits the routine rather than something those
cortisol studies actually pinned down.

Sources:

- Monteleone P et al. 1992, *Eur J Clin Pharmacol* 42:385–388 — phosphatidylserine blunted the ACTH/cortisol response to physical stress (400–800 mg/day, brain-cortex PS). https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00280123

## Ch11.10 — magnesium L-threonate "crosses into the brain more readily" · edit + endnote

Attaches at the L-threonate claim in Ch11 (marker ⟦EN:Ch11.10⟧).

The reason L-threonate gets singled out for sleep is that animal studies show it raises
magnesium levels in the brain more than other forms do. That edge mostly rests on those animal
studies, though — and to be straight about it, the human evidence that any magnesium form
meaningfully improves sleep is still thin and low-quality. I take it because it's gentle on my
stomach and the tracker liked it, not because the science is settled.

Sources:

## Ch11.11 — magnesium dose (elemental vs compound) · edit + endnote

Attaches at the magnesium dosing sentence in Ch11 (marker ⟦EN:Ch11.11⟧).

A quick note on the numbers, because the labels trip people up. Magnesium supplements are
usually labelled by the weight of the whole compound, most of which isn't magnesium — so a
2-gram serving of magnesium L-threonate delivers only about 144 mg of actual (elemental)
magnesium. That's why the L-threonate figure on the bottle looks so much larger than the
citrate or glycinate one even though they're in the same ballpark for magnesium itself.
Research servings of L-threonate are around 1.5–2 g of the compound, often split between
morning and night rather than taken all at once in the evening.

Sources:

- Liu G, Weinger JG, Lu Z-L, Xue F, Sadeghpour S 2016, *J Alzheimers Dis* 49(4):971–990 — RCT of MMFS-01 (magnesium L-threonate); research dosing was 1.5–2 g/day of the compound, split between morning and evening. https://doi.org/10.3233/JAD-150538
- Elemental-magnesium conversion: magnesium L-threonate is only ~7–8% magnesium by weight, so a ~2 g (2000 mg) serving of the compound supplies ~144 mg of elemental magnesium. This per-serving figure is stated on manufacturer labelling (e.g. Magtein®/Sports Research); there is no peer-reviewed source for it — it follows arithmetically from the compound's molecular weight.

## Ch12.9 — temperature is "wired into the biology of sleep itself" · edit + endnote

Attaches at the reworded opening sentence of the temperature section (marker ⟦EN:Ch12.9⟧).

I've put it this way — that temperature is part of the sleep mechanism rather than the factor
with "the most direct connection" — because that's what the research actually shows. When the
body can't cool the way it needs to, sleep suffers across the board: a review of the thermal
environment found that heat or cold exposure increases wakefulness and cuts both REM and deep
(slow-wave) sleep, and that these effects are tied directly to thermoregulation. It's not that
temperature out-ranks light or noise on some scale; it's that cooling down is built into how
sleep starts and holds.

Sources:

- Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *Journal of Physiological Anthropology* 31:14 — "The stereotypical effects of heat or cold exposure are increased wakefulness and decreased rapid eye movement sleep and slow wave sleep. These effects of the thermal environment on sleep stages are strongly linked to thermoregulation." https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14

## Ch12.15 — the near-naked "around 29 °C" figure · accept as-is + endnote (summary plus sources)

New prose added in the temperature-section restructure (not an original-book claim ID — keyed
Ch12.15 as the next free number). Attaches at the near-naked-sleeper sentence (marker
⟦EN:Ch12.15⟧): "...the ideal room temperature turned out to be around 29 degrees Celsius."

To be straight about where that 29 °C comes from: it's the comfortable point from old laboratory
studies where people slept nearly naked — just shorts, no covers at all — a small, dated
experiment rather than a modern, real-bedroom trial. So I'm using it to make a point, not to tell
you to set your thermostat to 29. What's far better established is the flip side: once you're under
bedding, the warm layer trapped against your skin stays fairly steady whatever the room is doing,
which is why a cool room and a warm one can give you much the same night's sleep. The room was
never really the lever. The covers are.

Sources:

- Haskell EH, Palca JW, Walker JM, Berger RJ, Heller HC 1981, *Electroencephalography and Clinical Neurophysiology* 51(5):494–501 — six men slept nude except for shorts; thermoneutrality (the least disruptive condition) was ~29 °C, and cold was generally more disruptive than heat. *Credibility caveat: small (n=6), near-naked, no bedding, 1981 — illustrative of the principle, not a room-temperature prescription.* https://doi.org/10.1016/0013-4694(81)90226-1
- Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *Journal of Physiological Anthropology* 31:14 — review: with bedding/clothing the "bed climate" stays around 32–34 °C and sleep is unaffected across a wide room range, so bed coverings — not room air — set the temperature the body actually feels. https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14

## Ch13.6 / 13.7 — caffeine's half-life · edit + endnote

Attaches at the end of the half-life worked example (marker ⟦EN:Ch13.6⟧).

A note on the number, because I had it wrong for years. I'd read "six-hour half-life" and run
with it, but caffeine actually clears a bit faster than that — most of the pharmacology puts
the half-life at around four to five hours. The European food-safety review landed near four
hours, and a 2026 clearance study agreed. It varies a lot from person to person — pregnancy,
some medications and individual liver differences can stretch it well past that — so I've used
five hours here as a fair middle. The lesson holds at any figure in the range: a morning habit
is still working on you at night.

Sources:

- Agboola et al. 2026, *Current Therapeutic Research, Clinical and Experimental* 104:100832 — caffeine pharmacokinetics (half-life ~4 h). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.curtheres.2026.100832
- EFSA 2015, *Scientific Opinion on the safety of caffeine*, *EFSA Journal* 13(5):4102 — plasma half-life ~4 h in healthy adults. https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102
- Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine and Sleep* — effects last 3–7 hours, up to ~24 hours to fully eliminate. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-and-sleep

I've softened this from "suppresses" because the chain has two links and only the first is

## Ch18.32 — the "528 Hz" cue · accept as-is + endnote

No prose change — the line already hedges it ("Some claim the frequency itself aids sleep…").
The endnote just puts the one real study on the table and is honest about how little it proves.

To be straight about the 528 Hz part: there's one small study people point to. A 2018 crossover
experiment found that listening to music tuned to 528 Hz lowered salivary cortisol and raised
oxytocin compared with standard 440 Hz tuning. It's suggestive, but it's weak evidence — nine
participants, a single listening session, published in a minor journal, and as far as I can tell
never replicated. Whether 528 Hz does anything beyond what any calming audio would do is still an
open question. None of which matters for how I use it: the point isn't the frequency, it's that
I've turned the same neutral track into a personal signal for calm.

Sources:

- Akimoto K et al. 2018, "Effect of 528 Hz Music on the Endocrine System and Autonomic Nervous System," *Health* 10:1159–1170 (SCIRP) — crossover, n=9; 528 Hz ↓ salivary cortisol and ↑ oxytocin vs 440 Hz. *Credibility caveat: very small sample, single session, lower-tier journal, unreplicated — treat as preliminary, not established.* https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperabs?paperid=87146

## Ch22.7 / 22.8 — elevated cortisol → a bladder signal that wakes you · accept as-is + endnote

No prose change — it reads as a mechanism I'm proposing, which is what it is. Marker ⟦EN:Ch22.7-8⟧.

Two halves to this, and they're not equally proven. The part where deeper sleep means you hold
more through the night is well established: overnight your body releases more of the hormone that
concentrates urine, and lighter, more broken sleep simply makes you aware of a bladder signal you'd
otherwise sleep straight through. The extra step — that elevated cortisol specifically is what
turns that signal up — is my own joining of the dots from cortisol's role in light, fragmented
sleep, not something the research has demonstrated directly. It fits, and it matched what happened
as my sleep deepened, but treat it as my mechanism rather than settled fact.

Sources:

- George CP, Messerli FH, Genest J, Nowaczynski W, Boucher R, Kuchel O, Rojo-Ortega JM 1975, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 41(2):332–338 — classic demonstration of the overnight vasopressin rise: plasma vasopressin was significantly higher at night than by day, peaking around midnight–4am, and "a rise in [plasma vasopressin] explains, to some extent, the known nocturnal decrease in urine output." *Establishes the well-grounded half: overnight vasopressin concentrates urine so a deep sleeper holds more through the night.* https://doi.org/10.1210/jcem-41-2-332
- Andersson KE, Van Kerrebroeck P 2018, *Current Urology Reports* 19(1):8 — review of nocturia/nocturnal polyuria; impaired circadian release of arginine vasopressin (which reduces nighttime urine production by promoting water re-absorption) is central to nocturnal polyuria, and lighter/fragmented sleep lowers the arousal threshold so bladder filling is noticed. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11934-018-0750-y
- *Caveat:* the specific cortisol→bladder-signal step is the author's own inference, not directly demonstrated — these sources support only the vasopressin + sleep-depth/arousal physiology around it.

## Ch23.13a — protein "may help steady blood sugar overnight" · edit + endnote

Attaches at the softened sentence in Ch23 (marker ⟦EN:Ch23.13a⟧): "Protein may help steady blood
sugar overnight."

I've softened this to "may help" because the evidence is genuinely split. For someone managing a
glucose problem, a protein snack to steady overnight blood sugar is sound, standard advice — which
is why I still nudge that way in the next line. But in healthy people, controlled trials are mixed
on whether the *type* of bedtime snack changes overnight glucose much at all. And it's worth being
honest that the major sleep-nutrition guides don't pick protein over carbs: they recommend a small
snack with *both*, and they actually credit complex carbohydrates — not protein — with keeping
blood sugar steady, while pairing protein with the tryptophan effect. So "both have a case" is the
accurate position; the protein-for-blood-sugar point is a lean, not a settled winner.

Sources:

- Sleep Foundation, *Healthy Bedtime Snacks to Eat Before Sleep* — recommends a snack combining a carbohydrate and protein; complex carbs help keep blood sugar stable, protein supports tryptophan/serotonin. https://www.sleepfoundation.org/nutrition/healthy-bedtime-snacks
- Northwestern Medicine, *Eats to Help You Sleep* — carbohydrate + protein combination; carbohydrates help tryptophan cross into the brain to raise serotonin. https://www.nm.org/healthbeat/healthy-tips/nutrition/eats-to-help-you-sleep
- Riley TM, Kris-Etherton PM, Hart TL, Petersen KS 2024, *J Nutr* 154(4):1219–1231 — 12-wk randomized crossover trial in adults with prediabetes: a protein/fat-rich nighttime pistachio snack had "similar effects" on glycemic markers (no difference in fasting glucose, HbA1c, insulin, HOMA-IR) versus a carbohydrate-exchange snack. *Primary support for the "type isn't a settled winner" position, even in a glucose-vulnerable group. Caveat: part-funded by the American Pistachio Growers, though the finding is a null (no advantage to either snack).* https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tjnut.2024.01.021

## Ch24.11 + Ch24.16 / 24.17 — the sugar → cortisol → 2–3am waking chain · accept as-is + endnote (one shared)

No prose change — ¶40 already hedges this ("for a lot of people," "one more thing that can light that
fuse," "It's easy to assume you woke to use the bathroom"). One endnote covers the whole chain. Marker
⟦EN:Ch24.11-16-17⟧ at the end of ¶40.

Every step in this chain is on solid ground on its own: a big sugar load can be followed by a rebound dip
in blood glucose (reactive hypoglycaemia is a real, named phenomenon); the brain runs on glucose and the
body answers a low with cortisol; and cortisol running higher than it should in the small hours is a known
way to surface out of deep sleep. What I'm doing is joining those established parts into a single story —
a late sugar hit waking you at two or three in the morning. That's a reasonable mechanism rather than a
directly proven cause: symptomatic rebound lows from ordinary evening sweets aren't well demonstrated in
otherwise-healthy people, and no study has traced the full dessert-to-2am-waking sequence end to end.
Treat it as a plausible explanation worth testing against your own tracker, not a settled fact.

Sources:

- Stuart K, Field A, Raju J, Ramachandran S 2013, *Case Rep Med* 2013:273957 — reactive (postprandial) hypoglycaemia as a recognised phenomenon. https://doi.org/10.1155/2013/273957
- Sweatt SK, Thomas DM, LaPorte GJ, Chauff S, Stefanovski D, Gower BA 2026, *Nutrients* 18(5) — "Defining and Characterizing Postprandial Reactive Hypoglycemia"; corroborates the rebound-low mechanism. https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12986748
- Verberne AJ, Sabetghadam A, Korim WS 2014, *Front Neurosci* 8:38 — "Neural pathways that control the glucose counterregulatory response"; review confirming the counter-regulation step: declining plasma glucose triggers secretion of glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol and growth hormone to recover from a low (the "body answers a low with cortisol" link). https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2014.00038

## Ch24.20 — food sensitivities "generating what may be a low-grade immune response" · edit + endnote (summary plus sources)

Attaches at the hedged sentence in Ch24 (marker ⟦EN:Ch24.20⟧).

I've written "what may be" deliberately, because the mechanism here is part-evidenced and part-contested.
On the supportive side: reactions to food that aren't classic allergy are real — there's a recognised
category of non-IgE immune-mediated food disorders, and there's good evidence that diet can drive
low-grade, body-wide inflammation over time. On the doubtful side: the specific idea that an ordinary
person's food "sensitivity" keeps producing a background immune reaction that you'd feel as tiredness or
poor sleep rests largely on food-specific IgG — which is also just a normal marker of having eaten
something, and which has no validated test behind it. That's the same reason I'm careful, a few lines
later, to call the reactivity blood tests contested. So treat this as a plausible mechanism I'm pointing
at, not a settled one — and note it isn't the same thing as a diagnosable allergy.

Sources:

- Myszkowska D, Zapała B, Bulanda M, Czarnobilska E 2021, *Medicina (Kaunas)* 57(11):1245 — "Non-IgE Mediated Hypersensitivity to Food Products or Food Intolerance—Problems of Appropriate Diagnostics"; non-IgE food reactions are real, but IgG4/cytotoxic tests are "not clinically relevant" and should not drive elimination diets (no validated test for "sensitivity"). https://doi.org/10.3390/medicina57111245
- Minihane A-M, Vinoy S, … Calder PC 2015, *Br J Nutr* 114(7):999–1012 — "Low-grade inflammation, diet composition and health: current research evidence and its translation" (diet drives chronic low-grade inflammation; mechanism is dietary-pattern-level, not individual-sensitivity-level). https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114515002093
