# Phase 6 – optimise your inputs
# Chapter 22 - Habit 10: Hydration & waking up to pee

Hydration is the first chapter in Phase 6 — and probably the one that feels least like a sleep habit.

I knew I should be drinking a couple of litres a day, but I didn't really know why. And when life got busy — three kids, full days, a brain already running on fumes — it just didn't register on the priority list. I'd remember at three in the afternoon that I'd barely had anything and try to catch up. It still doesn't feel dramatically different during the day when I get it right. But it shows up on the tracker. A few more minutes of deep sleep. Slightly higher recovery scores. Small numbers. But they kept showing up.

The pattern was working against me in ways I hadn't connected to sleep at all.

Even mild dehydration — the kind you don't consciously notice — is enough for the body to register as a low-level stress state. Cortisol rises in response. Not dramatically, not in a way that feels like anything in particular, but enough to raise the baseline at a time when it should be falling. The Wake Executive gets a little more motivation to work harder than he needs to today.

The sleep system doesn't fail in one go — it gets quietly undermined by small things stacking on top of each other. Dehydration is one of the smaller things and one of the easiest to fix.

Hydration, in this sense, is not about optimising. It's about removing an unnecessary stress signal.

It works in another direction too. Dehydration also makes it harder for the body to cool down — and cooling down is part of what triggers sleep onset. When you're mildly dehydrated, that process slows. Sleep still comes — just slightly harder to reach when you want it.

There's a third piece, specific to the middle-of-the-night bathroom trips. It's not always about how much you drank. When cortisol is elevated and sleep is light, the bladder signal that most people sleep through becomes loud enough to wake you. The body is designed to hold more at night — but that system relies on cortisol being low and sleep being deep. Lose either one, and a signal that was always there breaks through.

The amount still matters — which is why you taper in the evening. But if you're doing that and still waking up, the problem might not be your bladder. It might be your sleep depth.

As my sleep improved, I noticed the night-time bathroom trips becoming less frequent. I'm down to once a night now. It doesn't bother me anymore. It's a quick trip in the dark, back to bed, and straight back to sleep. Just like a normal person. Another indication that  something significant had changed.

The rules that got me there don't require much.

Drink most of your fluids through the morning and early afternoon. Don't rely entirely on thirst as your guide. Its slightly delayed, so by the time you feel it, you're already behind. I made it as easy as possible for myself. I bought a two-litre bottle, filled it every morning, and made sure I'd got through it before the evening. No tracking, no measuring — just one visible target that reset each day. For most adults, around two litres is usually a reasonable daily amount — a little more if you're active or it's hot — but the bottle gave me the number without having to think about it.

Start tapering in the early evening. Small sips after dinner are fine. Large volumes are what cause the problem. If you want a rough signal for whether you're getting it right, urine colour is the simplest one. Pale straw means the body has what it needs. Dark yellow means you're behind. Clear means you've overdone it.

The one thing the system can't absorb is a late catch-up. As a rule, you don't want to be taking in large amounts in the last three hours or so before bed — roughly from dinner onward. If you've drunk barely anything by mid-afternoon and then try to hit your target in the hours before bed, you've solved one problem by creating another: a large volume close to bedtime is a reliable way to wake up several times in the night.

If you do get to late afternoon well behind, the honest answer is there's no clean fix — distributing through the day was the move, and you've missed it. But the least-bad version isn't to slam two or three litres at dinner. Bring your intake up before 5pm (or five hours before bed), then taper off toward 7pm (or 3 hours before bed), in smaller amounts. And if it's already late evening, don't try to catch up at all — small sips if you're thirsty, otherwise accept the shortfall and reset in the morning. A slight deficit tonight costs less than a disrupted night trying to correct it.

That's all there is to it. Hydration quietly helps cortisol stay a little lower, and the body cool down more easily. The system gets a clear run at night without something working against it. It's not dramatic, and it won't fix poor sleep on its own. But it removes a source of friction that most people don't even know is there — and removing friction is the whole point of Phase 6.

Fill the bottle in the morning. Start tomorrow.

No hacks. No complexity. Just giving your body what it already expects.
