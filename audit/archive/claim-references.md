## Ch05 — Adenosine

### Ch05.5 — Deep sleep clears adenosine overnight — [HIGH] · Group A-adenosine
- **Expanded:** Deep (slow-wave) sleep clears adenosine; after a proper night, levels are near zero by morning.
- **Book:** "During sleep, particularly deep (slow wave) sleep, the brain clears adenosine." / "By morning… levels are back to near zero."
- **Tier:** T3
- **Citation:** Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP (2022), *J Sleep Res* 31:e13597 (review). DOI 10.1111/jsr.13597; corrob. NINDS *Brain Basics*.
- **Quote:** "Today, adenosine is widely accepted as an endogenous sleep-regulatory substance." (NINDS: "Release of a chemical called adenosine from cells helps make you feel sleepy.")
- **Number check:** "near zero by morning" is qualitative. The accumulate-during-waking / dissipate-during-sleep homeostatic model is well established; the precise overnight-clearance dynamics (and the slow-wave-specific clearing) are the standard description rather than figures stated in these abstracts.
- **Evidence:** supports (homeostatic model).

### Ch05.12 — Alcohol sedates while suppressing slow-wave sleep — [HIGH] · Group F-alcohol (canonical: merged with Ch14.8)
- **Expanded:** Alcohol sedates rather than inducing natural sleep, and (book's claim) suppresses slow-wave/deep sleep.
- **Book:** "Alcohol feels like a sleep aid, but it sedates you while suppressing slow-wave sleep."
- **Tier:** T3 (+T2)
- **Citation:** Gardiner C et al. (2024), *Sleep Med Rev* 80:102030, DOI 10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030; Ebrahim IO et al. (2013), *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549, DOI 10.1111/acer.12006; Sleep Health Foundation (AU). https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep
- **Quote:** Gardiner: "Reductions in sleep onset latency and latency to deep sleep (i.e., non-rapid eye movement stage three (N3)) were only observed following the consumption of a high dose of alcohol (≥0.85 g·kg⁻¹ or approximately five standard drinks)." Ebrahim: "The majority of studies, across dose, age and gender, confirm an increase in slow wave sleep (SWS) in the first half of the night relative to baseline values."
- **Number check:** NA.
- **Evidence:** **CONTRADICTS the slow-wave/deep-sleep part.** The two best reviews agree alcohol does **not** suppress slow-wave/deep sleep: Ebrahim finds it *increases* early-night SWS; Gardiner finds only a *reduced latency to* N3 (reaching deep sleep faster) at high doses, with no robust suppression. Sedation ("sedates rather than induces") is fine; the *robust* harm is **REM** (delayed/reduced), not deep sleep.
- **Flag (important):** reword. Alcohol's signature is **REM suppression + second-half fragmentation**, not slow-wave suppression. This also undercuts the Ch05 "alcohol stops adenosine clearing via suppressed deep sleep" mechanism — the adenosine-clearance argument needs rethinking.
- **Searched:** Gardiner 2024 (Sleep Med Rev); Ebrahim 2013; SHF (AU).

## Ch07 — Cortisol

### Ch07.7 — Cortisol awakening response precedes waking — [HIGH] · Group E-cortisol
- **Expanded:** Cortisol begins rising before waking — the "cortisol awakening response."
- **Book:** "Before you open your eyes, it's already rising — called the cortisol awakening response."
- **Tier:** T3
- **Citation:** Fries E, Dettenborn L, Kirschbaum C (2009), *Int J Psychophysiol* 72:67–73. DOI 10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2008.03.014
- **Quote:** "the secretion of cortisol from the adrenal glands follows a diurnal cycle with a profound increase after awakening. This increase after awakening, a phenomenon termed the cortisol awakening response (CAR), … superimposing the circadian rhythmicity of cortisol secretion."
- **Number check:** NA.
- **Evidence:** partial — **mislabel flag.** The CAR is the surge *after* waking (peaks ~30–45 min later), superimposed on the overnight circadian rise. Suggest: cortisol rises through the late night (circadian), then surges further after waking (the CAR).

### Ch07.8 — Peaks first hour, declines through day — [HIGH] · Group E-cortisol
- **Expanded:** Cortisol peaks in the first hour after waking, then declines across the day.
- **Book:** "It peaks in the first hour or so after waking, then begins its long downward curve through the day."
- **Tier:** T3
- **Citation:** Fries 2009 (above).
- **Quote:** "…a profound increase after awakening… superimposing the circadian rhythmicity of cortisol secretion." (post-waking peak then diurnal decline is the standard description.)
- **Number check:** NA.
- **Evidence:** supports.

### Ch07.17 — Melatonin eases ~2am; cortisol fills gap — [HIGH] · Group E-cortisol
- **Expanded:** Melatonin holds high early then eases in the latter half of the night (~2am for a 10pm bedtime ≈ ~4 h after onset / before wake); if cortisol is riding high it fills the gap and wakes you.
- **Book:** "Melatonin holds high through the first stretch of the night, then from around 2 am it begins to ease… cortisol jumps right in to fill the gap."
- **Tier:** T3
- **Citation:** Chan S, Debono M (2010), *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138 (review). DOI 10.1177/2042018810380214
- **Quote:** "Cortisol has one of the most distinct and fascinating circadian rhythms in human physiology. This is regulated by the central clock located in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus."
- **Number check:** the established pattern (nadir ~midnight; rise begins ~02:00–03:00; peak ~08:30) is the standard cortisol rhythm widely reported in this literature; per your steer, the book's "~2am" — for a 10pm bedtime — maps to the early-morning rise / latter half of sleep. (The retrieved abstract states the rhythm generally; the exact hours are consensus physiology, detailed in the article's profiling data.)
- **Evidence:** supports (direction/timing). Pairs with Leproult 1997 ("the onset of the quiescent period of cortisol secretion") and Fries 2009 (post-waking surge).

### Ch07.18 — Late caffeine is a cortisol stimulus — [HIGH] · Group B-caffeine

- **Book:** "Every cup taken too late is a mild cortisol stimulus…"

- **Quote:** "Caffeine increases cortisol secretion in people at rest or undergoing mental stress… caffeine challenge doses caused a robust increase in cortisol across the test day (p < .0001)… cortisol levels were again elevated between 1:00 PM and 7:00 PM (p = .02 to .002) after the second caffeine dose taken at 1:00 PM."
- **Number check:** NA.
- **Evidence:** supports. Note: tolerance reduces but doesn't eliminate the response in daily users.

### Ch07.20 — Sleep deprivation is stress, raises cortisol — [HIGH] · Group E-cortisol

## Claims the author needs to see (contradicts / mixed / no-source / number-off)

1. **Ch05.12 / Ch14.8 — alcohol & deep sleep — CONTRADICTS.** The two best reviews agree alcohol does **not** suppress deep/slow-wave sleep: Gardiner 2024 (AU meta-analysis, 27 PSG studies) finds the robust effect is **REM** (delayed + reduced, dose-dependent from ~2 drinks) and only a *reduced latency* to deep sleep at ~5+ drinks; Ebrahim 2013 finds early-night SWS *increases*. Reword to "suppresses REM + fragments the second half of the night," and revisit the Ch05 adenosine-clearance mechanism.
2. **Ch14.9 / Ch14.10 — alcohol −15 / −40 min — STRUCTURE now sourced (Gardiner 2024); exact minutes unconfirmed.** The dose-response ("even ~1–2 drinks cut restorative/REM sleep; worse with more") is well-supported. The specific 15-min and 40-min figures aren't in Gardiner's abstract — check the paper's full-text pooled effect sizes (in minutes), or soften the numbers.
3. **Ch20.18 — finish vigorous exercise 4 h before bed — MIXED/CONTRADICTS.** Evidence (Stutz 2019) supports ~1 h for most; 4 h looks overstated (book's "healthy-sleeper" caveat is fair).
4. **Ch13.6 — caffeine "6-hour half-life" — NUMBER HIGH.** EFSA (2015) and a 2026 clinical PK study both put it at **~4 h**; literature ~4–5 h. "6 hours" overstates it — use "about four to five hours" (the halving illustration still works directionally).
5. **Ch06.9 — melatonin peak "midnight or one" — minor.** Peak timing is highly variable (~midnight–3am, some later); "midnight or one" is at the early edge but defensible. Low priority.
6. **Ch12.11 — core temp drop "1–2 degrees" — UNIT/MAGNITUDE.** Usual figure ~0.5–1 °C (≈1–2 °F); "1–2 °C" likely double — clarify unit.
7. **Ch07.7 — CAR "before you open your eyes" — MISLABEL.** The CAR is the *post-waking* surge.
8. **Ch10.15 — REM "where memory consolidation happens" — OVERSIMPLIFIED.** Memory needs both non-REM and REM.
9. **Magnesium L-threonate (Ch11.10) — OVERSTATED (now confirmed, T5).** "Better evidence base for sleep" isn't supported; absorption edge is animal-data; general Mg-sleep evidence is low quality. Soften.
10. **Phosphatidylserine dose (Ch11.7) — caution (confirmed).** Cortisol-blunting trial used 800 mg (brain-cortex PS) under exercise stress; book's 300–500 mg soy/sunflower "before bed" is lower and a different indication.
11. **Dehydration → cortisol (Ch22.4) — supported with caveat.** Robust effect is for exercise/heat dehydration; "mild everyday dehydration" is a softer extension.
