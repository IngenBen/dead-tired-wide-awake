# Chapter 2 - The Turning Point

I started with my doctor. He gave me something to help me sleep, but made it clear he didn't want to prescribe it again. What I really needed, he said, was to get to the root of it — and for that I'd have to see a psychologist, or someone like one. He didn't think a sleep study was worth it either. It didn't sound like apnea, and that's mostly what it would be looking for.

He was right on every count. It was also where the trail went cold.

A GP is a generalist, and this wasn't a generalist's kind of problem. The tools he had were a prescription he'd rather not repeat and a referral onward — and that kind of help is hard to reach, with few practitioners and long waits.

I kept looking.

Books, podcasts, YouTube, forums. The occasional deep dive into whatever study had just made the rounds online. There was always something new — some YouTuber who had found ‘the thing’, the mechanism, the explanation that was going to finally make sense of it all.

I would get genuinely excited. This one feels different. This one might actually be it.

It never was.

Some of the problem was volume. The good books didn't give you one thing to try, they gave you twenty. Sometimes more. Each with variations, caveats, individual differences to account for. For someone already exhausted and overwhelmed, the prospect of working through all of it was paralysing. So I'd pick one or two, and try for a couple of weeks. They wouldn't work — or I couldn't tell if they were working. So I’d stop them and move on to the next thing, wondering if I was ever going to find a fix.

The right information was there. I just didn't have the framework to make sense of it.

The moment things started to click, I was driving.

That year, our whole family was into mountain biking — proper downhill, chairlift, helmet-and-pads stuff. The trails we loved were about six hours away, which meant long car trips, several times over. On one of those drives, kids occupied, kilometres rolling past, I was listening to audiobooks about sleep and the nervous system.

I'd been turning something over in my mind for a while. I'd had days on the mountain that were the most physically intense of my life — hours of full adrenaline, every sense switched on, pushing harder than I ever had in a gym. And yet I wasn't sleeping better on those nights. In fact, I consistently slept worse. That just didn’t make sense.

But somewhere on that drive, it started to.

Cortisol, adrenaline, the nervous system, stress and how the body reacts to it — things I'd read about in isolation started connecting. I started to see a pattern.

I already knew that a stressful day at work led to a bad night’s sleep. But if daytime stress causes a nighttime problem, what about everything else that I did during the day?

Without realising it, I had been thinking about sleep entirely as a night-time problem — something with a magic switch - all I had to do was find it. But the night was never the problem. It was just where I noticed it.

I was beginning to see how much more the daytime mattered.

It reframed everything. Every tip I'd tried, every supplement, every bedtime routine — none of it was wrong exactly. Some of it was even well-supported by science.

Some people can try ‘one thing’ and swear by it. Most likely, they had a solid foundation. The tip worked because most of the system was already working. For me, the whole system was out of alignment - I spent all day doing the wrong things, so trying their ‘one thing’ was never going to work.

And if the day was where the problem existed, thats where the fix had to be.

That was when it became obvious. I needed to build a ‘good day’. The problem was, I didn’t know what a good day looked like. And I couldn’t find anything that showed me.

I didn’t need another book of tips or a rigid sleep protocol. I had a demanding job, a family, a commute, and the kind of tiredness that makes even simple things a chore. I needed something that held my hand, which worked within a modern busy schedule.

It had to be holistic. It had to be incremental - my willpower was running on empty. It had to be flexible enough that one bad week didn't unravel the whole thing.

Since a program like this was nowhere I could find, I decided I had to build it.

The starting point was biology.

If I was going to build something that actually worked, I needed to understand the system I was trying to support. Not at a research level — I wasn't looking to make new discoveries. Just to make sense of what had already been found and figure out what I could realistically do about it.

It began with re-reading and self-experimenting. Trying things, measuring the response, adjusting. It wasn’t a clean clinical trial. I couldn’t always tell what was working and what wasn’t. I paid close attention to how I felt during the day — energy levels, mood, the quality of my mornings. My WHOOP was tracking through all of it, too — though how that came about is a story for later. Because there were so many variables, it was never quite a process of: do this, get that.

The whole process took years. Years of not knowing what I was looking for, trying the wrong things, measuring (and guessing), discarding and starting again. I was building the program from scratch.

After all those years, I'd found what worked — and more importantly, what I could realistically sustain.

Fortunately, you don’t have to go through all of that. The program now exists.

And, here's what I believe: if you follow the program as it's laid out in this book, it will change your life — and far faster than the years it took me. I'll give you a sense of what to expect, and roughly when, before you begin.

All the figuring out is done. You just have to put in the effort to follow along.

If you are still hoping for 'one thing I can do for a good night's sleep', a program might sound like a lot. But compared to months, years or decades of searching without any permanent success, it's nothing.

You won't have to wait until the end to know it's working. Somewhere partway through the program, you'll see a noticeable improvement in your sleep, and you'll feel something profound: an undeniable signal that breaks a story you've been telling yourself for a long time. The one that goes: I will never be a good sleeper. 

Once that story breaks, the motivation to finish the program takes care of itself. 

My version of that took longer — I was doing and testing the program at the same time. But it came. And eventually, the insomnia was gone.

And sleep was just the beginning. 

Things I'd carried for years began to lift.

The anxiety went. Not dramatically, not overnight — but one day that background weight was lighter. The minor bouts of depression, gone. The irritability — this-shouldn’t-annoy-me-but-it-does — much less. My ability to handle stress improved - I hadn't realised how much of my stress response was caused by exhaustion.

The recurring dreams stopped too. No therapy, no reliving childhood trauma, no working out what any of it meant — I just started sleeping properly, and one day I realised they were gone.

Energy started coming back. And then something I hadn't felt in a very long time — excitement. Genuine anticipation about things I was going to do on the weekend. I hadn't experienced that emotion in more than a decade.

But the one that meant the most was even simpler.

I started showing up. For my wife, for my kids, for the people around me. Not just physically present — actually there. The gap I'd been managing for years - between who I was and who I wanted to be - had closed. Those ordinary moments that should have made me happy finally did.

I still have bad weeks. I'm not a perfect sleeper. Some weeks the habits slip and I feel it. But I now understand why, and I know what to do about it.

I've gone from years of broken, poor-quality sleep to 7+ hours of good quality sleep most nights.

It has been absolutely life changing.

I'm not sharing this because my story is unique. The specifics are mine — the mountain biking, the trial and error, the shape of my sleep — but the pattern will be familiar. The searching, the failed fixes, the exhaustion that makes everyday things feel like way too much. If any of that resonated, you're in the right place.

What I hope you take from this chapter isn't the story. It's the possibility.

Maybe you don't have to accept: I'm just a bad sleeper. Maybe this isn’t the rest of your life.

The moment where you stop hoping it might be true and actually feel it — that's closer than it probably feels right now.

And the biology is where it starts. I'm going to explain it in a way that you might not have seen before. The simplicity might make neuroscientists cringe, but we're not neuroscientists. We just need to understand sleep well enough to get a meaningful improvement.

Take in as much as you can, it's worth it.
