**Framing rule (agreed 2026-09-24):** the thesis is that sleep is *like* fitness: healthy daily habits, done consistently over time. The goal is to be **sleep fit**, not physically fit. Some habits overlap with physical fitness (morning walk, exercise), but wording must never read as 'exercise more'. ## Reference definitions (agreed 2026-09-24)

**Sleep fit (canonical, audit reference, not book prose):** Physical fitness mostly builds your capacity to go up: to work harder and go longer. Sleep fitness builds your capacity to come back down. Both need activation and calm. What differs is the half you're training. A sleep-fit system spikes when the day needs it, returns to calm after each spike, and lets calm win at night, on time. It's built the same way as any fitness: small daily habits, done consistently, over weeks.

**Activated ≠ active (agreed line, to use the first time Activating/Calming is tied to fitness):**

> Activated isn't the same as physically active. You can be lying perfectly still and highly activated: scrolling in bed, or awake at 2am with your mind racing.

## ch04 - Sleep Incorporated

> *[L13]* The science on light and sleep is straightforward. When light enters the bedroom during the night, your body reads it as a signal — almost morning, time to start waking up. It doesn't take much. Even low-level light can put your system into a state of mild alertness that runs quietly in the background all night, reducing the quality of sleep without you ever fully waking. You don't need to read deeply into the research to act on this one. Less light is better. It's worth stopping what you can.


### 13.2 · Groups D · L45–51
**Trigger:** “I fixed my insomnia without it”  
**Note:** The strongest 'fix' claim in the book. Under the fitness thesis this would be 'built/rebuilt my sleep'.

> *[L45]* So the practical target is simple: get your bedroom to roughly 29 degrees or below — which most rooms manage most of the year. From there, you tune the bed climate with the two things you can actually control: your bedding and what you wear.
>
> *[L47]* If you're lucky enough to have air conditioning in the bedroom, this might be easier to manage. Same bedding each night, same thermostat temperature.
>
> ▶ *[L49]* If you don't have air conditioning, and I didn't, the good news is: I fixed my insomnia without it.
>
> *[L51]* Where I landed was several thin layers of cotton blankets rather than a single heavy duvet. In practice it means I can adjust easily depending on the room temperature each night. Push a layer off, pull one back on — until the temperature feels right.


### 13.3 · Groups B · L71–75
**Trigger:** “your brain learns by association”  
**Note:** Conditioning, one meaning of training. Links to Ch07 L47 'learns by repetition'.

> *[L71]* Firstly, it introduces stimulation — news, drama, interesting series, content designed to hold your attention at the moment your nervous system should be winding down.
>
> ▶ *[L73]* Secondly, your brain learns by association. TV teaches it that the bed is also for being awake and entertained, not just sleeping. Note that the light output isn't really the main concern here — it's the stimulation and the association that are doing the damage.
>
> *[L75]* If you've got a TV in the bedroom, take it out. The bed is for sleep; remove anything that tells your brain otherwise. There's a fuller version of this in Chapter 16, where it matters most — but for this chapter, the simple version is enough.

## ch14 - Habit 1 - Caffeine

### 14.1 · Groups C · L4–10
**Trigger:** “the single habit most likely to be disrupting their sleep”  
**Note:** 'Single habit' here means the biggest single disruptor, not a cure, but the wording is close to 'one thing'.

> *[L4]* I was not a low-intake, well-timed coffee drinker. I was seven cups a day, last one at a bit after 3pm, and completely convinced it wasn't affecting my sleep.
>
> ▶ *[L6]* Phase 1 was about getting set up — a tracker running, a supplement routine bedded in, a bedroom that works for sleep rather than against it. None of that required you to give anything up. Phase 2 does. And caffeine is first, not because it's the easiest, but because for a significant number of people it is the single habit most likely to be disrupting their sleep. It's also the clearest activating input there is — which makes cutting it back, or simply moving it earlier, your first real move toward calm.
>
> *[L8]* Throughout this chapter, treat energy drinks the same as coffee — the same caffeine, the same half-life, the same rules apply.
>
> *[L10]* If coffee is not really your thing – either you don't drink it, or one cup in the morning and you're done — this chapter is still worth reading. The second half talks about how to actually change a habit. The mechanics — what worked when everything else didn't, and why. And if you're anything like I was, you'll assume cutting back has to be miserable. It doesn't.


### 14.2 · Groups B · L28–34
**Trigger:** “train the body to rely on the cup rather than its own waking response” / “Twenty years of interfering… takes time to surface… a slow burn”  
**Note:** Training language (dependency trained in). 'Slow burn' is a gradual-adaptation idea.

> *[L28]* That's not how it works. Every five hours or so, the amount in your system halves. Two double-shot espressos before 10am sounds reasonable. Halve that: it's the equivalent of a large coffee at 3pm. Halve it again: a single shot espresso at 8pm — still circulating well into the evening, still blocking the signal your brain needs to sleep.
>
> *[L30]* I'm still too scared to do the maths on my seven-cup days.
>
> ▶ *[L32]* There's also the morning. In the first sixty to ninety minutes after waking, two things are completing: cortisol is finishing the surges that wakes you and adenosine finishes its overnight clearance. Drinking coffee in this window competes with both. Over time, it appears to train the body to rely on the cup rather than its own waking response. Both point to the same recommendation: delay the first coffee by sixty to ninety minutes. Let the body do its own work first.
>
> ▶ *[L34]* This is the one I'm still working on. I spent years reaching for the cup the second I woke, and a habit that old doesn't let go quickly. Everything I've read backs it, and that's part of what keeps me at it. I can't point to a sign on my tracker yet — it's still early. ### 14.3 · Groups B,C · L38–46

> *[L38]* Once I understood what caffeine was actually doing, I knew something had to change. The maths was not in my favour.
>
> ▶ *[L40]* The obvious move was to quit. And I tried. Every time I discovered something I thought would help my sleep I'd draw a line and commit to it. Cold turkey. I did it with real conviction, more than once. But it never lasted. A few good weeks, and then life got busy, and I was back to my old habits before I knew it.
>
> ▶ *[L42]* It wasn't a willpower problem — or at least, not in the way I'd always assumed. Sleep deprivation measurably reduces your capacity for self-control. That's not a character flaw. That's biology. The problem was that cold turkey required willpower from a body too depleted to generate it. I was asking the least-resourced version of myself to make the hardest possible choices. It was never going to work.
>
> *[L44]* So I tried something different.
>
> ▶ *[L46]* Instead of cutting coffee out, I moved my last coffee of the day five to ten minutes earlier. That was it. And once that felt normal, I moved it another five to ten minutes earlier. Then every few days I stopped to give myself credit – *wow, I was now 30 minutes earlier than when I started.*


### 14.4 · Groups B,C · L48–60
**Trigger:** “if life got busy, I would pause. Holding where I was” / “cravings just gone” / “exactly what sustainable change looks like… The slow version is the only thing that stuck”  
**Note:** **Progressive build, then hold (a deload).** Fitness training logic in all but name, and the book's clearest 'how to change a habit' method.

> ▶ *[L48]* It took weeks. And if work got hard, or life got busy, I would pause. Holding where I was. I'd got there slowly, so the new time wasn't hard to hold. When things calmed down, I'd continue.
>
> *[L50]* As my late-afternoon coffee got closer to my mid-afternoon coffee, I no longer felt like I needed it. Dropping it entirely pushed my last coffee of the day forward by close to an hour in one step. It was a little harder – but not for long. I paused until it got easy.
>
> ▶ *[L52]* This was a huge win for motivation. The cup that had felt non-negotiable for a decade — I simply stopped wanting it. No hard work resisting cravings. Instead cravings just gone.
>
> *[L54]* The same pattern repeated as I moved everything earlier.
>
> *[L56]* The psychological link between waking up and walking straight to the machine was the last thing to go, and the hardest — harder than any of the afternoon coffees. But the method was the same.
>
> ▶ *[L58]* At the time of writing I'm at two cups, both before ten in the morning. I got there slowly, unglamorously, and without a single moment that felt like a breakthrough. Which is, I've come to think, exactly what sustainable change looks like. Cold turkey only ever gave me two good weeks and a story about trying. The slow version is the only thing that stuck.
>
> *[L60]* Cutting back on caffeine was the first change I made that moved the needle — noticeably. I just wish I'd been using a sleep tracker from the start. I would have loved some before and after data on that.

> *[L66]* If caffeine is a problem for you, the move is simple. Pick the last coffee you drink each day. And tomorrow, drink it five to ten minutes earlier than you did today.

> ▶ *[L68]* That's it. Not a new routine, not a different version of yourself, not cold turkey. One cup, moved slightly. When it feels normal, move again.

## ch15 - Habit 2 - Alcohol

### 15.1 · Groups B · L27–31
**Trigger:** “recovery the night never finished”  
**Note:** Recovery vocabulary.

> *[L27]* In the first part of the night, alcohol does something sneaky. It doesn't rob you of deep sleep — if anything you get more of it, pulled early, which is part of why the sleep feels so convincing. What it suppresses is REM, the dreaming sleep that handles memory and emotion. And it doesn't cost you evenly: even a drink or two measurably eats into that restorative sleep, and the more you drink, the more it takes.
>
> ▶ *[L29]* So you wake having ticked the deep-sleep box but missing the things that actually leave you feeling rested: unbroken sleep, a full share of REM, and a body that properly powered down overnight. That's a big part of the tiredness behind a hangover — not sleep you never got, but recovery the night never finished. Alcohol didn't create that tiredness; it just stopped the night from clearing it.
>
> *[L31]* There's a problem in the second part of the night too. After a heavier night of drinking, I would often crash out for the first three or four hours. Then wide awake. Three or four in the morning, heart going, no prospect of getting back to sleep.


### 15.2 · Groups B,C · L35–45
**Trigger:** “How much all this costs you depends on your reserve… Running on empty” / “not recommended for peak athletes” / “applied the same approach as caffeine”  
**Note:** Reserve and resilience are fitness ideas. L41 already reaches for athletes. L45 applies the progressive method again.

> *[L35]* If you do sleep through the metabolic rebound, you hit REM rebound.  The REM suppressed earlier springs back — often as more intense, vivid, sometimes disturbing dreams in the early hours. If you've ever woken from an unusually vivid dream after a night of drinking, that's what you were experiencing.
>
> ▶ *[L37]* How much all this costs you depends on your reserve. With no sleep debt, one disrupted night may not matter much. Running on empty, it's a substantial blow. It's why some people wake up fine after a few drinks while others — and I'm reliably in this camp — wake up feeling terrible.
>
> *[L39]* **What I actually do**
>
> ▶ *[L41]* Until I understood what alcohol was doing to my sleep, most weeks I would drink at least one beer every night to unwind from the day, and two or three on weekend nights. I knew it wasn't recommended for peak athletes. But I didn't think it was a problem for the average person wanting a decent night's sleep.
>
> *[L43]* The first thing that had to change was the frequency.
>
> ▶ *[L45]* A dry working week was the decision I landed on. Nothing on weeknights. This was hard at first. It was so engrained in my daily routine. So I applied the same approach as caffeine. I stopped one night a week to begin with. Then expanded on that. Until Sunday to Monday were clear.


### 15.3 · Groups C · L55–59
**Trigger:** “do something, not nothing” / “A small shift you hold onto is worth more than a big commitment you drop. The direction matters more than the distance.”  
**Note:** **One of the best existing thesis lines.** 'Direction matters more than distance' is fitness-native.

> *[L55]* If you're already drinking infrequently, just shrink the impact: earlier, with food, as little as the occasion allows — ideally two drinks max, finished with dinner.
>
> ▶ *[L57]* And if you're not ready to change anything yet — if the evening drink feels genuinely non-negotiable right now — I really recommend: do something, not nothing. Find the smallest possible move. One fewer drink. One night a week without.
>
> ▶ *[L59]* A small shift you hold onto is worth more than a big commitment you drop. The direction matters more than the distance.


## ch16 - Habit 3 - Can't sleep routine

### 16.1 · Groups B · L26–34
**Trigger:** “The brain learns by repetition… Every minute… adds a data point… teaches the brain”  
**Note:** Conditioning, the third instance (Ch07 L47, Ch13 L73).

> *[L26]* The first is hormonal. By now you know cortisol doesn't care what the stressor is — and lying awake annoyed at not sleeping is a stressor like any other. The more frustrated you get, the more cortisol rises, the more alert you stay, the less possible sleep becomes. The thinking doesn't stop; at 2am it finds the thing you're most worried about, which feeds the frustration, and round it goes. A downward loop.
>
> ▶ *[L28]* The second is about association. The brain learns by repetition. It builds connections between environments and expected states — between contexts and what the body should be doing in them. We discussed this briefly in the Chapter 7 - Cortisol. Your bedroom, ideally, is a place the brain associates strongly with sleep: you get in, the lights go off, the system begins to wind down.
>
> ▶ *[L30]* Every minute spent lying awake and anxious in bed adds a data point to a different association. Bed-equals-racing-mind, instead of bed-equals-sleep. Do that enough nights in a row and the bed itself becomes a cue for wakefulness — the brain activates the moment you get into it, because that's the pattern. This is why some people with chronic insomnia find they can fall asleep easily on the couch but the moment they move to bed their mind switches on.
>
> ▶ *[L32]* Every activity in bed — phone, TV, worrying — teaches the brain that bed is a place where those things happen. Sleep is the thing that gets crowded out.
>
> *[L34]* The goal, then, is not to force sleep back. It's to protect what the bed means — and that means getting out of it when sleep isn't happening.


### 16.2 · Groups B,C · L54–64
**Trigger:** “As the other habits in the program strengthened, that number came down on its own” / “over weeks it rewrites the association… hasn't had enough repetitions yet” / “one night at a time”  
**Note:** Habits working together (C) plus repetitions (B). Strongly on-thesis.

> *[L54]* For others it might be reading, music, bedtime stories, gentle stretching — the medium doesn't matter. (Television worked for me - see footnote about blue light and screens).
>
> ▶ *[L56]* In the early days of building this habit, I would watch for hours — four, sometimes five episodes before I felt drowsy enough to go back to bed. As the other habits in the program strengthened, that number came down on its own.
>
> *[L58]* I still use it — though these days I rarely need it. And when I do, I rarely make it through a single episode.
>
> ▶ *[L60]* The routine works on two timescales: tonight it breaks the spiral, and over weeks it rewrites the association — bed equals sleep, in the language the brain actually speaks. The first few nights may feel worse, not better; the new pattern hasn't had enough repetitions yet. That's expected.
>
> ▶ *[L62]* So the next time you're lying awake at 2am, you'll have something to do — and even when it doesn't get you straight back to sleep, it's moving you in the right direction, one night at a time.
>
> *[L64]* Phase 3 begins in the morning.


## ch17 - Habit 4 - Your Morning Routine

### 17.1 · Groups B · L1–6
**Trigger:** Phase heading: “Train your body clock”  
**Note:** The phase headings already use 'train' (Phase 3 here, Phase 4 at Ch19 L1).

> ▶ *[L1]* # Phase 3 – Train your body clock
>
> *[L2]* # Chapter 17 - Habit 4: Your Morning Routine
>
> *[L4]* For a long time, my solution to a bad night was to sleep-in as long as possible.
>
> *[L6]* It seemed obvious. I'd been awake for hours in the middle of the night — lying there, finally drifting back off sometime between 4 and 5am, alarm going off at 6. My job had flexibility on start time so I used it. I'd roll in late, make up the hours, it wasn't a problem. I thought I was being smart. Banking every hour of sleep I could get.


### 17.2 · Groups B,C · L8–18
**Trigger:** “It is not a bank… It is a timing system” / “Done consistently, your body knows what to expect”  
**Note:** Consistency over catch-up. You can't bank training either.

> *[L8]* I was completely unaware of the chaos I was causing for my Sleep and Wake Executives — adenosine and melatonin, cortisol and dopamine, the four we met earlier. The problem is what happens to your body clock when the start of your day keeps moving around.
>
> ▶ *[L10]* Your body is not keeping score of hours the way you might think it is. It is not a bank where you deposit what you missed and withdraw it later. It is a timing system. And the single most important input into that system is the time you wake up and get light into your eyes.
>
> *[L12]* When that time shifts, even by an hour or two, everything shifts with it. The cortisol awakening response, your body's natural alerting mechanism, fires later. The countdown to evening melatonin release starts later. By the time you want to sleep that night, your Sleep Executives haven't had enough time to build to the level they need. The window hasn't arrived yet. You're lying there, tired but wired, frustrated that you are losing all the extra sleep you gained that morning.
>
> *[L14]* The morning signal is the most powerful cue your body has to set its internal clock.
>
> ▶ *[L16]* Light entering your eyes in the first thirty to sixty minutes after waking tells your brain: it's daytime. The Wake Executives fire. The sleep countdown begins. Done consistently, your body knows what to expect. The melatonin that helps you sleep that night will be ready when you need it, because your system has been tracking the hours since morning light arrived.
>
> *[L18]* Without that signal — or with it arriving at a different time each day — the countdown starts from wherever your body happened to surface. And a system that can't predict when the day begins struggles to predict when sleep should begin.


### 17.3 · Groups B · L20–34
**Trigger:** “That's the habit worth building” / “As the sleep improved, the walks changed” / “not sure the sleep caused the walks to improve, or the walks helped the sleep”  
**Note:** **Watch for literal versus metaphorical fitness.** Here, and in Ch21, exercise is an actual habit. The fitness thesis must not read as 'exercise more'.

> *[L20]* The habit itself is simple. Get outside within thirty minutes of waking. Natural light, even on an overcast day, is dramatically brighter than indoor lighting — bright enough to trigger the response that indoor lamps, phone screens, and ceiling lights cannot replicate. Five to fifteen minutes in direct sunlight is usually enough. On an overcast day, aim for twenty minutes. The sky, even grey, delivers far more light than the inside of a house.
>
> ▶ *[L22]* Light alone will do the job. But movement alongside it helps too — exercise is its own gentle cue to the body clock. A morning walk gives you both at once. That's the habit worth building. It doesn't need to be long. It just needs to happen.
>
> *[L24]* The walk was typical of how insomnia played out for me. A daily habit I wanted to build — and, at the same time, one that fatigue had me really not wanting to do at all. That constant tension extended to so many activities in my life, and it was a punishment in itself. For quite a while I had to force myself out the door.
>
> *[L26]* It helped that my wife and our dog were already in the routine. But I joined them quiet, a bit grumpy, reluctant to be there, and not the most pleasant version of myself. I did the best I could to mask it. Some days I managed better than others.
>
> ▶ *[L28]* As the sleep improved, the walks changed. Slowly, reluctant became less reluctant. Less reluctant became keen. Keen became — I don't want to miss it. I started talking more. Noticing the light, the air, whatever was happening around me. At some point, the walk stopped being a thing I was forcing and started being a thing I wanted.
>
> *[L30]* Along the way, I made one simple addition. I stopped looking at the ground. Head up, shoulders back, eyes up and out — toward the horizon, the mountains, the trees, the clouds, rather than my feet or my phone. Taking in whatever nature was in front of me. It cost no extra time and almost no effort — I was already out there. And walking like that, I felt calmer, more confident, and more in control. For something that costs nothing, the return was surprisingly large.
>
> ▶ *[L32]* I'm not sure the sleep caused the walks to improve, or the walks helped the sleep improve, or both. But the grumpy, quiet version of me mostly disappeared.
>
> *[L34]* The movement doesn't have to be a walk — a run, a cycle, anything that gets you outside works just as well. If movement genuinely isn't possible, getting outside for fifteen minutes still delivers most of the signal. Sit and read, stand in the garden, whatever works. And if your schedule or situation keeps you inside entirely, a bright light therapy lamp at breakfast can be a workable substitute.


### 17.4 · Groups C,D · L38–44
**Trigger:** “I've broken this rule… spent the following week paying for it”  
**Note:** One lapse has a cost, so consistency matters. 'Broken' here is harmless (a broken rule).

> *[L38]* This is the rule I find hardest to follow. But the answer is always the same: wake time does not move. Get up. Even if only for a couple of hours. Let the Wake Executives turn up to work at the time they expect. Give the system its signal. Then, if you are absolutely desperate, the shortest nap you can manage. Twenty minutes at most. Not before mid-morning and not in the late afternoon. Just enough to take the edge off without pushing the evening window. Better still, turn to the napping chapter when you get there. There is another way to do it that doesn't cost you the following night.
>
> ▶ *[L40]* What you are trying to avoid is the full sleep-in. Rolling over and going back to sleep for a couple of hours sends an inconsistent signal to the executives. I've broken this rule — lain in on a Sunday after a rough Saturday, told myself it didn't matter, and spent the following week paying for it. I know what it costs. It's better to tough it out to ensure a quality sleep that evening, because the morning routine is not really about the morning.
>
> *[L42]* It is about the following evening.
>
> *[L44]* The signal you send at six or seven in the morning — consistent, bright light and at the same time each day — sets the timer for when the Sleep Executives need to be ready to take over. Everything that follows — the cortisol clearing, the adenosine building, the melatonin rising — runs from that fixed starting point.

**Trigger:** “You are setting a clock” / “When the clock is set well, the rhythm holds… sleep becomes something that arrives rather than something you chase”

> *[L44]* The signal you send at six or seven in the morning — consistent, bright light and at the same time each day — sets the timer for when the Sleep Executives need to be ready to take over. ## ch18 - Habit 5 - Your Evening Routine

### 18.1 · Groups C · L3–9
**Trigger:** “A couple of the right things, done consistently… That's all the system needs” / “stop interfering with it”  
**Note:** Consistency.

> ▶ *[L3]* The evening doesn't need to be elaborate. A couple of the right things, done consistently — a clear signal that the Wake Executives are ready to hand over to the Sleep Executives. That's all the system needs.
>
> *[L5]* What's already happening by the time dinner is done: cortisol — your primary Wake Executive — is naturally declining. Melatonin is beginning to rise. The sleep window is approaching on its own.
>
> ▶ *[L7]* What the evening requires, then, is that you stop interfering with it.
>
> *[L9]* Bright overhead light tells the Sleep Executives you're not ready for them yet. So does stimulating content, and so does any conversation that goes somewhere emotionally activating. Each of those inputs is a signal that the day isn't over yet — so the window slides later, and you arrive at bed exhausted but wide awake.


### 18.2 · Groups B · L23–33
**Trigger:** “the body can begin to recognise it as a signal” / “effects on heart rate and recovery… the effect compounds”  
**Note:** Conditioning plus recovery metrics.

> *[L23]* The bed is for sleep. Whatever didn't get resolved before I got into it doesn't get resolved in it. If I really need a conversation – this is real life – do it sitting up. Lying down means sleep. The only exception is intimacy.
>
> ▶ *[L25]* In practice, the day ends in the same place every time, so the body can begin to recognise it as a signal for sleep.
>
> *[L27]* The last part of this habit is the actual time itself.
>
> *[L29]* Start by working backwards from your wake-up time. Allow eight hours. That gives you roughly thirty minutes to fall asleep and seven and a half hours of actual sleep — a solid baseline. Of course, you may not be getting that at the beginning, but that is what you are working toward. That's your bedtime and it should be consistent, within reason.
>
> ▶ *[L31]* The science says keep your bedtime within 30 minutes of the same time, each night. There is reasonable evidence behind this — even modest deviations later than your usual bedtime show measurable effects on heart rate and recovery the following day, and the effect compounds the further you stretch it.
>
> *[L33]* In practice, this is a judgement call. If you genuinely aren't sleepy when your set bedtime arrives, going to bed anyway tends to mean lying there frustrated — which is the opposite of what we are trying to achieve. Stay up a little longer — keep the evening quiet, nothing activating — and let sleep find you.


### 18.3 · Groups C · L35–41
**Trigger:** “target-most-nights rather than a hard rule” / “the occasional late night stays recoverable instead of becoming drift”  
**Note:** **Resilience.** An occasional miss is recoverable, which is the fitness payoff.

> *[L35]* Going to bed earlier is a little more flexible. Earlier than your set time, providing you’re feeling sleepy, is generally fine. In fact, as my sleep improved, I found that bed-a-bit-earlier was the only sustainable way to catch up on a poor night without resorting to napping. Varying your bedtime carries a cost, but going earlier its less of a problem.
>
> ▶ *[L37]* So: aim for consistency, allow for tiredness in either direction, and treat the thirty-minute window as your target-most-nights rather than a hard rule.
>
> ▶ *[L39]* The one thing that doesn't flex is the morning. The wake-up time is the anchor. The bedtime can move a little in either direction. The morning cannot. Protect it, and the occasional late night stays recoverable instead of becoming drift.
