# Chapter 9 - What Goes Wrong

You've now met all four executives. One at a time. But you don't sleep one at a time — they all arrive at once. Here we bring them together: what alignment looks like, and what goes wrong.

Back in Chapter 4 I showed you this and said to hold it loosely.

A quick recap. Four executives. You don't run them directly — you send three signals across the day. A signal to be active. A signal to be calm. And a signal about timing — when to switch between them. Get those right and the hormones rise and fall as they should, landing in the right place at bedtime. That's a good night's sleep.

Now you can read the graph properly.

![[ExecutivesLineUp.png]]

Through the day, the Wake Executives run the shift. Through the evening, they stand down. The Sleep Executives take over. At bedtime, sleep hormones high, wake hormones low. Everything lands where it should.

You don't feel any of this. You just sleep.

## The bad night

Here is the chaos that is misalignment. Just understand That when things go wrong they go very wrong. Once I had this picture it suddenly became clear why a magic pill that put me o sleep for a quality 8 hours sleep was a unicorn. How could I possibly expect to pull this in to line with a quick fix or one simple trick. 

![[graph - misaligned night]]

Cortisol won't fall - still activated through the night. Dopamine's spikes, with the social media checks. Melatonin is rising, but fighting the two Wake Executives that won't go home. Also blunted due to long term insomnia. Adenosine continues building until it the rest of the hormones take notice, but too late to properly clear overnight. 

If that's a bit much to take in all at once don't worry if you don't understand it, I'll break it down further with specific examples below. 

## Struggle getting to sleep

**Cortisol**
During the day your nervous system should be returning you to baseline so you land in the evening at a low level, ready for sleep. Instead what can happen is one of two things (a) You are activated all day - hypervigilance, anxiety, stuck in go-mode - nervous system not bringing you back down. Or (b) you have developed an subconcious association with bed that provokes anxiety about sleep (or otherwise sets your mind in to top gear).

Either way you carry elevate cortisol through the night that is fighting hard to keep you awake. Your mind is now racing.

GRAPH

**Dopamine**
Cortisol by itself is enough to fight melatonin and adenosine, but a quick social media check, or an engaging television show, right before bed. Then another one in the middle of the night, now you have both wake executives fighting to keep your eyes open.

GRAPH

**Adenosine**
Since you still havent got to sleep Adenosine has continues building and building. If you do finally manage to get a few hours its only because adenosine got so strong, the other hormones had no choice but to listen and stand down for a few hours.

Problem is, only a few hours means adenonsine hasn't had time to clear properly, so you carry that sleep debt through the day and in to the next evening.

GRAPH


## Sleep window

The feeling of a narrow sleep window is roughly equivalent to the time at which the pull to sleep feels the strongest. This is the time period where melatonin is rising fastest - not the peak melatonin, but rising fastest -  adenonsine is at its peak, and another circadian system has taken off the brakes (this mechanism is left out for simplicity).  This is the convergence  discussed in Chapter XX.

If cortisol is elevated more than typical, but not enough to override, this is when sleep can occur. 

Miss that window, melatonin production has slowed, and the convergence has passed. Its now weak enough that its back to coritsol winning the fight.... you're awake. 

GRAPH


## Waking at 3am

If you managed to get to sleep in your window. The next most common complaint is waking in the middle of the night, around 2-4am, depending on what time you usually go to bed.

At this point of the night your circadian system begins to reverse the production to get you ready for waking.

At that point in the night melatonin has peaked and production stops. For a normal sleeper there's still enough melatonin to carry you through the rest of the night, but for a poor sleeper, either melatonin is blunted, or that change (rather than the total amount) is enough to weaken this executive from doing their job.

Adenosine clears at its fasted rate during deep sleep, which is usually front loaded in to first half of the night. So by 3am a large proportion has cleared, reducing the strength of sleep pressure. It's still at work, but losing steam and not supporting melatonin as strongly as it was earlier in the night. 

For a good sleeper the slowing down of the Sleep Execs is usually not a problem, because Cortisol is low and only just starting to ramp up. They may sleep through it, or they may just wake briefly, quick pee and then back to sleep. But for a poor sleeper with elevated cortisol, all of sudden this wake executive is strong enough to over power the weakening sleep executives, and flick you in to full racing-mind-mode. Sleep is done for tonight.

## Same problem, every time

Different nights. One cause.

Not a flaw in you. Not a bad sleeper. Misaligned — four executives arriving at bedtime in the wrong places.

Which is the good news. Alignment is something you can change. And you mostly change it during the day, not at night.

That's the program.
