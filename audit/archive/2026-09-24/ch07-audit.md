**Beat: caffeine morning window (paragraph beginning "That morning rise has a name")**
*Issue:* The practical caffeine recommendation ("delay the first coffee by sixty to ninety minutes") is the most textbook passage in the chapter and has no personal grounding. It also previews ch13's caffeine chapter without need. The mechanism detail (cortisol awakening response, 60–90 minute peak) is useful, but the embedded practical recommendation pulls the chapter away from its core argument — rhythm disruption at *night* — before that argument has been made. The paragraph works better trimmed.
*Proposed fix:* Remove the final two sentences of the paragraph ("There's some evidence that caffeine during that window may disrupt that peak… let the body's own system do its job first.") and reserve the caffeine-morning interaction for ch13, where it belongs.

Before:
> That morning rise has a name — the cortisol awakening response. In the first sixty to ninety minutes after waking, cortisol peaks sharply, then begins its long arc downward through the day. It's the body's own alerting system, and it works best when nothing interferes with it. ~~There's some evidence that caffeine during that window may disrupt that peak — though the research is still developing. The practical recommendation is the same either way: delay the first coffee by sixty to ninety minutes and let the body's own system do its job first.~~
>
> Through the day, cortisol responds to demand.

After:
> That morning rise has a name — the cortisol awakening response. In the first sixty to ninety minutes after waking, cortisol peaks sharply, then begins its long arc downward through the day. It's the body's own alerting system, and it works best when nothing interferes with it.
>
> Through the day, cortisol responds to demand.

## Stage 2 — Six-Category Review

---

### Contradictions

**Contradiction 1 — Caffeine morning window vs. ch13**
The chapter states: "There's some evidence that caffeine during that window may disrupt that peak — though the research is still developing." Ch13 (per index: `caffeine` appears in ch05, ch13) covers the caffeine morning window as a standalone mechanism. The hedged language here ("some evidence", "still developing") may conflict with how confidently the claim is made in ch13, or may introduce the topic in a weaker register that ch13 then has to work against. *Proposal: remove the caffeine sentences from this chapter entirely (see Beat Analysis above) and let ch13 own the topic with full context.*

No other contradictions found.

---

### Cadence and Flow

**Flow 1 — Memoir-to-theory interruption mid-anecdote**
The chapter sets up a personal anecdote: "Here's what I was doing in my worst period" → laptop, TV, phone. The reader is in the story. Then it cuts immediately to theory: "Here's the problem. Cortisol and melatonin should work in opposition…" before the anecdote has landed its consequence. The result is that the theory sits between the behaviour and its explanation, making the reader mentally backtrack.
*Proposal:* Move the cortisol/melatonin handover paragraph so it follows directly after "It didn't matter which one... The Sleep Executives never stood a chance." That paragraph already delivers the consequence. The theory paragraph should precede the three behaviour options, not interrupt them.

Reordered flow would be:
> Here's the problem. Cortisol and melatonin should work in opposition… [theory]
> Here's what I was doing in my worst period. [behaviour]
> It didn't matter which one… [consequence]

**Flow 2 — Tonal deceleration: the boredom paragraph**
"I think about what boredom used to feel like." is a reflective, nostalgic paragraph that works thematically but decelerates the chapter at the wrong moment — right after the modern-life critique, just before the news/notifications beat. It reads as an aside. In the author's usual register this kind of observation would land harder if compressed: one sentence rather than a full paragraph.
*Proposal:* Not a cut — the observation earns its place. But consider whether it can be folded into the surrounding text rather than given a standalone paragraph. The final sentence — "Real downtime — not chosen, not curated, just enforced by circumstance" — is the one that does the work.

---

### Weak Points

**Weak point 1 — Positive midnight thoughts left hanging**
The opening establishes that positive thoughts at 2am (new business idea, career move) carry the same cortisol signature as anxious ones. This is a good and counterintuitive claim. But the chapter never directly closes it: the reader who identifies with productive-minded insomnia (rather than anxiety-insomnia) may feel unsatisfied. The chapter pivots to the general mechanism without confirming: "yes, even that version is the same problem." One sentence would seal it.

*Proposed addition* (after "Regardless of the content of the thoughts. the biology underneath was identical."):

> ~~The biology doesn't care how you got there. The hormonal signature is the same. The physical experience is the same.~~
> **The biology doesn't care how you got there. Whether it was dread or a business plan, the hormonal signature was the same. The physical experience was the same.**

*(Minor: also note the full stop after "thoughts" in "Regardless of the content of the thoughts. the biology underneath was identical" — should be a comma or the sentence restructured.)*

**Weak point 2 — "Do it for long enough and it becomes a subconscious habit"**
Addressed in Beat Analysis. The sentence gestures at a meaningful claim (the waking cycle becomes habitual, self-sustaining) but doesn't develop it. If the book has a page on habit formation as it applies to sleep disruption, this is the place it would connect. As written it reads like a trailing observation rather than a beat that earns its position.

### Over-explaining

**Over-explain 1 — Double summary of the cortisol rhythm**
The paragraph "Through the day, cortisol responds to demand… Spike and return. Spike and return." and the paragraph immediately following ("That's the design. Rise when needed. Return to baseline when the demand passes. Fall away at night.") cover identical ground. The second is a clean-sentence restatement of the first. One of them should go.
*Proposal:* Cut "That's the design. Rise when needed. Return to baseline when the demand passes. Fall away at night." — the preceding paragraph already closes with "until by the time sleep approaches, cortisol is low enough for the Sleep Executives to take over." The next beat ("The goal was never to eliminate cortisol.") follows cleanly from there.

Before:
> Through the day, cortisol responds to demand. A difficult meeting, a hard workout, a deadline, a disagreement — each one triggers a temporary spike, giving the system what it needs to respond. Then, when the demand passes, a well-regulated system brings cortisol back down. Spike and return. Spike and return. Across the day, this creates a general downward baseline — the morning peak gradually declining through the afternoon and into the evening, until by the time sleep approaches, cortisol is low enough for the Sleep Executives to take over.
>
> ~~That's the design. Rise when needed. Return to baseline when the demand passes. Fall away at night.~~
>
> The goal was never to eliminate cortisol.

**Over-explain 2 — Final summary paragraph**
"The goal isn't a life without cortisol spikes… That mistakes midnight for midday… already making the coming night harder before the current one has finished." This paragraph restates the chapter's central argument in full. It follows the strong ending of the podcast anecdote and the 'day behaviours' section. The argument has already been made well. This paragraph delays the arrival at "Give cortisol a rhythm, and the rest of the system has a chance" — which is the real closing beat.
*Proposal:* Cut this paragraph. The close reads more powerfully as:

> …Watching something gripping overrides it. These are day behaviours. They tell the Wake Executive the shift is still running. The Wake Executive keeps sending the orders down.
>
> Give cortisol a rhythm, and the rest of the system has a chance. Without it, everything else — the melatonin signal, the sleep window, the adenosine pressure built up across the day — is working against a tide that never turns.
>
> Cortisol was the executive I hadn't accounted for.
> …

### Repetition

**Repetition 1 — "Every [thing] was an accelerant / wrong signal" (three iterations)**
The idea that the author's coping behaviours were cortisol-raising is stated three times in three different forms:
1. "Lying awake at 2am... I'd get up, open the laptop... Or I'd put on the television... Or I'd reach for the phone." (narrative)
2. "It didn't matter which one... cortisol mechanism is identical whether you're drafting emails or watching a thriller. Stimulation is stimulation." (restatement as principle)
3. "Every coping mechanism I'd reached for was, in cortisol terms, an accelerant." (final restatement)

Items 1 and 3 are necessary — one as the story, one as the conclusion. Item 2 ("It didn't matter which one… Stimulation is stimulation. The Sleep Executives never stood a chance.") may be cut or compressed, as it re-explains what the story already showed and what item 3 will re-confirm.
*Proposal:* Cut "The cortisol mechanism is identical whether you're drafting emails or watching a thriller. Stimulation is stimulation." from item 2. The remaining sentence — "The Sleep Executives never stood a chance" — lands the beat without the re-explanation.

*Note:* "overrides it / overrides it / overrides it" in the near-penultimate paragraph is deliberate anaphora and should be preserved.

**Cross-chapter repetition:**
Per index, cortisol rhythm is introduced in ch04. The spike-and-return explanation here is more detailed than ch04's treatment and is appropriate as the full-chapter explanation. No redundancy issue — this is the chapter that owns the concept. Flag for AUDIT pass: ensure ch04's treatment is brief enough not to front-run ch07's payoff.
