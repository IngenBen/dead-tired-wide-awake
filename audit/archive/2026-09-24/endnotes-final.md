## Chapter 1 — The Weight of It

**Does dropping off instantly mean you're sleeping well?**

A mean sleep-onset latency below 5–8 minutes on the Multiple Sleep Latency Test is
classified as pathological sleepiness; deliberate sleep deprivation drives it below one
minute. Falling asleep the moment one's head hits the pillow is a reliable marker of
significant sleep debt, though very short latency can also occur in sleep apnoea and
narcolepsy.

Sleep Foundation, *Sleep Latency* (MSLT norms summary).
https://www.sleepfoundation.org/how-sleep-works/sleep-latency

## Chapter 2 — The Turning Point

**Can hard training make sleep worse, not better?**

High-intensity exercise raises cortisol substantially (approximately 83% at 80% of
VO₂max) and produces prolonged post-exercise elevation in core body temperature, both
of which can delay or disrupt sleep onset. Very hard training sessions — particularly
late in the day — can worsen rather than improve sleep quality through this dual pathway.

Hill EE et al. 2008, *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18787373/
Kenny GP, McGinn R 2017, *J Appl Physiol* 122:933–944.
https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00517.2016

## Chapter 3 — The Uncomfortable Truth

**Is CBN really more sleep-targeted than CBD?**

Cannabinol (CBN) forms from the degradation of THC and has mild psychoactive properties.
The claim that it is more sleep-targeted than CBD is not established: the systematic
evidence for cannabinoids and sleep shows benefit primarily from THC-type compounds, with
no significant effect for CBD alone and only a small reduction in night-time waking in
the one published human CBN trial.

da Silva SHN et al. 2025, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* — meta-analysis; benefit came mainly
from THC-type compounds. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2025.102156
Bonn-Miller MO et al. 2024, *Exp Clin Psychopharmacol* — human CBD trial; no significant
sleep effect. https://doi.org/10.1037/pha0000682
Saleska JL et al. 2024, *J Am Nutr Assoc* — the only published human CBN trial; small
reduction in night-time waking only. https://doi.org/10.1080/27697061.2023.2203221

## Chapter 4 — Sleep Incorporated

**Does dopamine signal "reward" or "wanting"?**

Dopamine encodes incentive salience — the motivational "wanting" or "worth pursuing"
signal — rather than pleasure itself. It responds to opportunity and anticipation,
driving approach behaviour. This distinction matters: a dopamine-driven state is
activating regardless of whether the underlying urge is productive or threatening.

Berridge KC 2007, *Psychopharmacology* 191:391–431.
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6722949

## Chapter 5 — Adenosine

**What does "largely lifted" mean for adenosine by morning?**

By morning, the bulk of the sleep-pressure debt accumulated across the previous waking
day has cleared — but adenosine clearance is gradual and progressive across the whole
night rather than an overnight reset to a fixed baseline. Slow-wave sleep is most closely
linked to this process; the homeostatic sleep literature tracks it through the
slow-wave-activity curve, which reflects how much adenosine-related pressure has been
discharged. "Largely lifted" is accurate: the clearing is substantial but not necessarily
complete, particularly after disrupted or shortened slow-wave sleep.

Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31:e13597.
https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13597
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*.
https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Does an irregular sleep schedule reduce deep sleep?**

Deep sleep is front-loaded into the first half of the night. An irregular schedule
shifts circadian timing, so a later sleep onset reduces the window available for
slow-wave sleep. Social jetlag literature and standard sleep-stage data both support
this mechanism.

Roenneberg T et al. 2012, *Curr Biol* — social jetlag.
https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/43/10/zsaa076/5825065
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*.
https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Why doesn't alcohol appear in this chapter's list of deep sleep disruptors?**

Alcohol is absent from this chapter's disruptors list because its effect on slow-wave
sleep runs counter to the typical disruption pattern — most doses actually increase
early-night deep sleep rather than reducing it. The sleep cost of alcohol lies in REM
suppression and second-half fragmentation, which is why it is covered separately in
Chapter 15. Including it in a list of "things that reduce deep sleep" would have been
inaccurate.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030
Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549.
https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006

## Chapter 6 — Melatonin

**When does melatonin actually peak during the night?**

"The middle of the night" reflects the broad range in the literature: melatonin's
acrophase falls roughly between midnight and 3 a.m. in most people, then declines
gradually through the early morning hours. Exact timing varies considerably between
individuals and is anchored to habitual wake time — which is why naming a specific clock
hour would have been misleading. For most adults sleeping around 10–11 p.m., "the middle
of the night" corresponds to roughly 1–3 a.m.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*.
https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin

**Does the overnight melatonin signal really run low?**

Several small studies have found that people with chronic insomnia — especially those who
struggle to stay asleep — produce less melatonin through the middle of the night, with a
lower overnight peak, and the shortfall appears greatest once the insomnia has lasted
years. The evidence is suggestive rather than settled: the studies are small and not fully
consistent, and it remains unclear whether the lower signal helps drive the disturbed
sleep or simply travels with it. Recent circadian reviews lean toward a blunted overnight
amplitude in sleep-maintenance insomnia, but no study has tied a reduced level to a
specific mid-night awakening. It is best read as one contributing factor, not a deficiency
that explains insomnia on its own.

Hajak G et al. 1995, *J Pineal Res* 19:116–122 — mid-night melatonin peak blunted
(~82 vs ~117 pg/ml in controls), deficit greatest where insomnia had lasted over five
years; small sample, sleep-maintenance phenotype.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079x.1995.tb00179.x
Riemann D et al. 2002, *Psychiatry Res* 113:17–27 — nocturnal melatonin diminished in
primary insomnia; authors called its significance "a matter of debate"; small sample.
https://doi.org/10.1016/s0165-1781(02)00249-4
Léger D, Laudon M, Zisapel N 2004, *Am J Med* 116:91–95 — lower nocturnal melatonin
excretion in older insomnia patients vs age-matched controls.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2003.07.017
Lack LC, Micic G, Lovato N 2023, *J Sleep Res* 32:e13976 — review; assigns
sleep-maintenance insomnia to a "blunted amplitude" of circadian rhythm.
https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13976

**Does slow-release melatonin hold the night?**

Slow-release (prolonged-release) melatonin is designed to trickle out across the night
rather than arrive in a single early pulse, the aim being to sustain the overnight signal
and help maintain sleep. The trial evidence is modest: the studies are few, mostly in
older adults, largely self-reported, and much of the work comes from groups affiliated
with the manufacturer. They measured falling asleep more than staying asleep, and people
with low natural melatonin did not respond better — age predicted response, not melatonin
level. Where an overnight gain appeared it was small and by self-report, in the subgroup
most expected to benefit.

Wade AG et al. 2010, *BMC Medicine* 8:51 — prolonged-release melatonin 2 mg, n=791,
primary insomnia; total sleep time +13.1 min in low excretors (self-reported); low
melatonin did not predict response (age did); manufacturer-affiliated (Neurim).
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2933606/
Lemoine P et al. 2007, *J Sleep Res* 16:372–380 — prolonged-release melatonin 2 mg vs
placebo, n=170 aged ≥55; improved self-rated sleep quality and morning alertness, no
rebound or withdrawal. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2007.00613.x
Ferracioli-Oda E et al. 2013, *PLoS ONE* 8:e63773 — meta-analysis, 19 trials (mostly
immediate-release): small reductions in sleep latency, small increases in total sleep.
https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0063773

**Does a high melatonin dose carry over into the next morning?**

A high dose does not strengthen the sleep signal; it extends melatonin activity into the
following morning, which can produce grogginess. Over time, supraphysiological doses may
blunt sensitivity to the body's own melatonin signal through receptor desensitisation
(this mechanism rests primarily on animal and cell-line research).

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*.
https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin
Menczel Schrire Z et al. 2022, *J Pineal Res* 72(2):e12782.
https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.12782
Gerdin MJ et al. 2004, *FASEB J* 18(14):1646–1656 — MT2 receptor desensitisation
(rat/cell-line model). https://doi.org/10.1096/fj.03-1339com

## Chapter 7 — Cortisol

**Does cortisol start rising before you open your eyes?**

Cortisol follows a circadian rhythm that rises through the late night and early morning
as part of the normal diurnal cycle, so it is already elevated before waking. A distinct
post-waking phenomenon — the cortisol awakening response (CAR) — produces an additional
surge peaking 30–45 minutes after waking, superimposed on this overnight rise. The claim
that cortisol is "already rising before you open your eyes" is directionally correct for
the circadian component, which accounts for the gradual pre-waking increase. The CAR is
the sharper peak that follows.

Fries E, Dettenborn L, Kirschbaum C 2009, *Int J Psychophysiol* 72:67–73.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2008.03.014

## Chapter 8 — Dopamine

**Do screens desensitise the brain's reward system over time?**

Some evidence suggests that heavy exposure to fast, frequent, unpredictable digital
stimuli may reduce dopamine-receptor availability, making lower-intensity rewards less
satisfying over time. No systematic review has established this as a confirmed finding;
it is best understood as a plausible hypothesis supported by partial evidence.

Clark L, Zack M 2023, *Addict Behav* 140:107626.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2023.107626
Berridge KC 2007, *Psychopharmacology* 191:391–431.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-006-0578-x

**Does provocative content add cortisol on top of dopamine?**

Curiosity-driven content activates the dopamine reward system through incentive salience;
emotionally provocative or negative content additionally activates the stress-arousal
system. This is a reasonable synthesis of the dopamine "wanting" literature and the
cortisol-stress literature, though no single study makes the exact dual claim.

Berridge KC 2007, *Psychopharmacology* 191:391–431.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-006-0578-x
Kim EJ, Dimsdale JE 2007, *Behav Sleep Med* 5:256–278.
https://doi.org/10.1080/15402000701557383

**Is predictable TV genuinely less activating than interactive media?**

Low-stakes, predictable content produces fewer unpredictable dopamine-reward events than
interactive games or algorithmically curated feeds. This is a reasonable extension of the
variable-reward and novelty-seeking literature, though no direct head-to-head
experimental comparison exists.

Berridge KC 2007, *Psychopharmacology* 191:391–431.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-006-0578-x
Clark L, Zack M 2023, *Addict Behav* 140:107626.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2023.107626

**Are games and social feeds deliberately engineered for dopamine?**

Variable-ratio reinforcement — unpredictable rewards — maximises dopamine-driven approach
behaviour. Games and social-media feeds exploit this mechanism by design. The neuroscience
of variable reward is well established; the description of design intent as deliberate is
as much a design-ethics observation as a neuroscience claim.

Clark L, Zack M 2023, *Addict Behav* 140:107626.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2023.107626

## Chapter 11 — Your Feedback Loop

**Does HRV measure how well the nervous system balances its two modes?**

Heart rate variability measures autonomic regulation; higher HRV is associated with
greater parasympathetic (vagal) tone and better recovery capacity. "Balancing two modes"
is a useful simplification — the PNS and SNS contributions to HRV are non-linear — but
the directional interpretation (higher HRV ≈ better recovery) is well supported.

Shaffer F, Ginsberg JP 2017, *Front Public Health* 5:258.
https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2017.00258

**Is REM sleep where memory consolidation and emotional processing happen?**

REM sleep is central to memory consolidation and emotional processing, though
consolidation most likely requires both non-REM and REM. Attributing it solely to REM
oversimplifies the process. The amygdala is markedly active during REM, and REM sleep is
strongly associated with emotional regulation.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*.
https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Can sleep tracking increase anxiety about sleep?**

For some users, sleep-tracker data drives obsessive monitoring and heightened anxiety
about sleep quality — a phenomenon termed "orthosomnia." The appropriate response for
users who find tracking counterproductive is to set the tracker aside.

Baron KG et al. 2017, *J Clin Sleep Med* 13:351.
https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/10.5664/jcsm.6472

**Does the Oura Ring have the most published validation research?**

Among consumer wearables, the Oura Ring has accumulated the most published validation
studies. A 2024 PSG-comparison study (96 participants, 421,045 epochs) validated the
Gen 3 device; a 2024 multi-device study rated it most accurate in four-stage sleep
staging (~79% agreement). The caveat that Oura may simply publish more than competitors
is fair given the proportion of Oura-funded studies in the literature.

Svensson T, Madhawa K et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine* 115:251–263.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2024.01.020
Robbins R, Weaver MD et al. 2024, *Sensors* 24(20):6532.
https://doi.org/10.3390/s24206532

## Chapter 12 — Supplements

**Does phosphatidylserine reduce the evening cortisol response?**

Phosphatidylserine (PS) at 800 mg/day significantly blunted the ACTH and cortisol
response to physical exercise in a controlled trial. The evidence is specific to
exercise-induced cortisol, not the evening pre-sleep cortisol profile, so the
application to sleep is a reasonable extension rather than a directly demonstrated
effect. Soy- or sunflower-derived PS is the recommended current form (the brain-cortex
source used in the original trial is no longer available). Around 400 mg taken
30–60 minutes before bed is consistent with clinical dosing, though the original trial
used 400–800 mg under exercise-stress conditions.

Monteleone P et al. 1992, *Eur J Clin Pharmacol* 42:385–388.
https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00280123

**Does magnesium L-threonate cross into the brain more effectively?**

Magnesium L-threonate is often recommended on the basis that it crosses the blood–brain
barrier more readily than other forms. That advantage rests primarily on animal studies;
human sleep RCT evidence for L-threonate specifically is very limited, and the overall
evidence that any magnesium form meaningfully improves sleep is low to moderate quality.

Mah J, Pitre T 2021, *BMC Complement Med Ther* 21:125.
https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-021-03297-z

**How much elemental magnesium is in a standard evening dose?**

Around 150–300 mg of elemental magnesium in the evening is within the range studied in
insomnia trials; a post-intervention reduction in sleep-onset latency of roughly
17 minutes was observed across eligible RCTs, though evidence quality is low. When taking
magnesium L-threonate, the label refers to the whole compound: a 2 g (2,000 mg) compound
serving delivers only approximately 144 mg of elemental magnesium (~8.3% by weight,
derived from the compound's molecular weight — this is a calculation, not a
separately-cited peer-reviewed measurement, though it is consistent with manufacturer
labelling). Citrate and glycinate forms are typically labelled by elemental content and
are directly comparable to the studied range.

Mah J, Pitre T 2021, *BMC Complement Med Ther* 21:125.
https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-021-03297-z
Liu G et al. 2016, *J Alzheimers Dis* 49(4):971–990 — research dosing for magnesium
L-threonate (MMFS-01). https://doi.org/10.3233/JAD-150538

## Chapter 13 — Your Sleep Environment

**Why is temperature specifically "wired into the biology" of sleep?**

Temperature is embedded in sleep physiology in a way light and noise are not: the core
body temperature drop is part of the initiating mechanism of sleep, not merely a
condition to manage around it. Elevated ambient temperature increases wakefulness and
reduces both slow-wave and REM sleep, while the core temperature decline is most closely
aligned with NREM sleep onset. The chapter's other environmental factors — light and
noise — affect the sleep system primarily through signalling pathways; temperature
directly constrains the physiological conditions sleep requires to occur.

Harding EC, Franks NP, Wisden W 2019, *Front Neurosci* 13:336.
https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.00336
Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *J Physiol Anthropol* 31:14.
https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14

**Where does the 29 °C near-naked sleep figure come from?**

This figure traces to an early laboratory study in which six men slept in shorts only
with no bedding across five ambient temperatures; thermoneutrality — the least
disruptive condition — was around 29 °C. It is a small (n=6), dated, near-naked study,
useful for illustrating the principle that the body needs to shed heat, not for
prescribing a room temperature. Its insight is comparative: stripped of covers, the room
must supply all the warmth, so the comfortable point sits far above the 16–20 °C usually
quoted for a covered sleeper. The more robust finding from later work is that with
bedding, the "bed climate" against the skin stays roughly 32–34 °C across a wide range
of room temperatures — covering up matters more than room air temperature.

Haskell EH, Palca JW, Walker JM, Berger RJ, Heller HC 1981, *Electroencephalogr Clin
Neurophysiol* 51(5):494–501 (n=6, near-naked; illustrates the principle rather than
prescribing a room temperature).
https://doi.org/10.1016/0013-4694(81)90226-1
Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *J Physiol Anthropol* 31:14 (bed-climate stability
across room temperatures). https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14

**How much does body temperature drop at sleep onset?**

The physiology literature consistently places the core temperature decline at sleep
initiation at approximately 0.5–1 °C — the range the book gives, with "about one to two
degrees Fahrenheit" as the accurate conversion. The decline is not a single moment: core
temperature begins falling in the hour before sleep onset and continues through early
NREM sleep, with the steepest rate of change coinciding with the transition into
slow-wave sleep. Heat is shed outward through peripheral vasodilation — primarily via the
hands and feet — which is why cold extremities can slow sleep onset and why a warm bath
before bed (which drives peripheral vasodilation and accelerates the subsequent cooling)
can help initiate sleep.

Harding EC, Franks NP, Wisden W 2019, *Front Neurosci* 13:336.
https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.00336

## Chapter 14 — Caffeine

**What is caffeine's actual half-life?**

"Roughly five hours" is a reasonable estimate. The European Food Safety Authority's 2015
opinion gives approximately four hours as the average; a 2026 clinical pharmacokinetic
study measured it at 4.0 hours; the broader literature spans approximately 3–7 hours
depending on individual variation, medications (oral contraceptives and some SSRIs
prolong it notably), and pregnancy (which can extend it to 8–15 hours). The practical
implication of the calculation in this chapter — that caffeine from a mid-morning double
shot is still circulating at a meaningful level by evening — holds across the full range
of individual values, and is more pronounced for anyone toward the longer end of the
distribution.

Agboola OE et al. 2026, *Curr Ther Res Clin Exp* 104:100832.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.curtheres.2026.100832
EFSA 2015, *Scientific Opinion on the safety of caffeine*, *EFSA Journal* 13(5):4102.
https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine and Sleep*.
https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-and-sleep

**Does delaying the first coffee make caffeine work better?**

Waiting 60–90 minutes before the first cup is promoted on the grounds that drinking
during the natural cortisol-awakening surge and ongoing adenosine clearance wastes the
effect and builds tolerance faster. The morning cortisol-caffeine evidence base for this
specific recommendation is limited; no tracker-confirmed difference has been established.
It is a reasonable, low-confidence practical tip rather than established science.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine and Sleep*.
https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-and-sleep

## Chapter 15 — Alcohol

**Does alcohol affect deep sleep — and if so, how?**

Alcohol does not reduce slow-wave sleep; at most doses it increases it in the first half
of the night, and at higher doses the primary change is a reduced latency to deep sleep —
reaching it faster rather than getting more of it overall. This appears to be a sedative
artefact: alcohol's CNS depressant effects push the system into deeper non-REM states
earlier than it would naturally arrive. The cost comes in the second half of the night,
when the alcohol has metabolised and its suppression of REM sleep dominates. Early deep
sleep pulled forward by alcohol is not a bonus — it comes at the expense of the overall
architecture, displacing the REM that would have followed.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030
Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549.
https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006

**Does alcohol suppress REM and disrupt the second half of sleep?**

Alcohol reliably delays the onset of REM sleep and reduces its duration in a
dose-dependent manner, with disruption observed even at low doses (approximately two
standard drinks). In the second half of the night, alcohol causes increased sleep
disruption and more frequent awakenings. These two effects — REM suppression and
second-half fragmentation — are the robust and reproducible sleep costs of alcohol.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030
Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549.
https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006

**Is zero alcohol the recommended position for sleep?**

The zero recommendation the book references is the current general-health position —
major health bodies, including WHO, state that no amount of alcohol is safe for overall
health, and the book applies this direction to sleep. Sleep-specific guidance from sleep
foundations typically operationalises the same evidence as "drink as little as possible,
as infrequently as possible, finishing as early as possible" rather than a categorical
prohibition, because dose-dependent effects mean less is meaningfully better even when
zero is not the practical position. Both framings are defensible and consistent with the
evidence; the book presents both — the health-guidance floor, then the realistic
compromise.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*.
https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep
WHO (2023), *No level of alcohol consumption is safe for our health*.
https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/news/item/04-01-2023-no-level-of-alcohol-consumption-is-safe-for-our-health

**How much restorative sleep do one to four drinks cost?**

The dose-dependent effect is well supported. Even low doses — approximately two standard
drinks — produce measurable reductions in REM sleep, with disruption worsening at
approximately five standard drinks. A 2024 systematic review (Gardiner et al.) provides
the most comprehensive available quantification of these dose-response effects across
published studies. Specific per-drink minute estimates vary between studies and no single
authoritative pooled figure has been established, but the directional claim — that each
additional drink increases the cost, with a detectable effect starting as low as one to
two drinks — is robust.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030
Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549.
https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006

## Chapter 16 — Can't-Sleep Routine

**Where do the lux figures in this footnote come from?**

Light's effect on melatonin follows a dose-response curve rather than a threshold. The
50% suppression point (ED50) across study populations is approximately 24–25 lux, the
group-level figure used in the footnote. Individual sensitivity ranges across more than
an order of magnitude: roughly 6–10 lux in the most sensitive people, up to around
350 lux in the least. The figures for a TV at three metres (~5 lux) and a phone held
close (~25–50 lux) apply the inverse-square principle and are illustrative rather than
directly measured at these exact positions. The practical conclusion — that a distant
screen is unlikely to affect even sensitive individuals, while a close-held phone sits
around the group-level ED50 — follows directly from these values.

Zeitzer JM et al. 2000, *J Physiol* 526:695–702.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10922269/
Phillips AJK et al. 2019, *PNAS* 116:12019–12024.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31138694/
Bauducco S et al. 2024, *Sleep Med Rev* 76:101933.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38657359/

**Does a TV across the room deliver far less light than a phone in your hand?**

A standard television at three metres delivers approximately 5 lux to the eye; a phone
held 30 cm away delivers approximately 25–50 lux. These are illustrative figures applying
the inverse-square principle, not pinned measurements — actual levels vary with screen
brightness, size, and ambient conditions. The underlying principle is well established: a
distant screen delivers far less light to the eye than a close-held one. (No primary
measurement study was identified for these exact values at these exact distances; the
figures are consistent with the light-sensitivity data in Zeitzer et al. 2000 and
Phillips et al. 2019, cited above.)

## Chapter 17 — Morning Routine

**Does morning movement help set the body clock alongside light?**

Morning exercise has modest circadian phase-shifting effects independent of light:
physical activity is recognised as a secondary zeitgeber — a time-of-day cue — that
reinforces the primary light signal. The book's framing ("its own gentle cue" that helps
alongside light) accurately reflects the evidence: movement acts as a complementary
zeitgeber rather than a measurable amplifier of the light response. The practical upshot
is the same: combining a morning walk with outdoor light provides two consistent
time-of-day signals rather than one.

Brown TM et al. 2022, *PLoS Biology* 20:e3001571.
https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571

## Chapter 19 — Meditation

**Does nasal breathing naturally slow the breath?**

Nasal breathing imposes higher airway resistance than mouth breathing, which reduces peak
airflow and naturally slows respiration rate. This mechanism is well established in
respiratory physiology.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309.
https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817

**Does regular breathing practice train the vagus nerve to stay calmer?**

Slow breathing reliably shifts autonomic balance toward the parasympathetic (vagal) side —
a link well established in the HRV-biofeedback literature. The further step — that regular
practice keeps the cortisol response lower over time — is a reasonable inference from the
connection between vagal tone and stress regulation, but has not been directly
demonstrated. Read as "helps turn down" the stress response rather than "suppresses" it.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309.
https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817

**Does the 528 Hz frequency itself aid sleep?**

A neutral cue repeated consistently during meditation can become a conditioned calm signal
through classical conditioning — that mechanism is sound. The separate claim that the
528 Hz frequency confers a physiological benefit beyond conditioning is supported only by
one small preliminary study: a crossover of nine participants found reduced salivary
cortisol and raised oxytocin at 528 Hz compared with 440 Hz. Given the very small sample,
single session, lower-tier journal, and absence of replication, this should be treated as
preliminary evidence only.

## Chapter 20 — Napping

**When is it too late in the day to nap?**

Standard guidance recommends avoiding naps within 7–8 hours of bedtime. The Sleep
Foundation (US) specifies "at least 8 hours before bedtime" and cautions against napping
after 3 p.m.; Mayo Clinic aligns with this. The Sleep Health Foundation (AU) endorses a
15–30 minute optimal nap duration.

## Chapter 21 — Exercise

**Does regular exercise lower cortisol and improve sleep quality?**

The book's claim is well supported. A systematic review and meta-analysis (De Nys et al.
2022) found regular physical activity reduces cortisol levels (standardised mean
difference −0.37) and improves sleep quality (SMD −0.30). The evidence base was
predominantly from breast-cancer patient populations, so the effect size for
otherwise-healthy people is less precisely characterised — but the directional finding,
that regular exercise supports healthier cortisol regulation and better sleep, is
consistent with the broader exercise-physiology literature.

De Nys L et al. 2022, *Psychoneuroendocrinology* 143:105843.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2022.105843

**How close to bedtime can you exercise vigorously?**

Research supports four hours as the evidence-based threshold: vigorous exercise ending
at least four hours before sleep onset is not associated with sleep changes, while
exercise within that window is linked to delayed sleep onset, shorter sleep, lower
quality, higher nocturnal heart rate, and lower HRV (from 14,689 active adults across
4,084,354 person-nights of wearable data). A personal margin of six hours is not
contradicted by the evidence — it simply exceeds the research floor — and is a
reasonable conservative choice for anyone sensitive to late training.

Leota J et al. 2025, *Nat Commun* 16:3297.
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58271-x
Stutz J, Eiholzer R, Spengler CM 2019, *Sports Med* 49:269–287.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-1015-0

## Chapter 23 — Hydration

**Does mild dehydration raise cortisol?**

Hypohydration is associated with significantly elevated cortisol (standardised mean
difference approximately 1.12) across 21 studies in systematic review and meta-analysis.
The strongest evidence comes from exercise- or heat-induced dehydration; the everyday
version — mild dehydration from simply not drinking enough — is a reasonable extension of
this finding rather than something directly demonstrated in passive-restriction studies
alone.

Zaplatosch ME, Adams WM 2020, *Nutrients* 12:2526.
https://doi.org/10.3390/nu12092526

**Does cortisol make the bladder signal louder during lighter sleep?**

Nocturnal urine production is naturally suppressed by the overnight rise in vasopressin
(antidiuretic hormone), which peaks around midnight–4 a.m. During lighter or fragmented
sleep, the arousal threshold is lower, making the bladder signal more likely to break
through. The specific mechanism by which elevated cortisol contributes — by lightening
sleep — is a plausible inference but has not been directly demonstrated.

George CP, Messerli FH, Genest J, Nowaczynski W, Boucher R, Kuchel O, Rojo-Ortega JM
1975, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 41(2):332–338 — classic demonstration of the nocturnal
vasopressin rise. https://doi.org/10.1210/jcem-41-2-332
Andersson KE, Van Kerrebroeck P 2018, *Curr Urol Rep* 19:8.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s11934-018-0750-y

## Chapter 24 — Food Timing

**Does protein steady overnight blood sugar?**

Protein may help steady overnight blood sugar, particularly for people managing a glucose
condition, but the evidence in otherwise-healthy people is mixed. Controlled trials have
not consistently shown that the type of bedtime snack meaningfully changes overnight
glucose. Major sleep-nutrition sources credit complex carbohydrates — not protein — with
blood-sugar stability, and associate protein with tryptophan-to-serotonin pathway support
rather than glycaemic effects.

Sleep Foundation, *Healthy Bedtime Snacks to Eat Before Sleep*.
https://www.sleepfoundation.org/nutrition/healthy-bedtime-snacks
Riley TM, Kris-Etherton PM, Hart TL, Petersen KS 2024, *J Nutr* 154(4):1219–1231.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tjnut.2024.01.021
