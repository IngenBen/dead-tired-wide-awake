# Chapter 24 - Habit 12: Sugar & Food Sensitivities

I knew sugar wasn't good for me. I'd known that for years. It didn't stop me.

There was a slump that came every couple of hours — mid-morning, after lunch, mid-afternoon, the commute home, after dinner. Each one needed managing.

Sugar was how I managed it. A biscuit with a coffee. Something sweet after lunch. A handful of whatever was in the cupboard when the three o'clock wall arrived. I wasn't eating it for pleasure, particularly. I was eating it to get through the next couple of hours so I could get through the next thing. How else was I supposed to have the energy to do everything that needed doing? Coffee and sugar was the answer.

I tried to quit several times. It never stuck. So I attacked it the way I'd attacked caffeine — reduce, not quit, and do it incrementally. Two sugars in my coffee, then one and a half, then one, then half, then none. Pausing at each stage long enough for the new level to feel normal before dropping again. This worked to change my taste a little. But the craving for the energy it gave me didn't go away. I was still hitting the wall every couple of hours. I'd just stopped reaching for the same thing to get over it.

What did most of the heavy lifting, in the end, wasn't focusing on sugar directly. It was focusing on everything else.

By then I understood what I'd been up against. We already saw, with caffeine, that sleep deprivation measurably reduces your capacity for self-control — willpower runs on a battery that poor sleep never fully recharges. 

But it gets worse. Lack of sleep also pulls two of the appetite managers out of line. It raises ghrelin, which translates to "I'm hungry", and lowers leptin, which translates to "I'm not full yet".

And ghrelin doesn't only make you hungry — it acts on the brain's reward system, tilting what you crave specifically toward sweet, fatty food. So I wasn't fighting a vague urge to eat. 
I was being pushed straight toward a sugary snack, while the willpower to stop was on zero battery.  Cravings turned up, willpower turned down, and both pointed at sugar. No wonder quitting never stuck.

And that was the part no one had ever told me. Every other time I'd tried to quit, I'd been doing it the hardest way possible — willpower on empty, the appetite managers pulling hard in the opposite direction. For a long time I believed I just needed to be tougher — that I had to want it more. I suspect that idea came from a lifetime of advice written by people who'd never had a sleep problem. I now believe that if you want stronger willpower, sleep comes first.

So when the rest of the program started improving my sleep, it undid the double bind I'd been fighting — cravings high, willpower low. It felt like an unfair advantage I'd never had going into any of those earlier attempts. The slumps flattened out and those managers settled back into line — leptin and ghrelin saying the right things at the right times again. The cravings lost enough of their grip to become resistible. I didn't engineer that part directly — everything else clicking into place took the pressure off. A conscious effort did the rest.

That's why sugar comes this late in the program. Caffeine and sugar both put pressure on the same systems — tackling them simultaneously is too much change at once. Build the other habits first, let sleep improve, then use this chapter to address what's left.

Those slumps and crashes have a mechanism that explains what's actually happening when you eat sugar and feel that brief lift.

Blood glucose rises quickly — especially with refined sugar or simple carbohydrates. The body responds by releasing insulin to bring it back down. If the sugar load is large, or if this is happening repeatedly across the day, the correction overshoots. Glucose drops below where it started. The brain, which runs almost exclusively on glucose, registers this as an emergency. And the body's response to emergencies is cortisol.

Cortisol mobilises stored energy and raises blood glucose back to safe levels. The problem is that cortisol is also a Wake Executive — it's the signal that says stay alert, stay ready, don't rest. Every sugar crash is quietly asking your body to produce more of it. Do this often enough, across enough years, and the Wake Executive can become overworked and reactive. Firing when it shouldn't. Flat when you need it.  And not clocking off when he's supposed to.

It's a terrible irony. The symptoms of that dysregulation — the fatigue, the difficulty concentrating, the low mood between meals — are exactly the feelings that send you back to sugar for another hit. The loop feeds itself. You're not weak-willed. You're caught in a hormonal cycle that sugar both causes and temporarily relieves.

This matters most in the evening.

Cortisol follows a daily curve — highest in the morning, lowest by the time the Sleep Executives take over at night. A significant sugar hit late in the evening — a dessert, something sweet out of habit — disrupts that handover directly. The spike arrives, the crash follows, cortisol fires again to correct it — and suddenly the Wake Executive is back at work at exactly the moment he was supposed to clock off.

The result isn't always that you can't fall asleep. Sometimes you can. But the sleep you get is shallower, more fragmented, easier to break.

And for a lot of people, there's a specific hour — somewhere between two and three in the morning — where you wake up alert, anxious, or hot, for no obvious reason. The cortisol chapter explained why it tends to be then: as melatonin eases in the small hours, any cortisol riding higher than it should jumps in to fill the gap. A late sugar hit is one more thing that can light that fuse — a blood sugar dip provoking a cortisol surge of its own, pulling you out of deep sleep at the worst possible time. It's easy to assume you woke to use the bathroom — but often the cortisol surge comes first, and the bathroom is simply what you notice once you're awake.

Knowing that changes what you do about it.

In practice, my approach to sugar looks a lot like my approach to alcohol.

I don't eat sugar after dinner — or if I do, it's as little as possible and as low-sugar as I can make it. A small scoop of ice cream. A low-sugar hot cocoa. Something that satisfies without loading the system. During the day, I still enjoy the occasional sweet treat. Rarely soft drink -  zero sugar if I do. And only some days, not at every slump every couple of hours the way it used to be.

The method is the same as everywhere else: don't try to quit, just reduce. Adjust until that feels normal, then reduce again. The taste follows the behaviour if you give it enough time.

There's one more layer worth knowing about — particularly if you've done the work and still feel like something's off.

Some people have food sensitivities that are quietly generating what may be a low-grade immune response in the background — not an allergy, nothing immediate or dramatic, but a slow, delayed reaction that keeps the body mildly stressed. The kind of thing that's easy to misread as something else.

The symptoms are often subtle and disconnected from eating: disrupted sleep, persistent fatigue, brain fog, bloating, unexplained skin reactions, or a stuffy nose that never quite goes away. That last one is worth noting specifically — nasal congestion at night disrupts breathing, fragments sleep, and can be caused by food sensitivities in people who'd never think to make the connection.

The most commonly reactive foods are gluten, dairy, eggs, and soy. If any of those feature heavily in your diet and you recognise the symptoms above, an elimination approach is the most reliable place to start — remove the suspected food for a few weeks, reintroduce it, and watch what happens. Blood tests that assess immune reactivity to a broad range of foods are also available, returning results in a simple traffic-light format.

While I found these tests extremely helpful — they helped uncover the cause of persistent mild congestion - it’s important to note the science is contested. Many clinicians consider them unreliable, and the results should be treated as a prompt for further investigation, not a diagnosis.

If something is still disrupting your sleep, sensitivities are worth investigating. For some people, it's the final piece.

Your starting point for this chapter: draw the after-dinner line on sugar and hold it. If you suspect a sensitivity, start with the most likely culprit and give elimination a few weeks. Use your tracker to watch what happens.

The sugar piece is a good illustration of how the whole program works. It wasn't one thing fixing the other. Better sleep made cutting down manageable — and cutting down made the sleep better still. One thing led to another, the way it does when the system is working as it should. An upward spiral. Each part making the next part easier.

And that's it.

That's the last habit. Twenty-four chapters, twelve habits. The program is built.
