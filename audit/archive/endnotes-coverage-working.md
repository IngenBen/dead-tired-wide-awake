## Chapter 1 — The Weight of It

**Is poor sleep linked to serious disease?**

Insufficient sleep is associated with increased risk of heart disease, diabetes, high blood pressure, stroke, depression and obesity, and both the CDC and NINDS recognise these links. Long-term studies also link persistent short sleep in midlife to a higher risk of dementia later in life.

CDC, Chronic Disease Indicators: Sleep (2024). https://www.cdc.gov/cdi/indicator-definitions/sleep.html
NINDS, Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Sabia S et al. 2021, *Nat Commun* 12:2289. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-22354-2 (about 8,000 UK adults followed for 25 years; six hours or less in one's 50s and 60s linked to about 30% higher dementia risk; observational, so cause and effect is not established)

## Chapter 3 — The Uncomfortable Truth

(existing) **Is CBN really more sleep-targeted than CBD?**

**Can anyone truly function on little sleep?**

Natural short sleeping exists but is rare. It has been traced to variants in genes such as DEC2 and ADRB1, each found in a small number of families. For most people, sleep restriction carries a cost they do not notice: performance keeps declining while the feeling of sleepiness levels off.

He Y et al. 2009, *Science* 325:866–870. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1174443 (DEC2 variant identified in one family)
Shi G et al. 2019, *Neuron* 103:1044–1055. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.07.026 (ADRB1 variant identified in one family)
Van Dongen HPA et al. 2003, *Sleep* 26:117–126. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117 (48 healthy adults; 14 days at 4, 6 or 8 hours in bed; self-rated sleepiness rose only slightly while performance kept declining)

**Does poor sleep raise long-term health risks?**

Yes. Insufficient sleep is associated with increased risk of heart disease, diabetes and depression, and with long-term cognitive decline. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 1, in the note "Is poor sleep linked to serious disease?"

**Is driving tired as risky as driving drunk?**

Being awake for 17 hours produces impairment comparable to a blood-alcohol level of 0.05%, and being awake for 24 hours to roughly 0.10%. The comparison rests on studies showing similar impairment of cognitive and physiological functioning from fatigue and from alcohol.

CDC/NIOSH, Training for Nurses on Shift Work & Long Work Hours, Module 3 (2020). https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod3/08.html

**Does sleep depend on hormone rhythms?**

Sleep depends on several chemical signals, and calling all of them "hormones" is a simplification. Melatonin and cortisol are hormones that follow a circadian pattern of roughly 24 hours, and when that pattern is disrupted, the conditions for sleep degrade. Adenosine is classified as a neuromodulator and builds across waking hours before clearing during sleep. Dopamine acts mainly as a neurotransmitter. Grouping all four as hormones is a convenient shorthand for a wider picture, but the framing is well supported. All four respond to daily behaviour, which is the basis for the book's focus on habits. The individual habits, and the evidence for each, are covered in the chapters that follow.

NINDS, Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31:e13597. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13597

## Chapter 4 — Sleep Incorporated

**Does adenosine drive sleep pressure?**

Yes. Adenosine builds across waking hours and clears during sleep, and it is a main driver of the pressure to sleep. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 5, in the note "What does "largely lifted" mean for adenosine by morning?"

**Does melatonin set the timing of sleep?**

Yes. Melatonin rises in the evening and marks biological night, signalling when sleep should occur rather than forcing it. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 6, in the note "When does melatonin actually peak during the night?"

**Does cortisol supply waking energy?**

Yes. Cortisol follows a daily rhythm that starts rising before waking and peaks in the morning, helping mobilise energy for the day. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 7, in the note "Does cortisol start rising before you open your eyes?"

(existing) **Does dopamine signal "reward" or "wanting"?**

**Is adenosine really a hormone?**

Not strictly. Adenosine is classified as a neuromodulator, and grouping it with hormones is a simplification. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 3, in the note "Does sleep depend on hormone rhythms?"

**Do sleeping pills fix the underlying problem?**

Sleeping pills can give short-term relief, but they act on the symptom rather than on the habits and thought patterns that keep insomnia going. For that reason, the American College of Physicians recommends cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-i) as the first treatment for adults with chronic insomnia, with medication treated as a short-term addition when CBT-i alone hasn't worked.

Qaseem A et al. 2016, *Ann Intern Med* 165:125–133. https://doi.org/10.7326/M15-2175 (guideline recommendation; the medication advice is graded weak, based on low-quality evidence)

**Does menopause disrupt sleep, and can HRT help?**

Sleep problems become more common through perimenopause and menopause, and the fall in oestrogen and progesterone is linked to the change, along with hot flushes and night sweats. Hormone therapy improves sleep quality on average, but the benefit appears in women who have hot flushes or night sweats; in women without them, studies show no clear improvement. This fits the idea that HRT helps most when menopause symptoms are the main thing disrupting sleep. The evidence does not show HRT fixing sleep problems that began before menopause or have other causes.

Cintron D et al. 2017, *Endocrine* 55:702–711. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12020-016-1072-9 (systematic review and meta-analysis; moderate-quality evidence; benefit limited to women with vasomotor symptoms)
The North American Menopause Society 2022, *Menopause* 29:767–794. https://doi.org/10.1097/GME.0000000000002028 (hormone therapy position statement)

## Chapter 6 — Melatonin

**Is melatonin a sleep signal rather than a sleeping pill?**

The brain releases melatonin as light fades in the evening, marking the start of biological night. Alongside this timing role, it slightly lowers core body temperature and reduces alertness, moving the body toward a state in which sleep can happen. It does not force sleep on its own.

NINDS, Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), Melatonin. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin
Cagnacci A et al. 1997, *J Biol Rhythms* 12:509–517. https://doi.org/10.1177/074873049701200604 (core temperature)
Lok R et al. 2019, *J Pineal Res* 67:e12583. https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.12583 (alertness)

(existing) **When does melatonin actually peak during the night?**

**Does evening light delay melatonin?**

Melatonin release depends on darkness, so bright light in the evening tells the brain it is still daytime: the rise is delayed and the amount released can be reduced. Short-wavelength (blue) light, which screens produce, has the strongest effect.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), Melatonin. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin
NINDS, Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Does an irregular schedule throw off melatonin timing?**

Yes. Without consistent sleep and wake times, the body clock loses its reference point, so the evening melatonin rise can arrive at the wrong time. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 5, in the note "Does an irregular sleep schedule reduce deep sleep?"

**Can stress override rising melatonin?**

Yes. Cortisol and melatonin work in opposition, and a nervous system running hot can hold sleep off even as melatonin rises. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 7, in the note "Do cortisol and melatonin work against each other?"

**Do morning light and a consistent bedtime set melatonin timing?**

Yes. Morning light and regular sleep times anchor the body clock, which in turn sets when melatonin rises in the evening. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 18, in the note "Is morning light the strongest cue for the body clock?"

**Is overnight melatonin lower in people with insomnia?**

Several small studies have found that people with long-term insomnia, especially those who struggle to stay asleep, produce less melatonin through the middle of the night than good sleepers. The finding is suggestive rather than settled: the studies are small, not all agree, and it is unclear whether lower melatonin causes broken sleep or simply accompanies it. Recent reviews support the broader idea that a weaker overnight rhythm is linked to trouble staying asleep.

Hajak G et al. 1995, *J Pineal Res* 19:116–122. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079x.1995.tb00179.x (about 10 participants; deficit greatest where insomnia had lasted more than five years)
Riemann D et al. 2002, *Psychiatry Res* 113:17–27. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0165-1781(02)00249-4 (small sample; authors call the finding's significance a matter of debate)
Léger D, Laudon M, Zisapel N 2004, *Am J Med* 116:91–95. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2003.07.017 (older adults)
Lack LC, Micic G, Lovato N 2023, *J Sleep Res* 32:e13976. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13976 (review)
Scott H et al. 2025, *J Pineal Res* 77:e70069. https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.70069

**Does slow-release melatonin help you stay asleep?**

Slow-release melatonin is designed to release over several hours rather than all at once, with the aim of holding the overnight signal steady; a 2 mg form is licensed in Europe for short-term insomnia in adults aged 55 and over. The trial evidence is modest: studies are relatively few, mostly in older adults, rely largely on self-reported sleep, and much of the research is linked to the manufacturer. The trials mainly measured how quickly people fell asleep, and the gain in total sleep time was small (around a quarter of an hour) and seen only in some groups.

Lemoine P et al. 2007, *J Sleep Res* 16:372–380. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2007.00613.x (170 adults aged 55 and over, three weeks; improved self-rated sleep quality and morning alertness)
Wade AG et al. 2010, *BMC Medicine* 8:51. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2933606/ (791 adults, up to six months; total sleep time up 13 minutes in people with low natural melatonin, self-reported; manufacturer-affiliated)
Ferracioli-Oda E et al. 2013, *PLoS ONE* 8:e63773. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0063773 (meta-analysis of 19 trials, mostly immediate-release; modest benefits)

**When is taking melatonin genuinely useful?**

Melatonin's best-supported use is shifting the body clock when it is out of step with local time, as with jet lag or shift work, rather than as a direct sleep aid. For this purpose, low doses (around 0.5–5 mg) appear to work as well as higher ones, with no added benefit from taking more, and the usual guidance is to take it about an hour before the target bedtime.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), Melatonin. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin

## Chapter 7 — Cortisol

> Written against the Ch7 prose AFTER Ben merges audit/ch07-cortisol-dopamine-clean.md (urgency framing).

**Does cortisol respond to urgency, even when the thoughts are positive?**

Cortisol responds to demands, not only to danger, and the demands that raise it most are those that feel pressing, hard to control, or carry a risk of being judged. It rises more after waking on days when a challenge is expected, and it rises with hard physical effort. But it does not respond to every kind of arousal: a large review of laboratory studies concluded that cortisol is not responsive to all types of stressors. No study has directly tested whether enthusiastic planning in the middle of the night raises cortisol; the link rests on the urgency those thoughts carry rather than on a direct measurement.

Dickerson SS, Kemeny ME 2004, *Psychol Bull* 130:355–391. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.355 (meta-analysis of laboratory stress studies)
Wetherell MA, Lovell B, Smith MA 2015, *Stress* 18:42–48. https://doi.org/10.3109/10253890.2014.993967 (the anticipated challenge was an assessed, stressful task)
Hill EE et al. 2008, *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587–591. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03345606

(existing) **Does cortisol start rising before you open your eyes?**

**Does cortisol return to baseline once a demand passes?**

Cortisol follows a daily baseline curve, and short-term demands produce temporary rises above it. In laboratory studies cortisol climbs during a stressful task and falls back afterwards, with the largest and slowest-to-settle responses coming from situations that feel uncontrollable or involve being judged by others. Physical demands work the same way: moderate-to-hard exercise raises cortisol, while light exercise does not.

Dickerson SS, Kemeny ME 2004, *Psychol Bull* 130:355–391. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.355 (meta-analysis of laboratory stress studies)
Hill EE et al. 2008, *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587–591. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03345606 (cortisol rose at 60% and 80% of maximum aerobic capacity, not at 40%)

**Do cortisol and melatonin work against each other?**

Cortisol and melatonin follow roughly opposite daily rhythms. Cortisol peaks in the morning and declines through the day to its lowest point around the start of the night, while melatonin is low by day and rises in the evening. When cortisol stays high into the evening, as it does under stress, its alerting effect can override melatonin's signal, much as a stimulant can mask tiredness: melatonin may still be rising, but the body stays too activated for sleep to take hold. The two rhythms are well established; the override fits the hyperarousal model of insomnia rather than having been measured directly as an interaction between the two hormones.

Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), Melatonin. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin
Riemann D et al. 2010, *Sleep Med Rev* 14:19–31. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2009.04.002 (hyperarousal model of insomnia)

**Does cortisol start rising in the early hours?**

Cortisol reaches its lowest point around midnight and begins climbing in the early hours, roughly 2 to 3 a.m., on its way to a morning peak. This falls in the second half of the night, when melatonin is past its peak and sleep is lighter. The timing makes raised cortisol a plausible reason people wake at this hour, but that has not been directly shown.

Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214
NINDS, Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Does late caffeine raise cortisol?**

Caffeine raises cortisol, both at rest and under stress. In people who had stopped caffeine for several days, an early-afternoon dose kept cortisol raised into the early evening. In daily coffee drinkers the response was smaller and shorter, but an afternoon dose still produced a rise.

Lovallo WR et al. 2005, *Psychosom Med* 67:734–739. https://doi.org/10.1097/01.psy.0000181270.20036.06 (48 men and 48 women; after five days without caffeine, a 1 p.m. dose raised cortisol through to 7 p.m.; in daily users the morning response disappeared but cortisol still rose after the afternoon dose)

**Does constant stimulation keep cortisol higher?**

Partly. Provocative content brings in the stress system as well as dopamine, but the idea that everyday stimulation steadily raises baseline cortisol has not been directly measured. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 8, in the note "Does provocative content add cortisol on top of dopamine?"

**Does a bad night raise cortisol the next day?**

Sleep loss acts as a stressor. After a night of partial or total sleep deprivation, cortisol the following evening was higher than normal and its evening decline was slower, leaving the next night starting from a worse position.

Leproult R et al. 1997, *Sleep* 20:865–870. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/20.10.865 (evening cortisol up 37% after partial and 45% after total sleep loss; healthy young men)

**Why avoid daytime activities at night?**

Behavioural treatment for insomnia advises keeping the bed for sleep and leaving it when awake, rather than working or scrolling, so that bed and night become cues for sleep. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 17, in the note "Does CBT-i make getting out of bed when awake a central recommendation?"

(revised) **Does provocative content add cortisol on top of dopamine?**

Curiosity-driven content engages the dopamine system, which signals that something is worth pursuing. Provocative content, the kind that provokes a strong reaction before there's time to think, can also engage the stress system and cortisol. Cortisol responds most to demands that feel pressing, uncontrollable or judged by others, rather than to whether the content is negative or positive. This combines two well-studied areas, dopamine "wanting" and the stress response, but no single study has measured both responses to online content directly.

Berridge KC 2007, *Psychopharmacology* 191:391–431. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-006-0578-x
Dickerson SS, Kemeny ME 2004, *Psychol Bull* 130:355–391. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.355 (meta-analysis of laboratory stress studies)
Kim EJ, Dimsdale JE 2007, *Behav Sleep Med* 5:256–278. https://doi.org/10.1080/15402000701557383

**Does the brain respond to novelty and unpredictability?**

The brain's dopamine system responds strongly to things that are new or uncertain. Dopamine neurons fire most when it is unclear whether a reward will arrive, and new images on their own activate the brain's dopamine centres. These findings come from laboratory tasks rather than studies of scrolling, so applying them to social feeds is a reasonable extension, not something measured directly.

Fiorillo CD, Tobler PN, Schultz W 2003, *Science* 299:1898–1902. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1077349 (recordings in monkeys; dopamine activity peaked when reward probability was 50%)
Bunzeck N, Düzel E 2006, *Neuron* 51:369–379. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2006.06.021 (brain imaging in healthy adults)

## Chapter 9 — What Goes Wrong

**Do poor sleepers have less melatonin overnight?**

Possibly. Small studies have found lower overnight melatonin in long-term insomnia, though the finding is not settled. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 6, in the note "Is overnight melatonin lower in people with insomnia?"

**Does the bed itself become a trigger for wakefulness?**

It can. After enough nights lying awake there, the bed can become a cue for alertness, which is why behavioural treatment for insomnia keeps the bed for sleep. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 17, in the note "Does CBT-i make getting out of bed when awake a central recommendation?"

**Does melatonin tail off after the middle of the night?**

Yes. Melatonin peaks roughly between midnight and 3 a.m., then declines towards morning. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 6, in the note "When does melatonin actually peak during the night?"

**Can melatonin's overnight decline trigger an early wake?**

In people who wake too early, the body's daily rhythms, including melatonin, often run earlier than in good sleepers. Their melatonin begins to fall and their body temperature begins to rise while they are still asleep, and waking tends to follow. This suggests the timing of the overnight turn matters, not only how much melatonin is produced. The decline is best understood as a marker of that turn rather than a proven cause of waking on its own, and the studies are small.

Lack LC, Mercer JD, Wright H 1996, *J Sleep Res* 5:211–219. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.1996.00211.x (melatonin and temperature rhythms 2–4 hours earlier in early-waking insomniacs; small laboratory study)
Lack LC, Micic G, Lovato N 2023, *J Sleep Res* 32:e13976. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13976 (review)

**Does deep sleep clear sleep pressure, mostly early in the night?**

Yes. Deep sleep is concentrated in the first half of the night, and that is when most sleep pressure is cleared; clearing can fall short when deep sleep is disrupted. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 5, in the note "What does "largely lifted" mean for adenosine by morning?"

**Does cortisol start climbing around 2 to 3 a.m.?**

Yes. Cortisol reaches its low around midnight and begins rising in the early hours. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 7, in the note "Does cortisol start rising in the early hours?"

## Chapter 11 — Your Feedback Loop

**Is above 85% a good target for sleep efficiency?**

Sleep efficiency is the share of time in bed actually spent asleep. A National Sleep Foundation expert panel identified 85% or higher as a marker of good sleep quality across adult age groups, and the same figure is used as a working target in behavioural treatment for insomnia. It is a guide rather than a hard line, and trackers' estimates of time spent awake in bed are among their least accurate measures.

Ohayon M et al. 2017, *Sleep Health* 3:6–19. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2016.11.006 (expert consensus panel)

**Is deep sleep when the body does its physical repair?**

Deep (slow-wave) sleep is the stage most closely associated with physical restoration and with waking refreshed. Describing it as the body's repair time is a fair simplification: restorative processes are not confined to a single stage, and much of the evidence links deep sleep to recovery rather than measuring repair directly.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**How accurate are consumer sleep trackers?**

Consumer trackers are most accurate for total sleep time and least accurate for sleep stages. Averaged across groups of people, the better devices estimate total sleep close to laboratory measurement, though any single night can be out by considerably more. Long stretches of lying still while awake are often recorded as sleep, and devices make further errors when they guess bedtime for themselves, so correcting the recorded bedtime improves accuracy. For light, deep and REM sleep, a single night's figure can be out by up to an hour, and some devices consistently over- or under-count a particular stage. Averaging several nights does not remove all of this error, because part of it is consistent for the same person on the same device. That same consistency is why the trend over weeks on one tracker is more informative than any single night, although trend accuracy itself has not been tested directly.

Chinoy ED et al. 2021, *Sleep* 44:zsaa291. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsaa291 (seven devices, 34 healthy young adults over three nights; deep-sleep limits of agreement roughly ±55–75 min for the better devices; accuracy worse on disrupted nights; flags errors from automatic bedtime detection)
Svensson T et al. 2024, *Sleep Med* 115:251–263. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2024.01.020 (Oura Gen 3, 96 adults; up to three nights averaged per person, deep-sleep limits still about ±65 min; mainly funded by Oura)
Robbins R et al. 2024, *Sensors* 24:6532. https://doi.org/10.3390/s24206532 (Oura, Fitbit, Apple Watch, 35 adults, one night; Apple undercounted deep sleep by 43 min on average; funded by Oura)
Schyvens AM et al. 2025, *Sleep Adv* 6:zpaf021. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleepadvances/zpaf021 (six devices; WHOOP 4.0 overcounted deep sleep by about 30 min on average; single night)
Kainec KA et al. 2024, *Sensors* 24:635. https://doi.org/10.3390/s24020635 (five devices; all poor at detecting wake after sleep onset)

## Chapter 12 — Supplements

> Written against audit/ch12-supplements-clean.md (3 edits: 2 ashwagandha, 1 magnesium evidence line).

**Does the form of a supplement matter?**

The same nutrient can come in different chemical forms, and they are not absorbed equally. For magnesium, organic forms such as citrate are generally absorbed better than inorganic forms such as oxide, which is the cheapest and most common. A systematic review supports this broad pattern, though the studies are small and measure absorption in different ways, and better absorption has not been shown to translate directly into better sleep.

Lindberg JS et al. 1990, *J Am Coll Nutr* 9:48–55. https://doi.org/10.1080/07315724.1990.10720349 (citrate more soluble and better absorbed than oxide)
Pardo MR et al. 2021, *Nutrition* 89:111294. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nut.2021.111294 (systematic review)

(existing) **Does phosphatidylserine reduce the evening cortisol response?**

**Does ashwagandha reduce stress and help sleep?**

Standardised ashwagandha root extracts have reduced self-reported stress and improved sleep in several small randomised trials, and a meta-analysis found a modest overall benefit for sleep, clearer at higher daily doses and over eight weeks or more. KSM-66 is the most widely studied extract, including a trial that found lower morning cortisol after two months, but other standardised extracts have trials of their own and none has been shown to outperform the others head to head. Trials usually split the dose across the day, so no best time of day has been established. Many trials were small, and the extract was often supplied by its manufacturer.

Cheah KL et al. 2021, *PLoS One* 16:e0257843. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0257843 (meta-analysis; effect clearer at 600 mg/day or more and 8 weeks or longer)
Chandrasekhar K, Kapoor J, Anishetty S 2012, *Indian J Psychol Med* 34:255–262. https://doi.org/10.4103/0253-7176.106022 (KSM-66, 300 mg twice daily, 64 adults with chronic stress, 60 days; morning serum cortisol fell 28% vs 8% on placebo)

## Chapter 13 — Your Sleep Environment

**Can light in the bedroom affect sleep without waking you?**

At room-light levels, yes. In a laboratory study, healthy young adults who slept one night with the overhead light on had a higher heart rate and a shift towards the body's alert state all night compared with sleeping in near-darkness, and spent less time in deep and REM sleep, without any increase in awakenings. They were also less sensitive to insulin the next morning. The light used was ordinary room lighting rather than a faint glow, melatonin was not measurably affected, and the study was small, so the effect of very dim sources such as standby lights is less certain.

Mason IC, Grimaldi D, Reid KJ et al. 2022, *PNAS* 119:e2113290119. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2113290119 (20 adults; one night at 100 lux vs under 3 lux)

**Does the body stop regulating its temperature during REM sleep?**

During REM sleep the brain's control of body temperature is largely suspended, so the body responds less to heat and cold and is more influenced by the room. ## Chapter 14 — Caffeine

**Does caffeine block the brain's sleep-pressure signal?**

Yes. Caffeine works mainly by blocking the brain's adenosine receptors, so the build-up of sleep pressure goes unread rather than cleared; the adenosine itself keeps accumulating. This mechanism is well established. Its effect on sleep can be large and easy to miss: in a controlled study, caffeine taken six hours before bed still shortened sleep by more than an hour, and participants did not notice.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-and-sleep
Drake C, Roehrs T, Shambroom J, Roth T 2013, *J Clin Sleep Med* 9:1195–1200. https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170 (12 healthy sleepers; 400 mg, about 2–3 cups; measured by home sleep monitor)

**Does caffeine wearing off make the afternoon slump worse?**

Partly. When caffeine wears off, the adenosine that built up behind it is felt again, so tiredness can return quickly. Regular users also develop tolerance, and a missed or delayed dose brings withdrawal fatigue and low alertness, which makes the next cup feel necessary. That the slump itself deepens over time is a reasonable reading of these effects rather than something measured directly.

Juliano LM, Griffiths RR 2004, *Psychopharmacology* 176:1–29. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-004-2000-x (review; fatigue and drowsiness are validated withdrawal symptoms, even after stopping 100 mg a day)
James JE, Rogers PJ 2005, *Psychopharmacology* 182:1–8. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-005-0084-6 (much of caffeine's apparent boost is reversal of withdrawal)

## Chapter 15 — Alcohol

**Does alcohol help you fall asleep, and is that real sleep?**

Alcohol does shorten the time it takes to fall asleep. It works as a sedative, though, and the sleep that follows is arranged differently from natural sleep: deep sleep is pulled into the early part of the night, REM is held back, and sleep becomes lighter and more broken later on.

Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549. https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006
Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Med Rev* 80:102030. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030

**Why does alcohol wake you in the early hours?**

As alcohol is cleared during the night, its sedation lifts and the body swings the other way: the second half of the night after drinking brings more awakenings and lighter, broken sleep at every dose studied. Wearable studies also find a higher heart rate and lower heart-rate variability on nights after drinking, a sign the body is not settling into recovery, and this shows from the first hours of sleep rather than only at the rebound. "Metabolic rebound" is a descriptive label rather than a formal term, and when the wake comes depends on how much was drunk and when.

Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549. https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006
Pietilä J et al. 2018, *JMIR Ment Health* 5:e23. https://doi.org/10.2196/mental.9519 (large real-world sample of Finnish employees; heart rate up and heart-rate variability down in the first hours of sleep, dose-dependent)

**Does alcohol cause vivid dreams through REM rebound?**

At higher doses, the REM sleep held back early in the night tends to return later, as the alcohol is cleared, in a fragmented and easily interrupted form. Vivid dreams and nightmares after drinking are commonly reported and fit this pattern, though the link to dream intensity rests mainly on people's own reports rather than direct measurement.

Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549. https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep

**Does food slow how quickly alcohol is absorbed?**

Yes. Food in the stomach slows alcohol's passage into the small intestine, where most of it is absorbed, so blood alcohol rises more slowly and peaks lower than on an empty stomach. It does not reduce the total amount of alcohol the body has to process.

NIAAA, *Alcohol Metabolism*. https://www.niaaa.nih.gov/publications/alcohol-metabolism

## Chapter 16 — Food Timing

**Does digesting a meal work against the body's cooling for sleep?**

Digesting food raises the body's energy use for several hours, a process called diet-induced thermogenesis, and with it body temperature. Because a falling core temperature is part of how sleep begins, a substantial meal close to bed works against it.

Westerterp KR 2004, *Nutr Metab* 1:5. https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-7075-1-5 (review; the rise lasts several hours and is larger after protein; effect on pre-sleep cooling not measured directly)

**Does eating close to bed make sleep worse?**

The evidence points that way. In a randomised trial, young women who ate dinner one hour before bed rather than five had lower sleep efficiency and more time awake and briefly roused during the night. Large surveys link later last meals with poorer sleep, and eating within three hours of bed with more night waking.

Enomoto M et al. 2026, *J Physiol Anthropol* 45:12. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40101-026-00430-0 (13 young women, crossover; dinner 1 h vs 5 h before bed)
Chung N, Bin YS, Cistulli PA, Chow CM 2020, *Int J Environ Res Public Health* 17:2677. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17082677 (793 Sydney university students; cross-sectional)
Yan LM et al. 2024, *PLoS ONE* 19:e0308172. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0308172 (7,023 US adults; cross-sectional)

**Is three hours the right gap between dinner and bed?**

Sleep health guidance recommends allowing two to three hours between the last main meal and bed, and advises against going to bed either hungry or straight after eating; a small, light snack is fine. Three hours sits at the top of that range.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep

**Does hunger raise cortisol?**

Going without food raises cortisol, a clear finding across fasting studies, which is one reason sleep health guidance advises against going to bed hungry.

Nakamura Y et al. 2016, *Stress* 19:30–37. https://doi.org/10.3109/10253890.2015.1121984 (meta-analysis; studies involved fasting for a day or more)
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep

## Chapter 17 — Can't-Sleep Routine

**Does CBT-i make getting out of bed when awake a central recommendation?**

Yes. Stimulus control, a core part of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, tells people to leave the bed when they cannot sleep, do something quiet elsewhere, and return only when sleepy, so the bed is kept for sleep. Repeated over weeks, this rebuilds the link between bed and sleep that nights of lying awake have weakened. Stimulus control is also recommended on its own as a treatment for chronic insomnia.

Edinger JD et al. 2021, *J Clin Sleep Med* 17:255–262. https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.8986 (AASM clinical practice guideline; stimulus control alone: conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence)
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Does lying awake frustrated keep you awake?**

Yes. Insomnia is understood as a state of heightened mental, emotional and physical arousal, and worry and frustration about not sleeping feed that arousal and keep the body alert.

Riemann D et al. 2010, *Sleep Med Rev* 14:19–31. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2009.04.002 (hyperarousal model, including raised stress-hormone activity)
Dressle RJ, Riemann D 2023, *J Sleep Res* 32:e13928. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13928 (updated review)

**What does ACT-i add to the advice to get up?**

Acceptance and commitment therapy for insomnia teaches people to accept being awake rather than fight it, because the struggle to sleep generates more arousal than the wakefulness itself. Reviews find it improves insomnia and sleep quality.

Salari N et al. 2020, *BMC Neurol* 20:300. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-020-01883-1 (systematic review of 19 studies)

**Why do mildly amusing shows work better than gripping ones?**

Predictable, low-stakes viewing gives the brain fewer reward signals to chase than gripping or interactive content. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 8, in the note "Is predictable TV genuinely less activating than interactive media?"

## Chapter 20 — Meditation

**Does regular meditation or slow breathing train the calming system?**

Yes. Regular slow-breathing practice makes the body's calming responses stronger over time: studies of heart-rate-variability training find the reflexes that steady heart rate become more responsive after weeks to months of practice. Mindfulness meditation also improves sleep quality in randomised trials.

Lehrer PM, Gevirtz R 2014, *Front Psychol* 5:756. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00756 (review of heart-rate-variability biofeedback)
Rusch HL et al. 2019, *Ann N Y Acad Sci* 1445:5–16. https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.13996 (meta-analysis of 18 randomised trials, 1,654 people; improved sleep quality compared with matched controls, and performed comparably to established sleep treatments)

**Are poor sleepers stuck in the activated state?**

Largely, yes. Insomnia is understood as a state of heightened arousal. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 17, in the note "Does lying awake frustrated keep you awake?"

**Does slow breathing work through the vagus nerve?**

Yes. The vagus nerve is the main pathway of the body's calming system, and slow breathing, especially a slow exhale, shifts the nervous system towards it, measurable as changes in heart rhythm. Breathing is the one automatic function that can also be taken over deliberately, which makes it the most direct lever on that system. Most of the vagus nerve's fibres carry information from the body up to the brain, so attending to bodily sensations engages it from that side as well.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817
Berthoud HR, Neuhuber WL 2000, *Auton Neurosci* 85:1–17. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1566-0702(00)00215-0 (about four-fifths of vagal fibres carry signals from body to brain)

## Chapter 25 — Sugar and Food Sensitivities

**Does sleep loss reduce willpower?**

Yes. Sleep loss impairs the ability to resist impulses. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 14, in the note "Does sleep loss reduce self-control?"

**Does poor sleep make you hungrier, especially for sweet food?**

Yes. In a controlled study, two nights of short sleep raised the hunger hormone ghrelin, lowered the fullness hormone leptin, and increased appetite, most of all for calorie-dense, carbohydrate-rich foods such as sweets. Ghrelin also acts on the brain's reward system, increasing the pull of food cues.

Spiegel K et al. 2004, *Ann Intern Med* 141:846–850. https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-141-11-200412070-00008 (12 healthy young men; two nights of four hours in bed vs ten; later studies vary on leptin)

**Can a high-sugar diet affect the cortisol system over time?**

There are signs it can. A glucose drink before a stressful task amplifies the cortisol response, people who habitually eat more sugar show a weaker cortisol response to stress, and people with type 2 diabetes have a flatter daily cortisol rhythm with higher cortisol at bedtime. Together these suggest the blood-sugar and cortisol systems shape each other over time.
