### Beat 1 — Line 13: the "safety / safely / safety" cluster

**Issue.** The safety beat fires the same word three times in two sentences: *for **safety*** → *do it **safely*** → ***safety** wins*. The first and third form a deliberate-feeling bookend, and "safety wins" is a strong closing beat. The middle "safely" is the weak one — it sits only a few words from "for safety," so it reads as accidental echo rather than design, and "do it safely" is vaguer than the rest of the sentence. Cutting it tightens the line and sharpens the intended bookend. (Raised by the author in chat.)

**Fix** (before / amended / after):

The standard advice on napping is reasonable. Keep it short. Don't nap within eight hours of your bedtime. And if you genuinely need one for safety — if you're about to get behind the wheel and you're not sure you can do it ~~safely~~ — then nap. That's not a habit question, that's a judgement call, and safety wins.

*Following paragraph (unchanged):* The reason comes down to adenosine — the sleep pressure that's been building since you woke up...

*Self-check:* removing "safely" leaves "not sure you can do it — then nap," which is clean, introduces no new repetition, and creates no abrupt join. Passes.

### Beat 2 — Lines 37 & 39 re-explain the mechanism already set out in Beat 15

**Issue.** The adenosine/Sleep-Executives mechanism is fully explained at line 15. The two closing outcome beats then re-justify themselves with the *same* mechanism, and partly with each other:

- 15: "...you arrive at your sleep window with the pressure already partially reduced. **The Sleep Executives don't have enough to work with.**"
- 37: "...won't hurt your sleep that night... 15 minutes won't meaningfully deplete adenosine... **The Sleep Executives still have what they need.**"
- 39: "...No adenosine spent. No risk to the night."

That's the mechanism on four passes (seeded at 11, explained at 15, restated at 37, restated again at 39). The eight-hours callback in 37 *does* earn its place — it pre-empts the reader's worry that a nap breaks the rule stated in 13. But the final "Sleep Executives" sentence in 37 simply restates 15, and "No adenosine spent. No risk to the night" in 39 covers the same ground a third time.

**Fix** (trim the restatement in 37; keep the eight-hours callback which does new work):

*Preceding paragraph (unchanged):* The intention is simple. Lie down wherever you are — couch, bed, floor — set a fifteen-minute alarm, and do the body scan...

If you drift off and sleep, the alarm catches you at fifteen minutes - a safety net there to stop you overshooting. A nap that short won't hurt your sleep that night, and it will typically be enough. 15 minutes won't meaningfully deplete adenosine — the usual eight-hours-before-bed rule doesn't apply here. ~~The Sleep Executives still have what they need.~~

If you don't sleep — even better. You've finished the meditation, you get up quieter than when you lay down, and you've added another calming system session to the day. ~~No adenosine spent. No risk to the night.~~

*Self-check:* 37 still ends on a substantive sentence (the eight-hours rule), so no abrupt drop — no replacement needed there. No new repetition introduced. Passes.

**Recommended replacement close for 39** (it's the chapter's final sentence, so it wants a landing rather than trailing off after the cut):

If you don't sleep — even better. You've finished the meditation, you get up quieter than when you lay down, and you've added another calming system session to the day. ~~No adenosine spent. No risk to the night.~~ **Nothing borrowed. Nothing owed.**

Rationale: callback to the debt metaphor from line 11 ("borrowing against a debt"), bookending the chapter — deliberate recurrence per style notes, no mechanism jargon. Runner-up if a direct contrast is preferred: **"A nap takes from the night. This doesn't."** Both are stacked fragments in the author's voice; "borrowed/owed" is a deliberate bookend, not accidental repetition.
