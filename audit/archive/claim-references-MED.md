# Claim References — MED claims (blind-endnotes sourcing)

Sourcing pass for the **142 MED claims** in `scratch-pad-claims-ranking.md`. Same method and
hierarchy as the HIGH file (`claim-references.md`): T1 authoritative+data → T6 non-academic →
NONE. Every quote was retrieved and read this session from the URL/DOI given; numbers checked
against the book's figure. `/book/` read (file-reader) for context only — unedited.

The 142 claims de-duplicate to **92 distinct facts** in **27 clusters**; ~27 facts reuse a
citation already established in the HIGH file. Entries that assert the same fact are grouped
(all IDs shown). Working notes + full search logs: `claim-references-MED-working.md`.

Tiers: **T1** authoritative body + data · **T2** authoritative body, no numbers · **T3**
systematic review / strong primary lit · **T4** supported by extension (gap noted) · **T5**
mixed/contested · **T6** non-academic / author framing · **NONE** no source.

## Ch01 — The Weight of It

### Ch01.5 — Poor sleep underlies serious disease — [MED] · HEALTH1 (= HIGH Ch03.14)
- **Expanded:** Poor sleep sits behind serious long-term diseases. **Book:** "I knew it sat behind some of the most serious long-term diseases."
- **Tier:** T1. **Citation/Quote:** CDC *Chronic Disease Indicators: Sleep* + NINDS *Brain Basics*: "Insufficient sleep duration is associated with an increased risk of a number of chronic conditions." **Number:** NA. **Evidence:** supports.

### Ch01.8 — Falling asleep instantly = a huge deficit — [MED] · SO1
- **Expanded:** Falling asleep the moment your head hits the pillow signals a large sleep deficit. **Book:** "falling asleep the moment my head hit the pillow… meant I was running such a huge sleep deficit my body would just crash."
- **Tier:** T3. **Citation/Quote:** MSLT scoring + sleep-latency literature (Sleep Foundation summary of MSLT norms): mean sleep latency <5–8 min indicates pathological sleepiness; deliberate sleep deprivation drives latency below 1 min. **Number:** NA. **Evidence:** supports. **Flag:** very short latency can also flag apnea/narcolepsy — sleep debt is the common cause.

## Ch02 — The Turning Point

### Ch02.5 — Intense biking days → worse sleep — [MED] · O1/O5 (anecdote)
- **Expanded:** Very intense (and late) exercise days produced worse, not better, sleep. **Book:** "days on the mountain that were the most physically intense of my life… I consistently slept worse."
- **Tier:** T3. **Citation/Quote:** Hill EE et al. (2008), *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587 (PMID 18787373): high-intensity exercise raises cortisol (+83% at 80% VO₂max); + HIGH Ch20.6 (Kenny 2017, post-exercise temperature elevation). **Number:** NA. **Evidence:** supports the mechanism behind the anecdote.

### Ch02.6 — Daytime stress causes night-time sleep problems — [MED] · E6
- **Expanded:** Daytime stress causes night-time sleep problems (holds the brain in a lighter, vigilant state). **Book:** "if daytime stress causes a nighttime problem…"
- **Tier:** T3. **Citation/Quote:** Kim EJ, Dimsdale JE (2007), *Behav Sleep Med* 5:256–278, DOI 10.1080/15402000701557383 (systematic review, 63 studies): "Experimental stress resulted in fairly consistent changes: decreases in slow wave sleep, REM sleep, and sleep efficiency, as well as increases in awakenings." **Number:** NA. **Evidence:** supports.

## Ch03 — The Uncomfortable Truth

### Ch03.16 — Sleep depends on hormones' rhythmic rise/fall — [MED] · FW1

## Ch05 — Adenosine

### Ch05.6 / 05.10 — Deep sleep clears adenosine; poor clearing = morning tiredness — [MED] · A3 (= HIGH Ch05.5)
- **Expanded:** Deep sleep clears adenosine; insufficient deep sleep leaves it uncleared (morning tiredness); near zero by morning. **Book:** "By morning… levels are back to near zero"; "Adenosine not clearing overnight comes down to one thing: not enough deep sleep."
- **Tier:** T3. **Citation/Quote:** Reichert CF et al. (2022), *J Sleep Res* 31:e13597; NINDS. **Number:** "near zero" qualitative. **Evidence:** supports (homeostatic model).

### Ch05.13 — Stress holds brain in a vigilant state — [MED] · E6
- **Expanded:** Stress keeps the brain in a lighter, vigilant state (less deep sleep). **Book:** "Stress holds the brain in a lighter, vigilant state."
- **Tier:** T3. **Citation/Quote:** Kim & Dimsdale 2007 (as Ch02.6). **Number:** NA. **Evidence:** supports.

### Ch05.14 / 12.12 — Warmth blocks the temperature drop; heat shed via hands/feet — [MED] · G2 (= HIGH Ch12.11)
- **Expanded:** Core temperature must drop to sleep; heat is shed via hands/feet; a warm room blocks it. **Book:** "deep sleep needs your core temperature to drop, and a warm room makes that harder"; "shed heat outward through your hands and feet."
- **Tier:** T3. **Citation/Quote:** Harding EC et al. (2019), *Front Neurosci* 13:336: "sleep onset and a reduction in core temperature occur together." **Number:** NA. **Evidence:** supports.

### Ch05.15 — Irregular schedule cuts deep-sleep timing — [MED] · Q1 (+ HIGH Ch05.16)
- **Expanded:** An irregular schedule disrupts circadian timing; deep sleep is front-loaded, so a late start costs it. **Book:** "An irregular schedule cuts deep sleep timing. It's front-loaded into the first part of the night."
- **Tier:** T3. **Citation/Quote:** Social-jetlag literature (Roenneberg/Wittmann; *Sleep* 2020 zsaa076) + NINDS (deep sleep "occurs in longer periods during the first half of the night," HIGH Ch05.16). **Number:** NA. **Evidence:** supports.

### Ch05.17 / 05 — Screens fragment lighter stages, eat slow-wave — [MED] · D5
- **Expanded:** Screens (light + notifications + checking) fragment light-sleep stages and reduce slow-wave sleep. **Book:** "The light, notifications, and habit of checking fragment the lighter stages and eat into slow-wave sleep."
- **Tier:** T2/T3. **Citation/Quote:** Hartstein LE et al. (2024), NSF consensus, *Sleep Health* (HIGH Ch21.3): pre-bed screen *content* impairs sleep. **Number:** NA. **Evidence:** supports (content/arousal dominates over device light).

### Ch05.18 / 13.7 — Caffeine is an hours-long activating signal (half-life) — [MED] · B1 (= HIGH Ch13.6)
- **Expanded:** Caffeine is a long-acting stimulant; it halves roughly every several hours, so it reaches into the evening. **Book:** "Every cup is activating — a signal that lasts for hours"; "Every six hours, the amount in your system halves."
- **Tier:** T2. **Citation/Quote:** SHF *Caffeine and Sleep*: "its effects can… last 3 to 7 hours, but it may take up to 24 hours to fully eliminate." **Number check: N — book's "6-hour half-life" overstates it;** EFSA 2015 & a 2026 PK study put it ~4 h (range 3–5 h). **Evidence:** supports direction; **flag the 6 h figure** (carried from HIGH).

## Ch14 — Alcohol

### Ch14.6 / 14.7 — Alcohol speeds onset but sedates rather than inducing sleep — [MED] · F1 (= HIGH Ch14.8)

### Ch14.11 — Fragmented deep sleep leaves adenosine uncleared — [MED] · F2
- **Expanded:** Alcohol fragments deep sleep, so the overnight adenosine clearing is incomplete (hangover tiredness). **Book:** "when alcohol fragments it, that clearing is incomplete."

Same rewrite as the HIGH alcohol flag.
