## Chapter 1 — The Weight of It

### Is poor sleep linked to the most serious long-term diseases?

Insufficient sleep is associated with increased risk of heart disease, diabetes, high blood pressure, stroke, depression, and obesity. The association is recognised by major public-health bodies including the CDC and NINDS.

CDC, *Chronic Disease Indicators: Sleep* (2024). https://www.cdc.gov/cdi/indicator-definitions/sleep.html
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does falling asleep instantly signal a large sleep deficit?

A mean sleep latency below 5–8 minutes on the Multiple Sleep Latency Test is classed as pathological sleepiness; deliberate sleep deprivation drives it below one minute. Falling asleep the moment one's head hits the pillow is a reliable marker of significant sleep debt, though very short latency can also flag conditions such as sleep apnoea or narcolepsy.

Sleep Foundation, *Sleep Latency* (MSLT norms summary). https://www.sleepfoundation.org/how-sleep-works/sleep-latency

## Chapter 2 — The Turning Point

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Can intense exercise produce worse sleep rather than better?

High-intensity exercise raises cortisol substantially (by approximately 83% at 80% of VO₂max) and produces significant post-exercise elevation in core body temperature, both of which can disrupt sleep. The exercise-intensity and timing interaction explains why very hard training days — especially late in the day — can worsen rather than improve sleep quality.

Hill EE et al. 2008, *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18787373/
Kenny GP, McGinn R 2017, *J Appl Physiol* 122:933–944 — post-exercise thermoregulation. https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00517.2016

### Does daytime stress cause night-time sleep problems?

Experimental stress consistently reduces slow-wave sleep, REM sleep, and sleep efficiency while increasing awakenings. A systematic review across 63 studies confirms that daytime stress holds the brain in a lighter, more vigilant state at night.

Kim EJ, Dimsdale JE 2007, *Behav Sleep Med* 5:256–278. https://doi.org/10.1080/15402000701557383

## Chapter 3 — The Uncomfortable Truth

### Is poor sleep really linked to serious disease?

Insufficient sleep is associated with increased risk of heart disease, diabetes, high blood pressure, stroke, depression, and obesity. The association is recognised by CDC and NINDS (neither states long-term cognitive decline; checked 2026-10-14). Persistent short sleep (≤6 h) in midlife is linked to ~30% higher later dementia risk (Sabia 2021; observational).

CDC, *Chronic Disease Indicators: Sleep* (2024). https://www.cdc.gov/cdi/indicator-definitions/sleep.html
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Sabia S et al. 2021, *Nat Commun* 12:2289. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-22354-2

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Is CBN a sleep-targeted cannabinoid formed from THC?

Cannabinol (CBN) is a mildly psychoactive cannabinoid formed from the degradation of THC. The claim that it is "more targeted for sleep than CBD" is unproven; there is scant objective scientific evidence supporting CBN's use as a sedative. The book's framing of this as a forum claim it doubts is well-calibrated to the evidence.

da Silva SHN et al. 2025, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* — meta-analysis; cannabinoid benefit came mainly from THC-type compounds; no significant effect for CBD alone, and the only CBN human trial showed only a small reduction in night-time waking. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2025.102156
Bonn-Miller MO et al. 2024, *Exp Clin Psychopharmacol* — human CBD trial; no significant sleep benefit for CBD alone. https://doi.org/10.1037/pha0000682
Saleska JL et al. 2024, *J Am Nutr Assoc* — the only published human CBN trial; small reduction in night-time waking only. https://doi.org/10.1080/27697061.2023.2203221

### Can only a very small fraction of people genuinely function on little sleep?

Natural short-sleep ability is rare. Variants enabling it (DEC2, ADRB1) have each been identified in single families; no reliable population frequency exists (the circulating "1 in 1,000" / "1–3%" figures trace to news coverage, not research — dropped 2026-10-14). Chronic restriction to 6 h impairs performance progressively while subjective sleepiness plateaus, so people underestimate their deficit (Van Dongen 2003).

He Y et al. 2009, *Science* 325:866–870 — DEC2 variant. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1174443
Shi G et al. 2019, *Neuron* 103:1044–1055 — ADRB1 variant. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.07.026
Van Dongen HPA et al. 2003, *Sleep* 26:117–126. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117

### Does sleep depend on hormones rising and falling in a predictable rhythm?

Sleep depends on multiple physiological systems, including melatonin and cortisol, whose circadian rhythms must rise and fall in a predictable 24-hour pattern. When those rhythms are disrupted — by irregular schedules, light exposure, or stress — the conditions for sleep degrade.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

### Is driving tired as dangerous as driving drunk?

Being awake for 17 hours produces impairment comparable to a blood-alcohol level of 0.05%; being awake for 24 hours is comparable to approximately 0.10%, which exceeds the legal driving limit in most jurisdictions. The equivalence between fatigue and alcohol impairment is well established in the research.

CDC/NIOSH, *Training for Nurses on Shift Work & Long Work Hours*, Module 3 (2020). https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod3/08.html

## Chapter 4 — Sleep Incorporated

### Does adenosine build sleep pressure from the moment you wake?

Adenosine accumulates as a byproduct of neural activity from waking onward, building "sleep pressure" across the day. The NINDS describes adenosine release from cells as a key mechanism that makes a person feel sleepy.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31:e13597. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13597

### Is melatonin a timing signal rather than a sedative?

Melatonin is a hormonal timing signal that enables sleep by signalling darkness to the body; it does not directly force or sedate. The Sleep Health Foundation and NINDS both describe melatonin as a darkness/night signal, not a sleep-inducing drug.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

### Is cortisol the body's daytime alertness and wake-driving hormone?

Cortisol follows a characteristic diurnal rhythm, rising through the late night and morning, peaking post-waking, and declining across the day. It is the primary hormonal driver of alertness and waking energy.

Fries E, Dettenborn L, Kirschbaum C 2009, *Int J Psychophysiol* 72:67–73. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2008.03.014
Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does dopamine signal reward and drive motivation?

Dopamine encodes incentive salience — the motivational "wanting" or "worth pursuing" signal — rather than pleasure itself. It responds to opportunity and anticipation, driving approach behaviour. The precise framing that the nervous system cannot distinguish a productive urge from a threat-driven one is an extension of the cortisol-arousal literature rather than a directly measured contrast.

Berridge KC 2007, *Psychopharmacology* 191:391–431 — incentive salience / "wanting." https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6722949

### Does CBT-i treat the root of insomnia more effectively than sleeping pills?

The American College of Physicians recommends CBT-i as the initial treatment for all adult patients with chronic insomnia, with pharmacotherapy only as a short-term adjunct. Sleeping pills address the symptom rather than the behavioural and cognitive factors that maintain insomnia.

Qaseem A et al. 2016, *Ann Intern Med* 165:125–133. https://doi.org/10.7326/M15-2175

### Are adenosine and dopamine technically hormones?

Adenosine is classified as an endogenous sleep-regulatory substance and neuromodulator, not a hormone. Dopamine acts mainly as a neurotransmitter; in the motivation/reward role the book describes it works as a broad neuromodulator (diffuse projections setting overall brain state), which is the hormone-like function that justifies the book's shorthand. Calling both "hormones" is a simplification, conceded in Ch04 footnote 1 (proposal: audit/ch04-hormone-simplification-proposed.md, 2026-10-14). Dopamine-specific source still to add.

Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31:e13597. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13597

### Does menopause sharply disrupt sleep?

Sleep complaints become more common through perimenopause and menopause. Falling oestrogen and progesterone in perimenopause and menopause are associated with increased sleep disturbance, supported by systematic review and meta-analysis.

Cintron D et al. 2017, *Endocrine* 55:702–711. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12020-016-1072-9

### Can HRT restore the conditions for sleep in menopause?

Menopausal hormone therapy improves sleep quality in women who have vasomotor symptoms at baseline (standardised mean difference −0.54, moderate-quality evidence); no benefit is seen when women without vasomotor symptoms are analysed separately. This aligns with the framing that HRT helps most when menopause is the primary driver of disruption.

Cintron D et al. 2017, *Endocrine* 55:702–711. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12020-016-1072-9
NAMS 2022 Hormone Therapy Position Statement, *Menopause* 29:767–794. https://doi.org/10.1097/GME.0000000000002028

## Chapter 5 — Adenosine

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does deep sleep clear adenosine overnight, and how much lifts by morning?

Adenosine is a widely accepted endogenous sleep-regulatory substance that accumulates during waking and dissipates during sleep. The clearance is associated with the homeostatic sleep drive, with slow-wave sleep the phase most closely linked to this process. By morning the bulk of the day's accumulated sleep pressure has cleared — the "largely lifted" framing is accurate — but clearance is gradual and progressive rather than a full overnight reset to a fixed baseline. The robust signal in the literature is the slow-wave-activity curve, which tracks how much pressure has been discharged across the night.

Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31:e13597. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13597
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

### Does poor overnight adenosine clearance explain morning tiredness?

See **Chapter 5: Does deep sleep clear adenosine overnight to near zero?** — insufficient deep sleep leaves adenosine insufficiently cleared, producing the tiredness that persists into the morning.

### Does stress hold the brain in a vigilant, lighter-sleep state?

Experimental stress consistently reduces slow-wave sleep, REM sleep, and sleep efficiency while increasing awakenings. Stress maintains a state of physiological hyperarousal that is incompatible with deep, restorative sleep.

Kim EJ, Dimsdale JE 2007, *Behav Sleep Med* 5:256–278. https://doi.org/10.1080/15402000701557383

### Does warmth block the temperature drop needed for deep sleep?

Core temperature must decline for sleep onset and maintenance to occur. A warm room impairs this cooling process, with heat shed via peripheral vasodilation in the hands and feet. Elevated ambient temperature increases wakefulness and reduces both slow-wave and REM sleep.

Harding EC, Franks NP, Wisden W 2019, *Front Neurosci* 13:336. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.00336
Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *J Physiol Anthropol* 31:14. https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does an irregular sleep schedule cut deep-sleep time?

Deep sleep is front-loaded into the first half of the night. An irregular schedule shifts circadian timing, so a later sleep onset reduces the window available for slow-wave sleep. Social jetlag literature and NINDS sleep-stage data both support this mechanism.

Roenneberg T et al. 2012, *Curr Biol* 22:939–943 — social jetlag. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2012.03.038
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

### Do screens and notifications fragment light sleep and reduce slow-wave sleep?

See **Chapter 23: Does screen content matter more than screen light for delaying sleep?** — screen content (arousal, notifications, checking behaviour) fragments lighter sleep stages and eats into slow-wave sleep.

### Is caffeine a long-lasting activating signal with a multi-hour half-life?

See **Chapter 14: What is caffeine's half-life?** — caffeine blocks adenosine receptors and has a half-life of approximately four to five hours, meaning a cup taken in the afternoon reaches well into the evening.

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Why is alcohol absent from the deep sleep disruptors list in Chapter 5?

Alcohol is absent from Chapter 5's deep sleep disruptors list because its effect on slow-wave sleep runs counter to the typical disruption pattern. Ebrahim et al. (2013) found that alcohol increases early-night slow-wave sleep at most doses; Gardiner et al. (2024) found only a reduced latency to deep sleep at high doses, not suppression of its amount. The real cost of alcohol is REM suppression and second-half fragmentation, covered in Chapter 15. Including alcohol in a "things that reduce deep sleep" list would have been inaccurate.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030
Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549. https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep

### Is deep sleep front-loaded into the first part of the night?

Stage 3 non-REM (deep) sleep occurs in longer periods during the first half of the night. Starting sleep later reduces the total amount available, as the window for deep sleep is not evenly distributed across the night.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

## Chapter 6 — Melatonin

### Does melatonin rise as evening light drops?

Melatonin secretion is gated by darkness; as evening light levels fall the brain begins releasing it. Both NINDS and the Sleep Health Foundation confirm that light exposure — particularly bright light — suppresses or delays melatonin production.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin

### Does melatonin lower core temperature and alertness as well as signalling night?

Melatonin decreases core body temperature (approximately 0.2–0.4°C) and produces decreased subjective alertness as part of its sleep-preparatory role — it does not merely signal darkness but actively shifts the body toward a sleep-ready state.

Cagnacci A et al. 1997, *J Biol Rhythms* 12:509. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/074873049701200604
Lok R et al. 2019, *J Pineal Res* 67:e12583 — daytime melatonin and light independently affect alertness and body temperature. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jpi.12583

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### How does the sleep window line up with melatonin?

In the hours before habitual bedtime the body clock holds alertness up, a stretch researchers call the wake maintenance zone, when falling asleep is hardest. Melatonin starts to rise about two hours before habitual sleep onset, and the switch from that alert stretch to strong sleepiness, often called the sleep gate, closely tracks the start of the rise. Melatonin then climbs steeply for the next few hours, so habitual bedtime usually falls within its fastest climb, before it peaks in the middle of the night. The link between melatonin and the opening of the gate is an association: melatonin is proposed, not proven, to be what opens it, and no study has measured a short window at the steepest part of the rise.

Sletten TL et al. 2010, *Front Neurol* 1:137. https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2010.00137 (28 young adults on an early or late sleep schedule for at least 3 weeks; salivary melatonin onset about 2 hours before sleep onset in both groups, with wide individual variation)
Shochat T, Luboshitzky R, Lavie P 1997, *Am J Physiol* 273(1):R364–R370. https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.1997.273.1.R364 (6 healthy men given a 7-minute chance to sleep every 20 minutes for 29 hours; the night-time melatonin rise and the opening of the sleep gate kept a consistent timing; abstract only)
Lavie P 1997, *J Biol Rhythms* 12(6):657–665. https://doi.org/10.1177/074873049701200622 (review; proposes that melatonin opens the evening sleep gate by damping the brain's wakefulness system; abstract only)
Rajaratnam SMW et al. 2004, *J Physiol* 561(Pt 1):339–351. https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2004.073742 (8 healthy men given 16-hour sleep opportunities starting in the afternoon; 1.5 mg melatonin added about 2 hours of sleep in the first 8 hours; the introduction describes the evening 'wake maintenance zone' and the abrupt switch to high sleepiness as melatonin rises)
Zeitzer JM et al. 2007, *Sleep* 30(11):1437–1443. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/30.11.1437 (hourly-averaged plasma melatonin in 90 young men: a steep rise across the hours around lights out, peaking in the middle of the night)

### Why does missing the window keep you awake?

Lying awake gives worry room to work, and worry delays sleep. People who kept going over a stressful task took longer to fall asleep that night, and watching the clock while trying to sleep raised worry and lengthened the time taken to drop off. Leading accounts of insomnia describe a loop in which worry about sleep and about the day drives arousal that keeps the person awake, and people with insomnia carry raised arousal into the evening, including in the stress-hormone system. Treatment guidance advises getting up when sleep does not come rather than lying in bed awake. The pull to sleep itself does not fade after the window: once the sleep gate opens around the start of the melatonin rise, sleepiness stays high and sleep pressure keeps building. That the window seems to close because a thought takes hold and brings in the slower stress system is the book's reasoning, built from these findings.

Zoccola PM, Dickerson SS, Lam S 2009, *Psychosom Med* 71(7):771–775. https://doi.org/10.1097/PSY.0b013e3181ae58e8 (70 participants gave a stressful speech in the afternoon; those who ruminated more took longer to fall asleep that night, measured by wrist actigraph; no link with total sleep or night waking; abstract only)
Tang NKY, Schmidt DA, Harvey AG 2007, *J Behav Ther Exp Psychiatry* 38(1):40–55. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbtep.2005.07.004 (two experiments, 60 good and poor sleepers, then 38 people with insomnia; watching a clock while trying to sleep raised pre-sleep worry and lengthened the time taken to fall asleep; abstract only)
Harvey AG 2002, *Behav Res Ther* 40(8):869–893. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0005-7967(01)00061-4 (theoretical model with a review of early supporting evidence; worry about sleep drives arousal and distress, which keep the person awake; abstract only)
Riemann D et al. 2010, *Sleep Med Rev* 14:19–31. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2009.04.002 (hyperarousal model of insomnia)
Dressle RJ et al. 2022, *Sleep Med Rev* 62:101588. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2022.101588 (meta-analysis of 20 case-control studies, 449 people with insomnia and 357 good sleepers; moderately higher cortisol, larger in blood samples; abstract only)
Dressle RJ, Riemann D 2023, *J Sleep Res* 32:e13928. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13928 (updated review of hyperarousal in insomnia)
Edinger JD et al. 2021, *J Clin Sleep Med* 17:255–262. https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.8986 (AASM clinical practice guideline; stimulus control, including leaving the bed when unable to sleep: conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence)
Rajaratnam SMW et al. 2004, *J Physiol* 561(Pt 1):339–351. https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2004.073742 (8 healthy men; describes the abrupt evening switch to high sleepiness as melatonin rises)

### Is nocturnal melatonin blunted in people with chronic insomnia?

Several small studies have found that people with chronic primary insomnia — particularly those who struggle to *stay* asleep — produce less melatonin through the middle of the night than good sleepers, with a lower overnight peak. The evidence is suggestive rather than settled: the studies are small, partly age-mismatched, and the finding is not uniform across the literature; even researchers who reported it have called its causal significance unsettled. It is also unclear whether a lower melatonin level drives the disturbed sleep or simply accompanies it. At the conceptual level the recent circadian-insomnia literature is more supportive: a 2023 review assigns sleep-maintenance insomnia specifically to a "blunted amplitude" of the circadian rhythm, and a 2025 study points to amplitude — rather than melatonin *timing*, which it found was not associated with sleep continuity — as the maintenance-relevant marker. Even so, no study has tied a reduced overnight amplitude to a specific mid-night awakening event, so the link to the 2–3 a.m. wake remains an inference. The reduction is best read as one contributing factor among several, not an established deficiency that explains insomnia on its own. (Ch06, since revised, now makes this distinction itself: it presents melatonin as able to fall short in two directions — arriving too late or too scattered to open the sleep window, or running too low overnight to hold it — and explicitly separates the author's own case, where the signal was intact and the problem was timing and arousal, from other poor sleepers in whom "the overnight signal really does run low." The earlier tension with a flat "not a deficiency" framing is therefore largely resolved at the chapter level. Note too that prolonged-release melatonin trials found low-melatonin excretors did *not* respond better on sleep latency — age predicted response — though they did show a small subjective total-sleep-time gain, which complicates a simple deficiency-replacement reading.)

Hajak G et al. 1995, *J Pineal Res* 19:116–122 — overnight plasma melatonin in chronic primary insomnia; mid-night peak blunted (~82 vs ~117 pg/ml in controls), deficit greatest where insomnia had lasted >5 years; small sample (~10), sleep-maintenance phenotype. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079x.1995.tb00179.x
Riemann D et al. 2002, *Psychiatry Res* 113:17–27 — nocturnal melatonin significantly diminished in primary insomnia; authors described its pathophysiological significance as "a matter of debate"; small sample. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0165-1781(02)00249-4
Léger D, Laudon M, Zisapel N 2004, *Am J Med* 116:91–95 — lower nocturnal 6-sulfatoxymelatonin excretion in older insomnia patients vs age-matched controls; ~⅓ "low excretors" responded better to melatonin replacement. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2003.07.017
Lack LC, Micic G, Lovato N 2023, *J Sleep Res* 32:e13976 — review; assigns sleep-maintenance insomnia to a "blunted amplitude of circadian rhythm" (review-level synthesis, not new primary data). https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13976
Scott H et al. 2025, *J Pineal Res* 77:e70069 — in people with insomnia, melatonin *timing* (DLMO) related to sleep schedule but not to sleep continuity/WASO, nominating amplitude as the maintenance-relevant marker. https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.70069

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does slow-release melatonin help you stay asleep?

Slow-release (prolonged-release) melatonin is formulated to trickle out over roughly eight to ten hours rather than arrive in a single early pulse, the idea being to sustain the overnight signal and so help maintain sleep; the 2 mg form is licensed in Europe for short-term primary insomnia in adults aged 55 and over. The trial evidence is modest and comes with caveats: the studies are relatively few, concentrated in older adults, rely largely on self-reported sleep rather than objective recording, and much of the work originates from research groups affiliated with the manufacturer. The trials also focused mainly on how quickly people fell asleep rather than on staying asleep, and the largest study found that low natural melatonin levels did *not* predict a better response — age did — which weakens the simple "replace the missing melatonin" rationale. Where a gain in total sleep time appeared it was small: on the order of thirteen minutes versus placebo, by self-report, in the subgroup most expected to benefit. Broader meta-analysis of melatonin (mostly the immediate-release form) similarly finds only small reductions in time to fall asleep and small increases in total sleep.

Lemoine P et al. 2007, *J Sleep Res* 16:372–380 — prolonged-release melatonin 2 mg vs placebo, 3 weeks, n=170 aged ≥55; improved self-rated sleep quality and morning alertness, no rebound or withdrawal. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2007.00613.x
Wade AG et al. 2010, *BMC Medicine* 8:51 — PR melatonin 2 mg up to 6 months, n=791 (analysed 746), aged 18–80, primary insomnia; sleep latency reduced in the elderly (−19.1 vs −1.7 min, P=0.002), total sleep time +13.1 min in low excretors (self-reported, P=0.035); low melatonin level did not predict response (age did); effects maintained without tolerance; manufacturer-affiliated (Neurim). https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2933606/
Ferracioli-Oda E et al. 2013, *PLoS ONE* 8:e63773 — meta-analysis, 19 trials, 1,683 subjects (mostly immediate-release): melatonin modestly shortens sleep latency, increases total sleep time, and improves sleep quality. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0063773

### Does an irregular sleep schedule misplace the melatonin rhythm?

Irregular sleep and wake times remove the consistent light-dark cue the melatonin rhythm uses as a reference point. Without it, the rise can occur at the wrong time or flatten, explaining why irregular schedulers often feel neither tired at the right time nor alert in the morning.

Roenneberg T et al. 2012, *Curr Biol* 22:939–943 — social jetlag. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2012.03.038

### Does cortisol and melatonin work in opposition across the 24-hour cycle?

Cortisol and melatonin oppose each other across the day-night cycle: cortisol peaks in the morning and declines through the day while melatonin is suppressed by light; by evening, as cortisol falls, melatonin begins to rise. Hyperarousal — elevated cortisol — can override the rising melatonin signal and delay the sleep window.

Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214

### Does morning light plus a consistent bedtime set the circadian clock?

Light entering the eyes in the first 30–60 minutes after waking is the strongest single environmental input for setting the circadian clock. A consistent wake time paired with morning light exposure establishes the reference that sequences every subsequent rhythm, including the evening melatonin rise.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

### Does timing matter more than dose for melatonin?

Low doses of melatonin (about 0.5–5 mg) appear to work about as well as higher ones, and taking more adds little. Higher doses mainly keep blood levels raised for longer, which can carry into the morning. For shifting the body clock, when the dose is taken matters: taken at the wrong time it can delay adjustment. The earlier idea that the body responds to the rate of the evening rise rather than its level has no support and was dropped (N4, 2026-10-19); the book's Ch6 dosage line was reworded to timing over amount (decision 4.4).

Herxheimer A, Petrie KJ 2002, *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* (2):CD001520. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001520 (Cochrane review of 10 trials; 9 found less jet lag when taken close to target bedtime after crossing five or more time zones; 0.5–5 mg similarly effective, with faster sleep after 5 mg; no gain above 5 mg; taken at the wrong time it can delay adjustment)
Gooneratne NS et al. 2012, *J Pineal Res* 52(4):437–445. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079X.2011.00958.x (27 older US adults; 4 mg kept melatonin raised for about 10 h vs 6.4 h for 0.4 mg, which the authors say could last into the daytime; next-day sleepiness not measured)
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin

### Does evening light suppress melatonin?

Bright light in the evening, particularly short-wavelength light from screens, delays or suppresses melatonin production. The Sleep Health Foundation confirms that melatonin production is normally reduced by bright light exposure.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin

### Is melatonin genuinely useful for jet lag and shift work?

Melatonin is effective for resetting the circadian clock when it is out of synchrony with local time, as in jet lag or shift work. This use — shifting the clock — is distinct from taking melatonin as a direct sleep aid, and is endorsed by sleep-health bodies and supported by AASM circadian practice guidelines.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin

### What dose and timing work for circadian shifting with melatonin?

For circadian adjustment, daily doses of 0.5–5 mg appear similarly effective, with no advantage to higher doses. The Sleep Health Foundation recommends taking it about one hour before the target bedtime. A dose of 0.5–1 mg taken 30–60 minutes before the new target bedtime is consistent with this guidance.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin

### Do typical over-the-counter melatonin doses exceed natural production?

Most over-the-counter melatonin runs at doses far higher than what the body naturally produces each night. The Sleep Health Foundation confirms that low doses (0.5–5 mg) are as effective as higher ones, and that there is no advantage to taking more. The direction — that common OTC doses are supraphysiological — is well supported.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin

<!-- endnote -->
### Does a high melatonin dose extend into morning and blunt sensitivity?

Taking a high dose does not increase the sleep-inducing effect; it extends the active period of melatonin into the following morning, which can produce grogginess, and over time may blunt sensitivity to the body's own melatonin signal. "More isn't more" is supported by the Sleep Health Foundation. The desensitisation mechanism is backed by receptor research (animal/cell-line model caveat applies). Higher-dose melatonin is associated with dose-related drowsiness in systematic review.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin
Menczel Schrire Z et al. 2022, *J Pineal Res* 72(2):e12782. https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.12782
Gerdin MJ et al. 2004, *FASEB J* 18(14):1646–1656 — MT2 receptor desensitisation (rat/cell-line model). https://doi.org/10.1096/fj.03-1339com

## Chapter 7 — Cortisol

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does cortisol begin rising before you wake up?

Cortisol follows a distinct circadian rhythm, rising through the late night and early morning hours as part of the normal diurnal cycle, so it is already elevated before waking. The claim that cortisol is "already rising before you open your eyes" is directionally correct for this circadian component, which accounts for the gradual pre-waking increase. The cortisol awakening response (CAR) is a distinct post-waking phenomenon — an additional surge peaking roughly 30–45 minutes after waking, superimposed on the overnight rise. The CAR is the sharper peak that follows.

Fries E, Dettenborn L, Kirschbaum C 2009, *Int J Psychophysiol* 72:67–73. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2008.03.014

### Does cortisol peak in the first hour after waking?

Cortisol peaks in the first hour or so after waking and then declines across the day in a characteristic diurnal curve. This post-waking surge (the cortisol awakening response) superimposed on the overnight circadian rise is well documented.

Fries E, Dettenborn L, Kirschbaum C 2009, *Int J Psychophysiol* 72:67–73. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2008.03.014

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
<!-- added 2026-10-19, N5 (Ben); sits with the new Ch7 paragraphs replacing line 25 -->
### Is fight or flight cortisol or adrenaline?

The body's stress response has two arms. A fast one, driven by adrenaline and noradrenaline, produces the immediate jolt of alertness and is short-lived. A slower one, a chain of signals from the brain that ends in cortisol, builds more gradually and can last much longer. Not every stressor brings in the slower arm to the same degree: in laboratory studies, cortisol rose most for tasks that felt out of the person's control or carried a risk of being judged, and took longest to settle afterwards. The book uses "cortisol" as shorthand for the whole slower system, because the evidence links that system to poor sleep more clearly than it links cortisol alone.

Godoy LD et al. 2018, *Front Behav Neurosci* 12:127. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2018.00127 (review; the fast sympathetic arm gives rapid, short-lasting responses, the hormonal arm both short- and long-term effects)
Dickerson SS, Kemeny ME 2004, *Psychol Bull* 130:355–391. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.355 (meta-analysis of laboratory stress studies)

### Does cortisol respond to anything perceived as urgent — threat or productivity alike?

Cortisol is released in response to any perceived demand, whether a threat or a positive productive urgency. The physiological stress response does not discriminate between negative and positive perceived urgency — both activate the HPA axis.

Kim EJ, Dimsdale JE 2007, *Behav Sleep Med* 5:256–278. https://doi.org/10.1080/15402000701557383

### Does cortisol spike in response to demand and return to baseline in a healthy system?

Cortisol has a diurnal baseline; acute demands produce reactive spikes above it. In a well-regulated system, cortisol returns to baseline after the demand resolves. Chronic or repeated demands can elevate the baseline over time.

Fries E, Dettenborn L, Kirschbaum C 2009, *Int J Psychophysiol* 72:67–73. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2008.03.014
Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
<!-- added 2026-10-19, cortisol-causation check (Ben); sits with Ch7 line 49, 'when the Cortisol executive refuses to pack up and go home' -->
### Does high cortisol actually keep you awake?

Possibly, but it has not been shown directly. People with chronic insomnia have moderately higher cortisol on average, most of all in the evening and the first half of the night, and long-term excess, from steroid medicines or Cushing syndrome, goes with disturbed sleep that partly recovers once the excess is treated. Against a simple cause-and-effect reading, a night-time dose of cortisol in healthy young men deepened sleep rather than disturbing it, and the brain's upstream stress signal, CRH, has the stronger evidence for causing wakefulness, more so in middle age. Sleep loss also raises cortisol the next evening, so higher cortisol may be partly a result of poor sleep as well as a contributor; in the one small trial of a drug that blocks cortisol's action, insomnia improved.

Dressle RJ et al. 2022, *Sleep Med Rev* 62:101588. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2022.101588 (meta-analysis of 20 case-control studies, 449 people with insomnia and 357 good sleepers; moderately higher cortisol, larger in blood samples; link with the amount of sleep lost not significant; abstract only)
Vgontzas AN et al. 2001, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 86(8):3787–3794. https://doi.org/10.1210/jcem.86.8.7778 (11 young adults with insomnia and 13 controls; 24-hour ACTH significantly higher and cortisol higher at borderline significance, most of all in the evening and first half of the night; the more disturbed sleepers secreted more cortisol; abstract only)
Huscher D et al. 2009, *Ann Rheum Dis* 68(7):1119–1124. https://doi.org/10.1136/ard.2008.092163 (1,066 people with rheumatoid arthritis; self-reported sleep disturbance rose steadily with glucocorticoid dose; observational; abstract only)
Ismailogullari S et al. 2021, *Horm Metab Res* 53(9):608–615. https://doi.org/10.1055/a-1542-8816 (34 people with Cushing syndrome and 23 controls, sleep-lab recordings; less deep and REM sleep, with deep sleep partly recovering 12 months after treatment; abstract only)
Born J et al. 1989, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 68(5):904–911. https://doi.org/10.1210/jcem-68-5-904 (10 healthy men given overnight cortisol infusions: more deep sleep and less REM sleep than on placebo; abstract only)
Friess E et al. 2004, *Neuropsychopharmacology* 29(3):598–604. https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.npp.1300362 (10 healthy young men given repeated hydrocortisone injections at night: more deep, slow-wave sleep; the authors propose it works by damping the brain's own CRH; abstract only)
Vgontzas AN et al. 2001, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 86(4):1489–1495. https://doi.org/10.1210/jcem.86.4.7370 (12 middle-aged and 12 young healthy men given CRH just after falling asleep; cortisol rose similarly in both, but only the middle-aged men had more wakefulness and less deep sleep; abstract only)
Leproult R et al. 1997, *Sleep* 20:865–870. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/20.10.865 (evening cortisol up 37% after partial and 45% after total sleep loss; healthy young men)

### Does melatonin ease around 2am, letting cortisol fill the gap?

Cortisol follows a circadian rhythm with a nadir around midnight, followed by a rise beginning approximately 2:00–3:00am and a peak around 8:30am. As melatonin eases in the second half of the night, this early-morning cortisol rise fills the gap. If cortisol is chronically elevated, it can surface a person from deeper sleep during this phase of natural transition.

Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214

### Does repeated stimulation raise the cortisol baseline over time?

Repeated stimulation and stress raise baseline cortisol over time, shallowing the evening decline that is needed for melatonin to rise. This is supported both by the caffeine-cortisol literature and by the sleep-deprivation-cortisol literature.

Lovallo WR et al. 2005, *Psychosom Med* 67:734–739. https://doi.org/10.1097/01.psy.0000181270.20036.06
Leproult R et al. 1997, *Sleep* 20:865–870. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/20.10.865

### Does caffeine taken late act as a cortisol stimulus?

Caffeine increases cortisol secretion both at rest and under mental stress. In a controlled crossover study (48 men, 48 women), a 1:00pm dose after five days' abstinence kept cortisol elevated to 7:00pm. In daily users (300–600 mg/day) the morning response disappeared and the afternoon response returned to placebo levels by evening: responses are "reduced, but not eliminated" (partial tolerance). Verified 2026-10-14. Note: the evening difference reflects tolerance, not caffeine clearance; the stronger bedtime effect of late caffeine is adenosine blockade, not cortisol.

Lovallo WR et al. 2005, *Psychosom Med* 67:734–739. https://doi.org/10.1097/01.psy.0000181270.20036.06

### Does sleep deprivation raise cortisol?

Sleep deprivation acts as a physiological stressor. Partial and total sleep deprivation are followed by elevated plasma cortisol in the evening of the subsequent day (37% and 45% increases, respectively, compared to baseline). The body responds to sleep loss with a cortisol response comparable to other stressors.

Leproult R et al. 1997, *Sleep* 20:865–870. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/20.10.865

## Chapter 11 — Your Feedback Loop

<!-- endnote -->
### What sleep efficiency should you aim for?

Sleep efficiency (time spent asleep as a proportion of time in bed) above 85% is the commonly used target for healthy adult sleep. This threshold is used both in validated sleep-quality criteria and as the titration target in CBT-i sleep restriction protocols.

Ohayon M et al. 2017, *Sleep Health* 3:6–19 — National Sleep Foundation sleep-quality recommendations. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2016.11.006 (DOI verified 2026-10-14)

<!-- endnote -->
### Does deep sleep do the body's physical repair work?

Stage 3 non-REM (slow-wave) sleep is the phase associated with physical restoration and repair. NINDS describes it as "the period of deep sleep that you need to feel refreshed" and associates it with restorative processes.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does HRV reflect how well the nervous system balances its two modes?

Heart rate variability is a measure of autonomic regulation; higher HRV is associated with greater parasympathetic (vagal) tone and better resilience. The framing of HRV as "balancing two modes" is a useful simplification — in reality, the PNS and SNS contributions to HRV are complex and non-linear — but the direction ("higher HRV ~ better recovery") is well supported.

Shaffer F, Ginsberg JP 2017, *Front Public Health* 5:258. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2017.00258

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Is REM sleep where memory consolidation and emotional processing happen?

REM sleep is central to memory consolidation and emotional processing. Memory consolidation most likely requires both non-REM and REM sleep — attributing it solely to REM oversimplifies the picture. Emotional processing during REM is strongly supported: the amygdala becomes increasingly active during this stage, and dreaming is associated with emotional regulation.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

Consumer trackers are strongest at detecting total sleep but weakest at sleep-stage classification. Trackers measure total sleep time fairly reliably (high epoch sensitivity) but they can miscount lying very still while awake as sleep, because consumer-grade accelerometry is poor at detecting quiet wakefulness. Stage-by-stage classification accuracy is lower.

## Chapter 12 — Supplements

<!-- endnote -->
### Does a supplement's specific form or extract matter more than the generic name?

The form, salt, or extract of a supplement determines its bioavailability and effect. Organic forms of magnesium (citrate, glycinate, L-threonate) are more bioavailable than inorganic forms (oxide). The "form matters" principle is supported by systematic review of magnesium bioavailability.

Phosphatidylserine (PS) at 800 mg/day significantly blunted the ACTH and cortisol responses to physical exercise in a controlled trial. The evidence base is specifically for cortisol response to physical or exercise stress, not evening or pre-sleep cortisol. Soy- or sunflower-derived phosphatidylserine is the recommended form (brain-cortex PS is no longer available). ## Chapter 13 — Your Sleep Environment

<!-- endnote -->
### Does night light in the bedroom register as a wake signal?

Light in the bedroom at night is read by the circadian system as a signal of daytime. Even low-level light can maintain a state of mild alertness that interferes with sleep onset and quality, consistent with the light-suppression-of-melatonin literature.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Why is temperature specifically "wired into the biology" of sleep?

Temperature is embedded in sleep physiology in a way light and noise are not: the core body temperature drop is part of the initiating mechanism of sleep, not merely a condition to manage around it. Elevated ambient temperature increases wakefulness and reduces both slow-wave and REM sleep, while the core temperature decline is most closely aligned with NREM sleep onset. The chapter's other environmental factors — light and noise — affect the sleep system primarily through signalling pathways; temperature directly constrains the physiological conditions sleep requires to occur.

Harding EC, Franks NP, Wisden W 2019, *Front Neurosci* 13:336. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.00336
Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *J Physiol Anthropol* 31:14. https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14

### Where does the "around 29 °C" near-naked sleep figure come from?

The figure traces to an early laboratory study in which six men slept nude except for shorts, with no bedding, across five ambient temperatures; thermoneutrality — the least disruptive condition — was around 29 °C, and cold was generally more disruptive than heat. ### How much does core body temperature drop during sleep onset?

The physiology literature consistently places the core temperature decline at sleep initiation at approximately 0.5–1 °C, the range the book gives, with "about one to two degrees Fahrenheit" as the accurate conversion. The decline is not a single moment: core temperature begins falling in the hour before sleep onset and continues through early NREM sleep, with the steepest rate of change coinciding with the transition into slow-wave sleep. Heat is shed outward through peripheral vasodilation — primarily via the hands and feet — which is why cold extremities can slow sleep onset and why a warm bath before bed (which drives peripheral vasodilation and accelerates subsequent cooling) can help initiate sleep.

Harding EC, Franks NP, Wisden W 2019, *Front Neurosci* 13:336. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.00336

### Does a warm room fragment sleep, especially REM?

Elevated ambient temperature increases wakefulness and decreases both REM and slow-wave sleep. These effects are directly linked to thermoregulation. A too-warm environment is a well-documented cause of sleep fragmentation.

Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *J Physiol Anthropol* 31:14. https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14

<!-- endnote -->
### Does the body largely suspend thermoregulation during REM?

During REM sleep, the function of thermoregulation is impaired; REM may be characterised as a period of transient heterothermy (the body temporarily takes on some properties of a poikilotherm — unable to regulate its own temperature effectively). This makes ambient room temperature a more direct influence on the body during REM than during non-REM sleep.

Cerri M, Luppi M, Tupone D, Zamboni G, Amici R 2017, *Front Physiol* 8:624. https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2017.00624 (mainly animal evidence)

<!-- endnote -->
### Do sudden noises pull you from deep sleep and lower quality?

Even at very low noise levels, physiological reactions to sudden sounds — increases in heart rate, body movements, and arousals — can be reliably measured. WHO Environmental Noise Guidelines recommend below 30 dB(A) in bedrooms at night. Sudden sounds can pull a sleeper from deep sleep without fully waking them, fragmenting the restorative stages.

WHO, *Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region*. https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/fact-sheets/item/noise

### Does watching TV in bed teach the brain that bed is for wakefulness?

The brain conditions stimulus-response associations between the sleep environment and sleep or wakefulness. Activities in bed that involve wakefulness — including watching TV, using a phone, or worrying — condition the bed as a cue for arousal rather than for sleep. This is the stimulus-control rationale underpinning CBT-i.

AASM, *Behavioral and Psychological Treatments for Insomnia* clinical practice guideline (2020). https://aasm.org/new-guideline-supports-behavioral-psychological-treatments-for-insomnia/

### For bedroom TVs, does stimulation matter more than the light output?

The primary sleep cost of a bedroom TV is the stimulating content and the wakefulness-conditioning association, not the light output. The NSF expert panel reached consensus that screen content impairs sleep but did not reach consensus on screen light. This asymmetry supports the book's claim that "the light output isn't really the main concern here."

Hartstein LE et al. 2024, *Sleep Health* 10:373–384 — NSF consensus statement. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2024.05.001

## Chapter 14 — Caffeine

<!-- endnote -->
### How does caffeine keep you awake?

Caffeine works primarily by blocking adenosine receptors in the brain. Because it occupies the receptors that would otherwise read the accumulating sleep-pressure signal (adenosine), tiredness is masked rather than cleared. The underlying adenosine load continues to build.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

### Is caffeine the single biggest sleep disruptor for a significant number of people?

Caffeine can have a significant impact on falling asleep and staying asleep. Among afternoon caffeine consumers, a high proportion report at least one sleep problem. For this subgroup, caffeine is likely the primary habit disrupting sleep.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-and-sleep

### Does caffeine wearing off worsen the afternoon slump?

Caffeine masks adenosine accumulation. When it wears off and adenosine floods back, the signal is amplified relative to what it would have been without caffeine. Regular caffeine use thus makes the afternoon slump worse, not better, over time.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

### What is caffeine's half-life?

"Roughly five hours" is a reasonable estimate. The European Food Safety Authority's 2015 caffeine opinion gives an average of approximately 4 hours; a 2026 pharmacokinetic study measured 4.0 hours; the broader literature spans approximately 3–7 hours depending on individual variation, medications (oral contraceptives and some SSRIs prolong it notably), and pregnancy (which can extend it to 8–15 hours). ## Chapter 15 — Alcohol

<!-- endnote -->
### Does alcohol speed sleep onset but sedate rather than induce natural sleep?

Alcohol shortens sleep-onset latency, particularly at higher doses, but the resulting sleep is qualitatively different from natural sleep — the biological staging and recovery processes are disrupted. Sedation is not the same as restorative sleep.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030
Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549. https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does alcohol affect deep sleep — and if so, how?

Alcohol does not reduce slow-wave sleep; at most doses it increases it in the first half of the night, and at higher doses the primary change is a reduced latency to deep sleep — reaching it faster rather than getting more overall. This appears to be a sedative artefact: alcohol's CNS depressant effects push the system into deeper non-REM states earlier than it would naturally arrive. The cost comes in the second half of the night, when the alcohol has metabolised and its suppression of REM sleep dominates. Early deep sleep pulled forward by alcohol is not a bonus — it comes at the expense of the overall architecture, displacing the REM that would have followed.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030
Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549. https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006

<!-- endnote -->
### Does eating with alcohol slow absorption and soften the peak?

Food consumed with or before alcohol slows gastric emptying and delays absorption, resulting in a lower, delayed blood-alcohol concentration peak (approximately 9-23% lower than the same amount consumed fasted). This is why drinking on a full stomach produces a softer effect.

NIAAA, *Alcohol Metabolism*. https://www.niaaa.nih.gov/publications/alcohol-metabolism

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Is zero alcohol the recommended position for sleep?

The zero recommendation the book references is the current general-health position: major health bodies, including WHO, state that no amount of alcohol is safe for overall health, and the book applies this direction to sleep. Sleep-specific guidance from sleep foundations typically operationalises the same evidence as "drink as little as possible, as infrequently as possible, finishing as early as possible" rather than a categorical prohibition, because dose-dependent effects mean less is meaningfully better even when zero is not the practical position. The book presents both framings — the health-guidance floor, then the realistic sleep-specific compromise — which accurately reflects the dual position in the literature.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep
WHO (2023), *No level of alcohol consumption is safe for our health*. https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/news/item/04-01-2023-no-level-of-alcohol-consumption-is-safe-for-our-health

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does alcohol suppress REM and fragment the second half of the night?

Alcohol reliably delays the onset of REM sleep and reduces its duration in a dose-dependent manner, with REM disruption observed even at low doses (approximately two standard drinks or 0.50 g/kg or less). At higher doses (approximately five standard drinks or 0.85 g/kg or more), further changes in sleep architecture emerge. In the second half of the night, alcohol causes increased sleep disruption and more frequent awakenings. Note: the claim that alcohol "fragments deep sleep" is not supported by the same evidence — Ebrahim et al. (2013) found it increases early-night slow-wave sleep. The robust cost of alcohol is REM suppression plus second-half fragmentation.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030
Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549. https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Do one to two drinks measurably cut restorative sleep?

Even low doses of alcohol — approximately two standard drinks — produce measurable reductions in REM sleep in a dose-dependent manner, confirmed by Gardiner et al. (2024). No specific per-drink minute estimate can be stated from the available pooled data; the direction is clear and the effect is detectable even at low doses.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does more alcohol mean more restorative sleep lost?

The dose-response relationship in Gardiner et al. (2024) confirms that REM disruption worsens progressively with increasing alcohol doses, with more extensive sleep-architecture effects emerging at approximately five standard drinks. The direction — more drinks means more disruption — is robust; no specific per-dose minute estimate can be stated from the available pooled data.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030

<!-- endnote -->
### Does alcohol cause a metabolic rebound that wakes you in the early hours?

Alcohol causes a more consolidated first half of sleep followed by increased sleep disruption in the second half at all doses studied. This second-half rebound — associated with increased awakenings, elevated heart rate, and heightened arousal — is consistent with the pattern described as "metabolic rebound" in the small hours.

Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549. https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006

<!-- endnote -->
### Does alcohol cause vivid or disturbing dreams through REM rebound?

Second-half sleep after drinking alcohol is associated with more frequent awakenings, night sweats, and nightmares. This is consistent with REM rebound: the suppression of REM in the first half of the night is followed by increased REM pressure (and intensity) in the second half.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep

## Chapter 16 — Food Timing

<!-- endnote -->
### Does eating close to bed raise metabolism and core temperature, opposing cooling?

Diet-induced thermogenesis — the rise in energy expenditure and body temperature following a meal containing carbohydrate or protein — begins within minutes of eating and persists for several hours. Eating close to bed therefore means the body is generating heat at the same time it needs to be cooling down for sleep.

Westerterp KR 2004, *Nutr Metab* 1:5 — diet-induced thermogenesis review. https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-7075-1-5 (corrected 2026-10-14; old citation unverifiable)

<!-- endnote -->
### Does eating closer to bed worsen sleep?

The research is consistent that a gap between the last main meal and bedtime supports sleep quality. A two-to-three-hour gap is the recommendation from the Sleep Health Foundation. The three-hour target used in the book sits at the top of this range and is consistent with the guidance.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep

Enomoto M et al. 2026, *J Physiol Anthropol* 45:12. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40101-026-00430-0

Yan LM et al. 2024, *PLoS ONE* 19:e0308172. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0308172
Note 2026-10-14: evidence mostly observational/small; book's "consistent" softened to "mostly points one way".

### Does late eating ease onset but hurt sleep quality, like alcohol?

Eating close to bedtime may ease sleep onset — the post-meal metabolic state can promote drowsiness — but the quality and architecture of sleep suffer, with fragmentation more likely in the second half of the night. This parallels the alcohol pattern.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep

<!-- endnote -->
### Is three hours before bed the right pre-sleep eating target?

Three hours is the top of the evidence-supported 2-3 hour pre-bed meal gap, and is the author's personal target consistent with Sleep Health Foundation guidance.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep

<!-- endnote -->
### Is hunger itself a cortisol trigger?

Fasting and caloric restriction increase cortisol; a meta-analysis confirms a very strong fasting-cortisol effect. Going to bed hungry may therefore trigger a cortisol response that interferes with the low-cortisol environment sleep requires. The firmest evidence is for sustained fasting; going to bed lightly hungry is a milder extension of this finding.

Nakamura Y et al. 2016, *Stress* 19:30–37 — fasting-cortisol meta-analysis. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.3109/10253890.2015.1121984

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does protein steady blood sugar overnight?

Protein may help steady overnight blood sugar, particularly for people managing a glucose condition. The evidence in otherwise-healthy people is mixed: controlled trials have not consistently shown that the type of bedtime snack meaningfully changes overnight glucose. Major sleep-nutrition sources recommend a small snack combining both a carbohydrate and protein, and credit complex carbohydrates — not protein — with blood-sugar stability, while pairing protein with the tryptophan-to-serotonin pathway.

<!-- endnote -->
### Do carbohydrates help tryptophan reach the brain to support melatonin?

Dietary carbohydrate intake raises the plasma concentration of tryptophan, a precursor of serotonin (and downstream, melatonin). A controlled study found that a high-GI meal consumed four hours before bed shortened sleep-onset latency (9.0 minutes versus 17.5 minutes for a low-GI meal), supporting the carbohydrate-to-tryptophan-to-sleep chain.

Afaghi A, O'Connor H, Chow CM 2007, *Am J Clin Nutr* 85:426–430. https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.2.426

## Chapter 17 — Can't-Sleep Routine

### When the mind races, does getting out of bed help?

Frustration at being unable to sleep is itself a stressor. The hyperarousal model of insomnia — where cognitive, emotional, and physiological arousal maintains wakefulness — is well established. Stimulus control — consistently leaving the bed when awake and returning only when sleepy — gradually reconditions the bed-sleep association. The CBT-i literature and the conditioning principle underlying it confirm that reconditioning takes repeated nights and typically several weeks.

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy for Insomnia (ACT-i) is an established and effective insomnia treatment. Systematic review across 19 studies (n=1,577) found ACT has a significant effect on primary and comorbid insomnia and sleep quality. The ACT-i rationale — that struggling against wakefulness generates more arousal than the wakefulness itself — is the standard framework underpinning the approach.

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### What level of light is needed to start suppressing melatonin?

Light's effect on melatonin follows a dose-response curve rather than a threshold. The figures for a TV at three metres (~5 lux) and a phone held close (~25–50 lux) apply the inverse-square principle. The practical conclusion — a distant screen is unlikely to affect even sensitive individuals, while a close-held phone sits around the group-level ED50 — follows directly from these values.

A standard television at three metres delivers approximately 5 lux to the eye; a phone held 30 cm from the face delivers approximately 25-50 lux. These figures are illustrative of the inverse-square distance principle rather than pinned measurements — actual levels vary with screen brightness, screen size, and ambient conditions. The underlying principle is well established: a distant screen delivers far less light to the eye than a close one.

## Chapter 18 — Morning Routine

### Does shifting wake time shift the melatonin countdown later?

Moving wake time shifts the cortisol-awakening response and adjusts when the evening melatonin countdown begins, pushing the sleep window correspondingly later. Light and wake time together are the primary anchors of the circadian clock.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Fries E, Dettenborn L, Kirschbaum C 2009, *Int J Psychophysiol* 72:67–73. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2008.03.014

<!-- endnote -->
### Is morning light the most powerful cue for setting the circadian clock?

Light is the dominant external cue (Zeitgeber) for the human circadian system. Morning light, which aligns with the rising phase of the circadian cycle, is the strongest single environmental input for setting and anchoring the clock. NINDS identifies light exposure as "perhaps the greatest influence" on sleep-wake needs.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

Brown TM et al. 2022, *PLoS Biol* 20:e3001571. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571 (added 2026-10-14)

### Does 5-15 minutes of morning sun usually suffice, and does overcast beat indoor light?

Five to fifteen minutes of direct sun is usually enough for the circadian signal; overcast outdoor light is still far brighter than typical indoor environments (10,000-100,000 lux outdoors versus 100-500 lux indoors), so time outside on a grey day still meaningfully outperforms staying inside. The brightness contrast is uncontested; the 5-15 minute dose is a practical figure rather than a hard-cited threshold.

Brown TM et al. 2022, *PLoS Biology* 20:e3001571 — expert-consensus light recommendations. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571

<!-- endnote -->
### Is natural light dramatically brighter than indoor lighting, even on an overcast day?

Outdoor natural light is far brighter than typical indoor lighting under all sky conditions. Outdoor daylight ranges from approximately 10,000 to 100,000 lux; typical indoor environments provide 100-500 lux. The claim that natural light, even overcast, is dramatically brighter than indoor lighting is accurate and uncontested.

Brown TM et al. 2022, *PLoS Biology* 20:e3001571 — expert-consensus light recommendations. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does morning movement help set the body clock alongside light?

Morning exercise has modest circadian phase-shifting effects independent of light: physical activity is recognised as a secondary zeitgeber — a time-of-day cue — that reinforces the primary light signal. Movement acts as a complementary zeitgeber rather than a measurable amplifier of the light response. Combining a morning walk with outdoor light provides two consistent time-of-day signals rather than one.

Brown TM et al. 2022, *PLoS Biology* 20:e3001571. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571

<!-- endnote -->
### Can a bright light-therapy lamp substitute for morning sun indoors?

A bright light-therapy lamp (typically 10,000 lux) used for approximately 20-30 minutes in the morning can substitute for natural outdoor light exposure when getting outside is not possible. This is supported by the bright-light-therapy literature for circadian rhythm disorders and seasonal affective disorder.

NCCIH, *Sleep Disorders & Complementary Health Approaches*. https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/sleep-disorders-and-complementary-health-approaches

### Does a consistent wake time and bedtime anchor the circadian clock?

Holding a consistent wake time — even after a bad night — and keeping bedtime within approximately 30 minutes anchors the circadian system. Going to bed even 30 minutes later than usual is associated with a significantly higher resting heart rate throughout sleep that persists into the following day.

Faust L et al. 2020, *npj Digital Medicine* 3:39. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-020-0250-6

### How long should a daytime nap be, and when should it be avoided?

Short naps of approximately 20 minutes (within a 15-30 minute window) are optimal — short enough to avoid slow-wave sleep and the subsequent grogginess of sleep inertia, and not so late that they spend down the adenosine pressure needed for night sleep.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Napping*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/napping

## Chapter 19 — Evening Routine

### Does bright evening light and stimulating content slide the sleep window later?

Evening bright light and stimulating content both delay the sleep window. Bright overhead light suppresses or delays melatonin; stimulating content activates the cortisol/arousal system. Together they push back the convergence of melatonin rise and cortisol decline that creates the sleep opportunity.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin

<!-- endnote -->
### Do modest late-bedtime deviations affect next-day heart rate and recovery?

Going to bed even 30 minutes later than usual is associated with a significantly higher resting heart rate throughout sleep (approximately +0.18 bpm) that persists into the following day, converging with normal levels only in the early evening. The effect is specific to later-than-usual bedtimes. This finding comes from a large wearable-data study of 557 students across 255,736 nights.

Faust L et al. 2020, *npj Digital Medicine* 3:39. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-020-0250-6

## Chapter 20 — Meditation

### Do the ANS's two modes — sympathetic activating and parasympathetic calming — govern arousal?

The autonomic nervous system operates in two broad modes: sympathetic (activating, mobilising) and parasympathetic (calming, restorative). These modes are well established in basic physiology. Most people with persistent sleep problems spend too much time in the sympathetically dominant, activated state — the hyperarousal model of insomnia.

Riemann D et al. 2010, *Sleep Med Rev* 14:19–31 — hyperarousal model. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2009.04.002
Dressle RJ et al. 2023, *J Sleep Res* 32:e13928. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jsr.13928

<!-- endnote -->
### Is calming the body primarily channelled through the vagus nerve?

The vagus nerve is the primary channel of the parasympathetic nervous system. Slow breathing produces significant autonomic effects including changes in heart-rate variability and sympathovagal balance, consistent with vagal activation.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817

<!-- endnote -->
### Is the vagus nerve bidirectional, with most fibres running body-to-brain?

The vagus is a mixed nerve composed of approximately 80% afferent (sensory, body-to-brain) and 20% efferent fibres. Attending to body sensations therefore works the nerve from the sensory side — a bottom-up mechanism that gives behavioural access to the calming system.

Berthoud HR, Neuhuber WL 2000, *Auton Neurosci* 85:1–17 — primary. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1566-0702(00)00215-0 Physiopedia, Vagus Nerve (secondary summary). https://www.physio-pedia.com/Vagus_Nerve

<!-- endnote -->
### Are the lungs uniquely controllable off autopilot?

Breathing is the one autonomic function under reliable voluntary control, which is why it serves as the primary lever for deliberate autonomic regulation. This point is well supported by the slow-breathing and HRV-biofeedback literature.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does nasal breathing naturally slow the breath?

Nasal breathing imposes higher airway resistance than mouth breathing, which naturally reduces peak airflow and slows respiration rate. This is physiologically sound; a single clean authoritative verbatim source was not pinned in the sourcing pass, but the mechanism is well established in respiratory physiology.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309 — slow-breathing autonomic effects. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817

<!-- endnote -->
### Does a slow exhale mechanically activate the vagus nerve?

Slow breathing produces significant effects on the autonomic nervous system, including changes in heart-rate variability, respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and sympathovagal balance. These findings support the claim that a slow exhale shifts autonomic balance toward the parasympathetic (vagal) side.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does a trained vagus nerve suppress the cortisol response?

Slow breathing reliably shifts autonomic balance toward the parasympathetic/vagal side — that link is well established. The further step — that a regularly trained vagus keeps the cortisol response lower over time — is a reasonable inference from the connection between vagal tone and stress regulation, but has not been directly demonstrated in retrieved evidence. Read as "helps turn down" rather than "suppresses."

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817

<!-- endnote -->
### Does a longer exhale and pause net a slower heart rate?

Inhaling speeds heart rate; exhaling and pausing slows it. This is the mechanism of respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) — a well-established phenomenon in which heart rate tracks the respiratory cycle. A breathing pattern with a longer exhale and pause therefore produces a net slowing of heart rate.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817

<!-- endnote -->
### Does consistent breathing or meditation act as nervous-system strength training?

Regular slow-paced breathing and meditation strengthen parasympathetic regulation over time. HRV-biofeedback studies show that baroreflex activity increases after approximately three months of regular practice — the calming system becomes more responsive, not merely more active in the moment.

Lehrer PM, Gevirtz R 2014, *Front Psychol* 5:756. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00756/full
Rusch HL et al. 2019, *Ann N Y Acad Sci* 1445:5–16. https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.13996 (added 2026-10-14; 18 RCTs, n=1,654)

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does a repeated neutral cue like 528 Hz audio become a conditioned calm signal?

A repeatedly paired neutral cue — such as a piece of music consistently used for meditation — can become a conditioned signal for calm through classical conditioning. That mechanism is sound. The separate claim that the 528 Hz frequency itself aids sleep is not supported by robust evidence; the book already hedges this appropriately.

Classical/Pavlovian conditioning (foundational psychology).
Akimoto K et al. 2018, "Effect of 528 Hz Music on the Endocrine System and Autonomic Nervous System," *Health* 10:1159–1170 (SCIRP) — crossover, n=9; 528 Hz reduced salivary cortisol and raised oxytocin vs 440 Hz. (Very small sample, single session, lower-tier journal, unreplicated — treat as preliminary.) https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperabs?paperid=87146

## Chapter 21 — Napping

<!-- endnote -->
### Do naps clear adenosine, and do long or late naps spend the resource?

Naps reduce adenosine load (sleep pressure) and provide genuine restorative benefit when kept short and not too late. Long or late naps, however, spend down the adenosine pressure needed for night sleep, making it harder to fall asleep at the normal time.

## Chapter 22 — Exercise

### Does exercise spike cortisol, and does sleep deprivation slow its clearance?

Moderate-to-high intensity exercise raises cortisol substantially (approximately 40-83% at 60-80% of VO2max; low-intensity exercise actually lowers it). A recovered body clears this cortisol within a few hours. Sleep-deprived bodies recover more slowly from the same exercise stress.

Hill EE et al. 2008, *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18787373/

<!-- endnote -->
### How long does intense exercise elevate body temperature?

Intense exercise significantly raises body core temperature, and thermoregulatory recovery can be prolonged and sustained after exercise ends. The physiological ability to dissipate heat is suppressed for a time-dependent period post-exercise. A window of 30 minutes to two hours for temperature return toward baseline is consistent with the literature, though longer elevations occur with higher exercise intensity or environmental heat.

Kenny GP, McGinn R 2017, *J Appl Physiol* 122:933–944. https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00517.2016

### Do both exercise intensity and timing affect sleep?

Both the intensity and timing of exercise affect sleep. Vigorous exercise close to bedtime impairs sleep; moderate exercise at appropriate times supports it. The key variables interact — a moderate walk in the evening is typically benign; a hard session within the four-hour window before bed is not.

Stutz J, Eiholzer R, Spengler CM 2019, *Sports Med* 49:269–287. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-1015-0

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does regular exercise lower cortisol and improve sleep quality?

Regular physical activity reduces cortisol levels (SMD -0.37) and improves sleep quality (SMD -0.30), supported by systematic review and meta-analysis. The evidence base was predominantly from breast-cancer patient populations, so the effect size for otherwise-healthy people is less precisely characterised — but the directional finding, that regular exercise supports healthier cortisol regulation and better sleep, is consistent with the broader exercise-physiology literature. The book's claim — "Studies also link regular exercise to lower cortisol levels and better sleep quality over time" — accurately reflects this.

De Nys L et al. 2022, *Psychoneuroendocrinology* 143:105843. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2022.105843

<!-- endnote -->
### Should vigorous exercise be finished at least four hours before sleep?

Vigorous exercise bouts ending at least four hours before sleep onset are not associated with changes in sleep. Exercise within four hours of sleep is linked to delayed sleep onset, shorter sleep duration, lower sleep quality, higher nocturnal heart rate, and lower HRV. This finding comes from 14,689 active adults across 4,084,354 person-nights of wearable data. An earlier meta-analysis found the clearest impairment when vigorous exercise ended within one hour of bed; the four-hour threshold is better supported by the larger, more recent study.

Leota J et al. 2025, *Nat Commun* 16:3297. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58271-x
Stutz J, Eiholzer R, Spengler CM 2019, *Sports Med* 49:269–287. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-1015-0

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Is finishing vigorous exercise six hours before bed a safe personal margin?

The research supports four hours as the threshold at which vigorous exercise is not associated with sleep changes. An individual choosing six hours as a personal conservative margin is not contradicted by the evidence — it simply exceeds the research floor. The book frames this explicitly as personal self-experimentation, which is appropriate.

Leota J et al. 2025, *Nat Commun* 16:3297. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58271-x

<!-- endnote -->
### Do yoga, tai chi, and qigong train the nervous system's calming capacity?

Yoga and tai chi may improve sleep quality, per the NCCIH summary of complementary approaches to sleep disorders. These somatic practices combine movement with breath and attention in ways that train the calming nervous system. Evidence quality is limited to low; claims should remain modest.

NCCIH, *Sleep Disorders & Complementary Health Approaches*. https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/sleep-disorders-and-complementary-health-approaches

### Can you not blue-light-filter your way out of screen-related sleep disruption?

The NSF expert panel reached consensus that screen content impairs sleep; it did not reach consensus on screen light as the mechanism. Blue-light filtering addresses only the light component, not the stimulating or emotionally engaging content that is the more reliable disruptor.

The National Sleep Foundation expert panel reached consensus that screen content impairs sleep health in children and adolescents, but did not reach consensus on a statement about the effect of pre-bed screen light. This asymmetry backs the claim that stimulating or emotionally engaging content is a more reliable sleep disruptor than light exposure alone. The consensus was specific to children and adolescents; adult evidence was not agreed upon.

People respond most reliably to emotionally provocative, negative, or outrage-inducing content. A 17-country study found that negative information has a stronger influence on human attention, cognition, and emotion than positive information — a well-documented negativity bias.

## Chapter 24 — Hydration

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does mild dehydration raise cortisol?

Hypohydration is associated with significantly elevated cortisol (standardised mean difference approximately 1.12) across 21 studies in systematic review and meta-analysis. The strongest evidence comes from studies using exercise- or heat-induced dehydration; the everyday version — mild dehydration from simply drinking too little — is a reasonable extension of this finding rather than something directly demonstrated in passive-restriction studies alone.

Zaplatosch ME, Adams WM 2020, *Nutrients* 12:2526. https://doi.org/10.3390/nu12092526

### Does mild dehydration register as a low-level stress state?

See Chapter 24: Does mild dehydration raise cortisol? — the cortisol elevation associated with hypohydration is the physiological basis for describing dehydration as registering as a low-level stress state.

<!-- endnote -->
### Does dehydration impair the body's ability to cool down?

Dehydration reduces sweat rate and skin blood flow, impairing evaporative heat loss and raising core temperature during heat stress or exercise. This means dehydration interferes with the thermoregulatory cooling process that is necessary for sleep onset.

Sawka MN et al. (ACSM) 2007, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 39:377–390. https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597 (replaces Zaplatosch, which is the cortisol meta-analysis; 2026-10-14)

### Does cooling down help trigger sleep onset?

A falling core temperature is a key trigger for sleep onset; NREM onset is most closely aligned with the steepest rate of core-temperature decline. Anything that impairs the body's ability to cool — including dehydration and a warm room — can delay or disrupt sleep onset.

Harding EC, Franks NP, Wisden W 2019, *Front Neurosci* 13:336. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.00336

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does elevated cortisol make the bladder signal louder during lighter sleep?

Nocturnal urine production is naturally suppressed by overnight vasopressin release; during lighter or fragmented sleep, the arousal threshold is lower, so the bladder signal is more likely to break through. The specific mechanism that elevated cortisol contributes to this by lightening sleep is the author's inference, plausible but not directly demonstrated.

George CP, Messerli FH, Genest J, Nowaczynski W, Boucher R, Kuchel O, Rojo-Ortega JM 1975, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 41(2):332–338 — classic demonstration that plasma vasopressin is significantly higher at night, peaking around midnight–4am, explaining the nocturnal decrease in urine output. https://doi.org/10.1210/jcem-41-2-332
Andersson KE, Van Kerrebroeck P 2018, *Curr Urol Rep* 19:8. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11934-018-0750-y

<!-- endnote -->
### Does thirst lag behind actual dehydration?

Thirst is a late sign of dehydration — relying on thirst means a person is already meaningfully behind on fluid intake. NHS guidance explicitly states this.

NHS inform, *Hydration*. https://www.nhsinform.scot/campaigns/hydration/

<!-- endnote -->
### Is around two litres a day a reasonable daily fluid target for most adults?

EFSA's Dietary Reference Values for water set adequate intakes of 2.0 litres/day for women and 2.5 litres/day for men (total water, including approximately 20% from food), equivalent to roughly 1.5-2 litres of fluid. Around two litres of fluid per day is a reasonable adult target for most people, with more needed in hot conditions or with high activity.

EFSA 2010, *Dietary Reference Values for water*, *EFSA Journal* 8(3):1459. https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459

<!-- endnote -->
### Is urine colour the simplest hydration indicator?

Urine colour is a readily available, zero-cost hydration marker: pale straw indicates good hydration; dark yellow indicates dehydration. NHS hydration guidance uses urine colour as the simplest monitoring tool.

NHS inform, *Hydration*. https://www.nhsinform.scot/campaigns/hydration/

## Chapter 25 — Sugar and Food Sensitivities

### Does sleep deprivation reduce self-control and willpower?

Sleep deprivation measurably reduces capacity for self-control. A sleep-deprived individual who has expended self-regulatory resources is at increased risk of succumbing to impulsive desires, impaired attention, and compromised decision-making. See Does sleep deprivation reduce self-control? (Chapter 14, same source).

### Does poor sleep raise ghrelin and lower leptin?

Sleep restriction is associated with an average 28% increase in the appetite-stimulating hormone ghrelin and an 18% decrease in the appetite-suppressing hormone leptin, alongside a 24% increase in hunger and 23% increase in appetite reported by subjects.

Sleep restriction is associated with increased appetite specifically for calorie-dense, high-carbohydrate foods (increases of 33-45%). ### Does the brain run on glucose and treat a glucose drop as an emergency?

The brain normally relies on circulating blood glucose as a primary energy source, accounting for 60-80% of total body glucose uptake. When blood glucose falls, the brain registers this as an emergency and triggers counter-regulatory hormones.

Verberne AJ, Sabetghadam A, Korim WS 2014, *Front Neurosci* 8:38. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2014.00038

Cortisol is a counter-regulatory hormone that activates hepatic gluconeogenesis to raise plasma glucose and reduces insulin-dependent glucose uptake. It is one of several hormones the body uses to correct hypoglycaemia.
