# Proposal — Ch06 Melatonin: add the sleep-maintenance dimension

*Status: proposal only. `/book/` is read-only; apply by hand if accepted.*
*Date: 2026-06-29*

## Why

As written, Ch06 treats one failure mode: **missing the evening window** (sleep onset).
Its three reasons poor sleepers miss the window — light, irregular schedule, stress — are
all onset problems, and the chapter closes by declaring melatonin "wasn't my problem… not
a deficiency." But a second failure mode — **not holding the night** (sleep maintenance,
the 2–3 a.m. wake) — is absent, and the maintenance-insomnia literature points to a
genuinely *reduced overnight melatonin amplitude* in that subgroup (Hajak 1995, Riemann
2002, Léger 2004; see KB Ch06 entry "Is nocturnal melatonin blunted in people with chronic
insomnia?"). Author's editorial call: most sufferers experience **both** onset and
maintenance, so both belong in the chapter.

Adding maintenance creates one contradiction to resolve: the universal "not a deficiency"
close. The fixes below add the maintenance beat and rework the close so the two no longer
collide, while preserving the existing hand-off to cortisol (Ch07).

---

## Change 1 — NEW section (insert after current ¶: "…The window arrives on schedule.")

Insert as a distinct movement between the onset fix (consistent bedtime + morning light)
and the "Melatonin can still be useful…" supplement aside.

> But getting in is only half of it.
>
> The window is about falling asleep. Staying asleep is a different job — and it runs on
> the same hormone. Remember that melatonin peaks and holds high through the middle of the
> night? That plateau isn't decoration. It's the signal meant to keep the night quiet — to
> hold the Sleep Executives in their chairs while the Wake Executives stay home.
>
> For a lot of poor sleepers, that plateau sits lower than it should. The signal still
> comes, but weaker — less of it through the exact hours when the night is most fragile.
> And a weak hold is easy to break. A noise, a full bladder, a stray spike of stress —
> things a strong signal would absorb — are now enough to surface you. You wake at two or
> three in the morning and can't work out why.
>
> This is the part most of us never see, because we're asleep for it. We notice the window
> — the frustrating wait to get in. We don't notice the hold quietly failing. But most
> people who sleep badly aren't one or the other. They're both: slow to get in, quick to
> come back out.
>
> So melatonin can fall short in two directions. It can arrive too late, or too scattered,
> to open the window cleanly. And it can run too low through the night to hold it. The good
> news is the same fix covers both — consistent timing and morning light steady the whole
> rhythm, and a well-timed signal tends to be a strong one.

Notes:
- Uses the existing "peaks and holds high through the middle of the night" line (current
  ¶ near the diagram) as a deliberate call-back, so no new mechanism is introduced cold.
- Keeps the Wake/Sleep Executive metaphor consistent with current ¶ "…the Sleep Executives
  don't have to battle them out the door."
- Stays in author voice (short sentences, em-dashes, second person). No statistics — the
  numbers and caveats live in the endnote, per house style.

## Change 2 — REWORK the close (removes the contradiction)

CURRENT (final reflection):

> Looking back, melatonin wasn't my problem.
>
> My body was making it. The signal was being sent. What I'd spent years trying to fix
> wasn't a deficiency — it was a system working against itself. No supplement was going to
> fix that. But understanding what melatonin actually does — and what it doesn't — at least
> explained why some nights sleep came the moment my head hit the pillow — and others, it
> didn't come for hours.

PROPOSED:

> Looking back, my problem was mostly the window.
>
> My body was making melatonin. The signal was being sent — it just wasn't landing when I
> needed it. For me, that wasn't a deficiency so much as a system working against itself.
> For others, the overnight signal really does run low, and the night pays for it. Either
> way, the answer isn't a bottle. A scattered signal and a weak one are, underneath, the
> same problem — a rhythm that's lost its footing. And you set a rhythm with timing and
> light, not with a higher dose. Understanding what melatonin actually does — and what it
> doesn't — at least explained why some nights sleep came the moment my head hit the pillow,
> and others it didn't come for hours.

Why: "wasn't my problem / not a deficiency" was stated universally. Narrowing it to "*my*
problem was mostly the window" keeps the author's honest personal arc while conceding the
maintenance subgroup's genuinely lower overnight signal — so it no longer contradicts
Change 1. Keeps "the answer isn't a bottle / timing and light, not a higher dose," which
also sets up the dose caution already in the chapter.

## Change 3 — STRENGTHEN the cortisol hand-off (transition into Ch07)

CURRENT:

> Which raises the obvious question: what was keeping the Wake Executives so resistant in
> the first place? What was stopping the system from standing down?
>
> That's cortisol's problem. And cortisol, as it turns out, has a great deal to answer for.

PROPOSED:

> Which raises the obvious question. A weak overnight signal makes the night easy to break —
> but something still has to do the breaking. What keeps the Wake Executives so resistant?
> What walks back into the building at three in the morning and turns the lights on?
>
> That's cortisol's problem. And cortisol, as it turns out, has a great deal to answer for.

Why: now that the 2–3 a.m. wake is on the table, the bridge can carry it. The weak melatonin
hold explains *fragility*; cortisol explains the *trigger* — which is exactly the
convergence framing in `assets/why-we-wake-at-2-3am.md` (blunted melatonin amplitude +
elevated cortisol). Preserves the existing punchline line into Ch07 verbatim.
