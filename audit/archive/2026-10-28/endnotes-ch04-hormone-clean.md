## Chapter 1 — The Weight of It

**Is poor sleep linked to serious disease?**

Insufficient sleep is associated with increased risk of heart disease, diabetes, high blood pressure, stroke, depression and obesity, and both the CDC and NINDS recognise these links. Long-term studies also link persistent short sleep in midlife to a higher risk of dementia later in life.

CDC, Chronic Disease Indicators: Sleep (2024). https://www.cdc.gov/cdi/indicator-definitions/sleep.html
NINDS, Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Sabia S et al. 2021, *Nat Commun* 12:2289. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-22354-2 (about 8,000 UK adults followed for 25 years; six hours or less in one's 50s and 60s linked to about 30% higher dementia risk; observational, so cause and effect is not established)

**Does dropping off instantly mean you're sleeping well?**

A mean sleep-onset latency below 5–8 minutes on the Multiple Sleep Latency Test is
classified as pathological sleepiness; deliberate sleep deprivation drives it below one
minute. Falling asleep the moment one's head hits the pillow is a reliable marker of
significant sleep debt, though very short latency can also occur in sleep apnoea and
narcolepsy.

Sleep Foundation, *Sleep Latency* (MSLT norms summary).
https://www.sleepfoundation.org/how-sleep-works/sleep-latency

## Chapter 2 — The Turning Point

**Can hard training make sleep worse, not better?**

High-intensity exercise raises cortisol substantially (approximately 83% at 80% of
VO₂max) and produces prolonged post-exercise elevation in core body temperature, both
of which can delay or disrupt sleep onset. Very hard training sessions — particularly
late in the day — can worsen rather than improve sleep quality through this dual pathway.

Hill EE et al. 2008, *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18787373/
Kenny GP, McGinn R 2017, *J Appl Physiol* 122:933–944.
https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00517.2016

**Does daytime stress disrupt sleep at night?**

When stress was applied in experiments, it consistently reduced slow-wave sleep, REM sleep and sleep efficiency while increasing awakenings. A systematic review of 63 studies supports the same picture: stress holds the brain in a lighter, more vigilant state at night.

Kim EJ, Dimsdale JE 2007, *Behav Sleep Med* 5:256–278. https://doi.org/10.1080/15402000701557383

## Chapter 3 — The Uncomfortable Truth

**Is CBN really more sleep-targeted than CBD?**

Cannabinol (CBN) forms from the degradation of THC and has mild psychoactive properties.
The claim that it is more sleep-targeted than CBD is not established: the systematic
evidence for cannabinoids and sleep shows benefit primarily from THC-type compounds, with
no significant effect for CBD alone and only a small reduction in night-time waking in
the one published human CBN trial.

da Silva SHN et al. 2025, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* — meta-analysis; benefit came mainly
from THC-type compounds. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2025.102156
Bonn-Miller MO et al. 2024, *Exp Clin Psychopharmacol* — human CBD trial; no significant
sleep effect. https://doi.org/10.1037/pha0000682
Saleska JL et al. 2024, *J Am Nutr Assoc* — the only published human CBN trial; small
reduction in night-time waking only. https://doi.org/10.1080/27697061.2023.2203221

**Can anyone truly function on little sleep?**

Natural short sleeping exists but is rare. It has been traced to variants in genes such as DEC2 and ADRB1, each found in a small number of families. For most people, sleep restriction carries a cost they do not notice: performance keeps declining while the feeling of sleepiness levels off.

He Y et al. 2009, *Science* 325:866–870. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1174443 (DEC2 variant identified in one family)
Shi G et al. 2019, *Neuron* 103:1044–1055. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.07.026 (ADRB1 variant identified in one family)
Van Dongen HPA et al. 2003, *Sleep* 26:117–126. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117 (48 healthy adults; 14 days at 4, 6 or 8 hours in bed; self-rated sleepiness rose only slightly while performance kept declining)

**Does poor sleep raise long-term health risks?**

Yes. Insufficient sleep is associated with increased risk of heart disease, diabetes and depression, and with long-term cognitive decline. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 1, in the note "Is poor sleep linked to serious disease?"

**Is driving tired as risky as driving drunk?**

Being awake for 17 hours produces impairment comparable to a blood-alcohol level of 0.05%, and being awake for 24 hours to roughly 0.10%. The comparison rests on studies showing similar impairment of cognitive and physiological functioning from fatigue and from alcohol.

CDC/NIOSH, Training for Nurses on Shift Work & Long Work Hours, Module 3 (2020). https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod3/08.html

**Does sleep depend on hormone rhythms?**

Sleep depends on several chemical signals, and calling all of them "hormones" is a simplification. Melatonin and cortisol are hormones that follow a circadian pattern of roughly 24 hours, and when that pattern is disrupted, the conditions for sleep degrade. Adenosine is classified as a neuromodulator and builds across waking hours before clearing during sleep. Dopamine acts mainly as a neurotransmitter. Grouping all four as hormones is a convenient shorthand for a wider picture, but the framing is well supported. All four respond to daily behaviour, which is the basis for the book's focus on habits. The individual habits, and the evidence for each, are covered in the chapters that follow.

NINDS, Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31:e13597. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13597

## Chapter 4 — Sleep Incorporated

**Does adenosine drive sleep pressure?**

Yes. Adenosine builds across waking hours and clears during sleep, and it is a main driver of the pressure to sleep. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 5, in the note "What does "largely lifted" mean for adenosine by morning?"

**Does melatonin set the timing of sleep?**

Yes. Melatonin rises in the evening and marks biological night, signalling when sleep should occur rather than forcing it. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 6, in the note "When does melatonin actually peak during the night?"

**Does cortisol supply waking energy?**

Yes. Cortisol follows a daily rhythm that starts rising before waking and peaks in the morning, helping mobilise energy for the day. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 7, in the note "Does cortisol start rising before you open your eyes?"

**Does dopamine signal "reward" or "wanting"?**

Dopamine encodes incentive salience — the motivational "wanting" or "worth pursuing"
signal — rather than pleasure itself. It responds to opportunity and anticipation,
driving approach behaviour. This distinction matters: a dopamine-driven state is
activating regardless of whether the underlying urge is productive or threatening.

Berridge KC 2007, *Psychopharmacology* 191:391–431.
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6722949

**Are adenosine and dopamine really hormones?**

Not strictly. Adenosine is classified as a neuromodulator, and dopamine acts mainly as a neurotransmitter. In the roles this book describes, both behave in a hormone-like way: they act as broad signals that shift the overall state of the brain and vary across the day, rather than carrying single cell-to-cell messages. Grouping all four as hormones is a simplification. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 3, in the note "Does sleep depend on hormone rhythms?"

**Do sleeping pills fix the underlying problem?**

Sleeping pills can give short-term relief, but they act on the symptom rather than on the habits and thought patterns that keep insomnia going. For that reason, the American College of Physicians recommends cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-i) as the first treatment for adults with chronic insomnia, with medication treated as a short-term addition when CBT-i alone hasn't worked.

Qaseem A et al. 2016, *Ann Intern Med* 165:125–133. https://doi.org/10.7326/M15-2175 (guideline recommendation; the medication advice is graded weak, based on low-quality evidence)

**Does menopause disrupt sleep, and can HRT help?**

Sleep problems become more common through perimenopause and menopause, and the fall in oestrogen and progesterone is linked to the change, along with hot flushes and night sweats. Hormone therapy improves sleep quality on average, but the benefit appears in women who have hot flushes or night sweats; in women without them, studies show no clear improvement. This fits the idea that HRT helps most when menopause symptoms are the main thing disrupting sleep. The evidence does not show HRT fixing sleep problems that began before menopause or have other causes.

Cintron D et al. 2017, *Endocrine* 55:702–711. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12020-016-1072-9 (systematic review and meta-analysis; moderate-quality evidence; benefit limited to women with vasomotor symptoms)
The North American Menopause Society 2022, *Menopause* 29:767–794. https://doi.org/10.1097/GME.0000000000002028 (hormone therapy position statement)

## Chapter 5 — Adenosine

**What does "largely lifted" mean for adenosine by morning?**

By morning, the bulk of the sleep-pressure debt accumulated across the previous waking
day has cleared — but adenosine clearance is gradual and progressive across the whole
night rather than an overnight reset to a fixed baseline. Slow-wave sleep is most closely
linked to this process; the homeostatic sleep literature tracks it through the
slow-wave-activity curve, which reflects how much adenosine-related pressure has been
discharged. "Largely lifted" is accurate: the clearing is substantial but not necessarily
complete, particularly after disrupted or shortened slow-wave sleep.

Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31:e13597.
https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13597
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*.
https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Does stress make sleep lighter?**

Yes. Stress reduces deep and REM sleep and increases awakenings, holding the brain in a lighter, more vigilant state. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 2, in the note "Does daytime stress disrupt sleep at night?"

**Does a warm room reduce deep sleep?**

Yes. Sleep depends on core body temperature falling, and a warm environment slows that fall, increasing wakefulness and reducing deep and REM sleep. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 13, in the note "Why is temperature specifically "wired into the biology" of sleep?"

**Does an irregular sleep schedule reduce deep sleep?**

Deep sleep is front-loaded into the first half of the night. An irregular schedule
shifts circadian timing, so a later sleep onset reduces the window available for
slow-wave sleep. Social jetlag literature and standard sleep-stage data both support
this mechanism.

Roenneberg T et al. 2012, *Curr Biol* 22:939–943 — social jetlag.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2012.03.038
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*.
https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Do screens at night disrupt sleep?**

Yes. Screen content, notifications and the habit of checking are linked to poorer sleep, with the clearest expert consensus in children and adolescents. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 23, in the note "Does screen content matter more than screen light for delaying sleep?"

**Why doesn't alcohol appear in this chapter's list of deep sleep disruptors?**

Alcohol is absent from this chapter's disruptors list because its effect on slow-wave
sleep runs counter to the typical disruption pattern — most doses actually increase
early-night deep sleep rather than reducing it. The sleep cost of alcohol lies in REM
suppression and second-half fragmentation, which is why it is covered separately in
Chapter 15. Including it in a list of "things that reduce deep sleep" would have been
inaccurate.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030
Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549.
https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006

**Does caffeine's effect really last for hours?**

Yes. Caffeine's half-life averages around four to five hours, so a cup in the afternoon is still active in the evening. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 14, in the note "What is caffeine's actual half-life?"

## Chapter 6 — Melatonin

**Is melatonin a sleep signal rather than a sleeping pill?**

The brain releases melatonin as light fades in the evening, marking the start of biological night. Alongside this timing role, it slightly lowers core body temperature and reduces alertness, moving the body toward a state in which sleep can happen. It does not force sleep on its own.

NINDS, Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), Melatonin. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin
Cagnacci A et al. 1997, *J Biol Rhythms* 12:509–517. https://doi.org/10.1177/074873049701200604 (core temperature)
Lok R et al. 2019, *J Pineal Res* 67:e12583. https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.12583 (alertness)

**When does melatonin actually peak during the night?**

"The middle of the night" reflects the broad range in the literature: melatonin's
acrophase falls roughly between midnight and 3 a.m. in most people, then declines
gradually through the early morning hours. Exact timing varies considerably between
individuals and is anchored to habitual wake time — which is why naming a specific clock
hour would have been misleading. For most adults sleeping around 10–11 p.m., "the middle
of the night" corresponds to roughly 1–3 a.m.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*.
https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin

**Does evening light delay melatonin?**

Melatonin release depends on darkness, so bright light in the evening tells the brain it is still daytime: the rise is delayed and the amount released can be reduced. Short-wavelength (blue) light, which screens produce, has the strongest effect.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), Melatonin. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin
NINDS, Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Does an irregular schedule throw off melatonin timing?**

Yes. Without consistent sleep and wake times, the body clock loses its reference point, so the evening melatonin rise can arrive at the wrong time. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 5, in the note "Does an irregular sleep schedule reduce deep sleep?"

**Can stress override rising melatonin?**

Yes. Cortisol and melatonin work in opposition, and a nervous system running hot can hold sleep off even as melatonin rises. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 7, in the note "Do cortisol and melatonin work against each other?"

**Do morning light and a consistent bedtime set melatonin timing?**

Yes. Morning light and regular sleep times anchor the body clock, which in turn sets when melatonin rises in the evening. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 18, in the note "Is morning light the strongest cue for the body clock?"

**Is overnight melatonin lower in people with insomnia?**

Several small studies have found that people with long-term insomnia, especially those who struggle to stay asleep, produce less melatonin through the middle of the night than good sleepers. The finding is suggestive rather than settled: the studies are small, not all agree, and it is unclear whether lower melatonin causes broken sleep or simply accompanies it. Recent reviews support the broader idea that a weaker overnight rhythm is linked to trouble staying asleep.

Hajak G et al. 1995, *J Pineal Res* 19:116–122. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079x.1995.tb00179.x (about 10 participants; deficit greatest where insomnia had lasted more than five years)
Riemann D et al. 2002, *Psychiatry Res* 113:17–27. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0165-1781(02)00249-4 (small sample; authors call the finding's significance a matter of debate)
Léger D, Laudon M, Zisapel N 2004, *Am J Med* 116:91–95. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2003.07.017 (older adults)
Lack LC, Micic G, Lovato N 2023, *J Sleep Res* 32:e13976. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13976 (review)
Scott H et al. 2025, *J Pineal Res* 77:e70069. https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.70069

**Does slow-release melatonin help you stay asleep?**

Slow-release melatonin is designed to release over several hours rather than all at once, with the aim of holding the overnight signal steady; a 2 mg form is licensed in Europe for short-term insomnia in adults aged 55 and over. The trial evidence is modest: studies are relatively few, mostly in older adults, rely largely on self-reported sleep, and much of the research is linked to the manufacturer. The trials mainly measured how quickly people fell asleep, and the gain in total sleep time was small (around a quarter of an hour) and seen only in some groups.

Lemoine P et al. 2007, *J Sleep Res* 16:372–380. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2007.00613.x (170 adults aged 55 and over, three weeks; improved self-rated sleep quality and morning alertness)
Wade AG et al. 2010, *BMC Medicine* 8:51. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2933606/ (791 adults, up to six months; total sleep time up 13 minutes in people with low natural melatonin, self-reported; manufacturer-affiliated)
Ferracioli-Oda E et al. 2013, *PLoS ONE* 8:e63773. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0063773 (meta-analysis of 19 trials, mostly immediate-release; modest benefits)

**When is taking melatonin genuinely useful?**

Melatonin's best-supported use is shifting the body clock when it is out of step with local time, as with jet lag or shift work, rather than as a direct sleep aid. For this purpose, low doses (around 0.5–5 mg) appear to work as well as higher ones, with no added benefit from taking more, and the usual guidance is to take it about an hour before the target bedtime.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), Melatonin. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin

**Does a high melatonin dose carry over into the next morning?**

A high dose does not strengthen the sleep signal; it extends melatonin activity into the
following morning, which can produce grogginess. Over time, supraphysiological doses may
blunt sensitivity to the body's own melatonin signal through receptor desensitisation
(this mechanism rests primarily on animal and cell-line research).

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*.
https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin
Menczel Schrire Z et al. 2022, *J Pineal Res* 72(2):e12782.
https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.12782
Gerdin MJ et al. 2004, *FASEB J* 18(14):1646–1656 — MT2 receptor desensitisation
(rat/cell-line model). https://doi.org/10.1096/fj.03-1339com

## Chapter 7 — Cortisol

**Does cortisol respond to urgency, even when the thoughts are positive?**

Cortisol responds to demands, not only to danger, and the demands that raise it most are those that feel pressing, hard to control, or carry a risk of being judged. It rises more after waking on days when a challenge is expected, and it rises with hard physical effort. But it does not respond to every kind of arousal: a large review of laboratory studies concluded that cortisol is not responsive to all types of stressors. No study has directly tested whether enthusiastic planning in the middle of the night raises cortisol; the link rests on the urgency those thoughts carry rather than on a direct measurement.

Dickerson SS, Kemeny ME 2004, *Psychol Bull* 130:355–391. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.355 (meta-analysis of laboratory stress studies)
Wetherell MA, Lovell B, Smith MA 2015, *Stress* 18:42–48. https://doi.org/10.3109/10253890.2014.993967 (the anticipated challenge was an assessed, stressful task)
Hill EE et al. 2008, *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587–591. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03345606

**Does cortisol start rising before you open your eyes?**

Cortisol follows a circadian rhythm that rises through the late night and early morning
as part of the normal diurnal cycle, so it is already elevated before waking. A distinct
post-waking phenomenon — the cortisol awakening response (CAR) — produces an additional
surge peaking 30–45 minutes after waking, superimposed on this overnight rise. The claim
that cortisol is "already rising before you open your eyes" is directionally correct for
the circadian component, which accounts for the gradual pre-waking increase. The CAR is
the sharper peak that follows.

Fries E, Dettenborn L, Kirschbaum C 2009, *Int J Psychophysiol* 72:67–73.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2008.03.014

**Does cortisol return to baseline once a demand passes?**

Cortisol follows a daily baseline curve, and short-term demands produce temporary rises above it. In laboratory studies cortisol climbs during a stressful task and falls back afterwards, with the largest and slowest-to-settle responses coming from situations that feel uncontrollable or involve being judged by others. Physical demands work the same way: moderate-to-hard exercise raises cortisol, while light exercise does not.

Dickerson SS, Kemeny ME 2004, *Psychol Bull* 130:355–391. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.355 (meta-analysis of laboratory stress studies)
Hill EE et al. 2008, *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587–591. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03345606 (cortisol rose at 60% and 80% of maximum aerobic capacity, not at 40%)

**Do cortisol and melatonin work against each other?**

Cortisol and melatonin follow roughly opposite daily rhythms. Cortisol peaks in the morning and declines through the day to its lowest point around the start of the night, while melatonin is low by day and rises in the evening. When cortisol stays high into the evening, as it does under stress, its alerting effect can override melatonin's signal, much as a stimulant can mask tiredness: melatonin may still be rising, but the body stays too activated for sleep to take hold. The two rhythms are well established; the override fits the hyperarousal model of insomnia rather than having been measured directly as an interaction between the two hormones.

Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), Melatonin. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin
Riemann D et al. 2010, *Sleep Med Rev* 14:19–31. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2009.04.002 (hyperarousal model of insomnia)

**Does cortisol start rising in the early hours?**

Cortisol reaches its lowest point around midnight and begins climbing in the early hours, roughly 2 to 3 a.m., on its way to a morning peak. This falls in the second half of the night, when melatonin is past its peak and sleep is lighter. The timing makes raised cortisol a plausible reason people wake at this hour, but that has not been directly shown.

Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214
NINDS, Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Does late caffeine raise cortisol?**

Caffeine raises cortisol, both at rest and under stress. In people who had stopped caffeine for several days, an early-afternoon dose kept cortisol raised into the early evening. In daily coffee drinkers the response was smaller and shorter, but an afternoon dose still produced a rise.

Lovallo WR et al. 2005, *Psychosom Med* 67:734–739. https://doi.org/10.1097/01.psy.0000181270.20036.06 (48 men and 48 women; after five days without caffeine, a 1 p.m. dose raised cortisol through to 7 p.m.; in daily users the morning response disappeared but cortisol still rose after the afternoon dose)

**Does constant stimulation keep cortisol higher?**

Partly. Provocative content brings in the stress system as well as dopamine, but the idea that everyday stimulation steadily raises baseline cortisol has not been directly measured. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 8, in the note "Does provocative content add cortisol on top of dopamine?"

**Does a bad night raise cortisol the next day?**

Sleep loss acts as a stressor. After a night of partial or total sleep deprivation, cortisol the following evening was higher than normal and its evening decline was slower, leaving the next night starting from a worse position.

Leproult R et al. 1997, *Sleep* 20:865–870. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/20.10.865 (evening cortisol up 37% after partial and 45% after total sleep loss; healthy young men)

**Why avoid daytime activities at night?**

Behavioural treatment for insomnia advises keeping the bed for sleep and leaving it when awake, rather than working or scrolling, so that bed and night become cues for sleep. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 17, in the note "Does CBT-i make getting out of bed when awake a central recommendation?"

## Chapter 8 — Dopamine

**Does dopamine respond to opportunity?**

Yes. Dopamine signals that something is worth pursuing, a motivational "wanting" rather than pleasure itself. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 4, in the note "Does dopamine signal "reward" or "wanting"?"

**Do screens desensitise the brain's reward system over time?**

Some evidence suggests that heavy exposure to fast, frequent, unpredictable digital
stimuli may reduce dopamine-receptor availability, making lower-intensity rewards less
satisfying over time. No systematic review has established this as a confirmed finding;
it is best understood as a plausible hypothesis supported by partial evidence.

Clark L, Zack M 2023, *Addict Behav* 140:107626.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2023.107626
Berridge KC 2007, *Psychopharmacology* 191:391–431.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-006-0578-x

**Does provocative content add cortisol on top of dopamine?**

Curiosity-driven content engages the dopamine system, which signals that something is worth pursuing. Provocative content, the kind that provokes a strong reaction before there's time to think, can also engage the stress system and cortisol. Cortisol responds most to demands that feel pressing, uncontrollable or judged by others, rather than to whether the content is negative or positive. This combines two well-studied areas, dopamine "wanting" and the stress response, but no single study has measured both responses to online content directly.

Berridge KC 2007, *Psychopharmacology* 191:391–431. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-006-0578-x
Dickerson SS, Kemeny ME 2004, *Psychol Bull* 130:355–391. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.355 (meta-analysis of laboratory stress studies)
Kim EJ, Dimsdale JE 2007, *Behav Sleep Med* 5:256–278. https://doi.org/10.1080/15402000701557383

**Does the brain respond to novelty and unpredictability?**

The brain's dopamine system responds strongly to things that are new or uncertain. Dopamine neurons fire most when it is unclear whether a reward will arrive, and new images on their own activate the brain's dopamine centres. These findings come from laboratory tasks rather than studies of scrolling, so applying them to social feeds is a reasonable extension, not something measured directly.

Fiorillo CD, Tobler PN, Schultz W 2003, *Science* 299:1898–1902. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1077349 (recordings in monkeys; dopamine activity peaked when reward probability was 50%)
Bunzeck N, Düzel E 2006, *Neuron* 51:369–379. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2006.06.021 (brain imaging in healthy adults)

**Is predictable TV genuinely less activating than interactive media?**

Low-stakes, predictable content produces fewer unpredictable dopamine-reward events than
interactive games or algorithmically curated feeds. This is a reasonable extension of the
variable-reward and novelty-seeking literature, though no direct head-to-head
experimental comparison exists.

Berridge KC 2007, *Psychopharmacology* 191:391–431.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-006-0578-x
Clark L, Zack M 2023, *Addict Behav* 140:107626.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2023.107626

**Are games and social feeds deliberately engineered for dopamine?**

Variable-ratio reinforcement — unpredictable rewards — maximises dopamine-driven approach
behaviour. Games and social-media feeds exploit this mechanism by design. The neuroscience
of variable reward is well established; the description of design intent as deliberate is
as much a design-ethics observation as a neuroscience claim.

Clark L, Zack M 2023, *Addict Behav* 140:107626.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2023.107626

## Chapter 9 — What Goes Wrong

**Do poor sleepers have less melatonin overnight?**

Possibly. Small studies have found lower overnight melatonin in long-term insomnia, though the finding is not settled. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 6, in the note "Is overnight melatonin lower in people with insomnia?"

**Does the bed itself become a trigger for wakefulness?**

It can. After enough nights lying awake there, the bed can become a cue for alertness, which is why behavioural treatment for insomnia keeps the bed for sleep. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 17, in the note "Does CBT-i make getting out of bed when awake a central recommendation?"

**Does melatonin tail off after the middle of the night?**

Yes. Melatonin peaks roughly between midnight and 3 a.m., then declines towards morning. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 6, in the note "When does melatonin actually peak during the night?"

**Can melatonin's overnight decline trigger an early wake?**

In people who wake too early, the body's daily rhythms, including melatonin, often run earlier than in good sleepers. Their melatonin begins to fall and their body temperature begins to rise while they are still asleep, and waking tends to follow. This suggests the timing of the overnight turn matters, not only how much melatonin is produced. The decline is best understood as a marker of that turn rather than a proven cause of waking on its own, and the studies are small.

Lack LC, Mercer JD, Wright H 1996, *J Sleep Res* 5:211–219. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.1996.00211.x (melatonin and temperature rhythms 2–4 hours earlier in early-waking insomniacs; small laboratory study)
Lack LC, Micic G, Lovato N 2023, *J Sleep Res* 32:e13976. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13976 (review)

**Does deep sleep clear sleep pressure, mostly early in the night?**

Yes. Deep sleep is concentrated in the first half of the night, and that is when most sleep pressure is cleared; clearing can fall short when deep sleep is disrupted. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 5, in the note "What does "largely lifted" mean for adenosine by morning?"

**Does cortisol start climbing around 2 to 3 a.m.?**

Yes. Cortisol reaches its low around midnight and begins rising in the early hours. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 7, in the note "Does cortisol start rising in the early hours?"

## Chapter 11 — Your Feedback Loop

**Can sleep tracking increase anxiety about sleep?**

For some users, sleep-tracker data drives obsessive monitoring and heightened anxiety
about sleep quality — a phenomenon termed "orthosomnia." The appropriate response for
users who find tracking counterproductive is to set the tracker aside.

Baron KG et al. 2017, *J Clin Sleep Med* 13:351.
https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/10.5664/jcsm.6472

**Is above 85% a good target for sleep efficiency?**

Sleep efficiency is the share of time in bed actually spent asleep. A National Sleep Foundation expert panel identified 85% or higher as a marker of good sleep quality across adult age groups, and the same figure is used as a working target in behavioural treatment for insomnia. It is a guide rather than a hard line, and trackers' estimates of time spent awake in bed are among their least accurate measures.

Ohayon M et al. 2017, *Sleep Health* 3:6–19. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2016.11.006 (expert consensus panel)

**Is deep sleep when the body does its physical repair?**

Deep (slow-wave) sleep is the stage most closely associated with physical restoration and with waking refreshed. Describing it as the body's repair time is a fair simplification: restorative processes are not confined to a single stage, and much of the evidence links deep sleep to recovery rather than measuring repair directly.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Is REM sleep where memory consolidation and emotional processing happen?**

REM sleep is central to memory consolidation and emotional processing, though
consolidation most likely requires both non-REM and REM. Attributing it solely to REM
oversimplifies the process. The amygdala is markedly active during REM, and REM sleep is
strongly associated with emotional regulation.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*.
https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Does HRV measure how well the nervous system balances its two modes?**

Heart rate variability measures autonomic regulation; higher HRV is associated with
greater parasympathetic (vagal) tone and better recovery capacity. "Balancing two modes"
is a useful simplification — the PNS and SNS contributions to HRV are non-linear — but
the directional interpretation (higher HRV ≈ better recovery) is well supported.

Shaffer F, Ginsberg JP 2017, *Front Public Health* 5:258.
https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2017.00258

**How accurate are consumer sleep trackers?**

Consumer trackers are most accurate for total sleep time and least accurate for sleep stages. Averaged across groups of people, the better devices estimate total sleep close to laboratory measurement, though any single night can be out by considerably more. Long stretches of lying still while awake are often recorded as sleep, and devices make further errors when they guess bedtime for themselves, so correcting the recorded bedtime improves accuracy. For light, deep and REM sleep, a single night's figure can be out by up to an hour, and some devices consistently over- or under-count a particular stage. Averaging several nights does not remove all of this error, because part of it is consistent for the same person on the same device. That same consistency is why the trend over weeks on one tracker is more informative than any single night, although trend accuracy itself has not been tested directly.

Chinoy ED et al. 2021, *Sleep* 44:zsaa291. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsaa291 (seven devices, 34 healthy young adults over three nights; deep-sleep limits of agreement roughly ±55–75 min for the better devices; accuracy worse on disrupted nights; flags errors from automatic bedtime detection)
Svensson T et al. 2024, *Sleep Med* 115:251–263. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2024.01.020 (Oura Gen 3, 96 adults; up to three nights averaged per person, deep-sleep limits still about ±65 min; mainly funded by Oura)
Robbins R et al. 2024, *Sensors* 24:6532. https://doi.org/10.3390/s24206532 (Oura, Fitbit, Apple Watch, 35 adults, one night; Apple undercounted deep sleep by 43 min on average; funded by Oura)
Schyvens AM et al. 2025, *Sleep Adv* 6:zpaf021. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleepadvances/zpaf021 (six devices; WHOOP 4.0 overcounted deep sleep by about 30 min on average; single night)
Kainec KA et al. 2024, *Sensors* 24:635. https://doi.org/10.3390/s24020635 (five devices; all poor at detecting wake after sleep onset)

**Does the Oura Ring have the most published validation research?**

Among consumer wearables, the Oura Ring has accumulated the most published validation
studies. A 2024 PSG-comparison study (96 participants, 421,045 epochs) validated the
Gen 3 device; a 2024 multi-device study rated it most accurate in four-stage sleep
staging (~79% agreement). The caveat that Oura may simply publish more than competitors
is fair given the proportion of Oura-funded studies in the literature.

Svensson T, Madhawa K et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine* 115:251–263.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2024.01.020
Robbins R, Weaver MD et al. 2024, *Sensors* 24(20):6532.
https://doi.org/10.3390/s24206532

## Chapter 12 — Supplements

**Does the form of a supplement matter?**

The same nutrient can come in different chemical forms, and they are not absorbed equally. For magnesium, organic forms such as citrate are generally absorbed better than inorganic forms such as oxide, which is the cheapest and most common. A systematic review supports this broad pattern, though the studies are small and measure absorption in different ways, and better absorption has not been shown to translate directly into better sleep.

Lindberg JS et al. 1990, *J Am Coll Nutr* 9:48–55. https://doi.org/10.1080/07315724.1990.10720349 (citrate more soluble and better absorbed than oxide)
Pardo MR et al. 2021, *Nutrition* 89:111294. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nut.2021.111294 (systematic review)

**Does phosphatidylserine reduce the evening cortisol response?**

Phosphatidylserine (PS) at 800 mg/day significantly blunted the ACTH and cortisol
response to physical exercise in a controlled trial. The evidence is specific to
exercise-induced cortisol, not the evening pre-sleep cortisol profile, so the
application to sleep is a reasonable extension rather than a directly demonstrated
effect. Soy- or sunflower-derived PS is the recommended current form (the brain-cortex
source used in the original trial is no longer available). Around 400 mg taken
30–60 minutes before bed is consistent with clinical dosing, though the original trial
used 400–800 mg under exercise-stress conditions.

Monteleone P et al. 1992, *Eur J Clin Pharmacol* 42:385–388.
https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00280123

**Does ashwagandha reduce stress and help sleep?**

Standardised ashwagandha root extracts have reduced self-reported stress and improved sleep in several small randomised trials, and a meta-analysis found a modest overall benefit for sleep, clearer at higher daily doses and over eight weeks or more. KSM-66 is the most widely studied extract, including a trial that found lower morning cortisol after two months, but other standardised extracts have trials of their own and none has been shown to outperform the others head to head. Trials usually split the dose across the day, so no best time of day has been established. Many trials were small, and the extract was often supplied by its manufacturer.

Cheah KL et al. 2021, *PLoS One* 16:e0257843. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0257843 (meta-analysis; effect clearer at 600 mg/day or more and 8 weeks or longer)
Chandrasekhar K, Kapoor J, Anishetty S 2012, *Indian J Psychol Med* 34:255–262. https://doi.org/10.4103/0253-7176.106022 (KSM-66, 300 mg twice daily, 64 adults with chronic stress, 60 days; morning serum cortisol fell 28% vs 8% on placebo)
Langade D et al. 2019, *Cureus* 11:e5797. https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.5797 (KSM-66, 300 mg twice daily, 60 adults with insomnia, 10 weeks; faster sleep onset and better sleep efficiency; extract supplied by manufacturer)

**Does magnesium L-threonate cross into the brain more effectively?**

Magnesium L-threonate is often recommended on the basis that it crosses the blood–brain barrier more readily than other forms. That advantage rests primarily on animal studies; human sleep RCT evidence for L-threonate specifically is very limited. Magnesium is widely recommended for sleep, but few high-quality studies support it, and the overall evidence that any form meaningfully improves sleep is low to moderate quality. The most direct human evidence on how it might work comes from a small trial in older adults with insomnia, where magnesium improved sleep measures and lowered cortisol; this may reflect correcting a shortfall common at that age rather than a benefit for everyone.

Mah J, Pitre T 2021, *BMC Complement Med Ther* 21:125. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-021-03297-z
Abbasi B et al. 2012, *J Res Med Sci* 17:1161–1169. https://doaj.org/article/0948678c0dcd4ff7a9fca8655625591f (46 older adults with insomnia, 500 mg/day for 8 weeks; better sleep efficiency and sleep onset, lower serum cortisol, higher melatonin; total sleep time unchanged)

**How much elemental magnesium is in a standard evening dose?**

Around 150–300 mg of elemental magnesium in the evening is within the range studied in
insomnia trials; a post-intervention reduction in sleep-onset latency of roughly
17 minutes was observed across eligible RCTs, though evidence quality is low. When taking
magnesium L-threonate, the label refers to the whole compound: a 2 g (2,000 mg) compound
serving delivers only approximately 144 mg of elemental magnesium (~8.3% by weight,
derived from the compound's molecular weight — this is a calculation, not a
separately-cited peer-reviewed measurement, though it is consistent with manufacturer
labelling). Citrate and glycinate forms are typically labelled by elemental content and
are directly comparable to the studied range.

Mah J, Pitre T 2021, *BMC Complement Med Ther* 21:125.
https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-021-03297-z
Liu G et al. 2016, *J Alzheimers Dis* 49(4):971–990 — research dosing for magnesium
L-threonate (MMFS-01). https://doi.org/10.3233/JAD-150538

## Chapter 13 — Your Sleep Environment

**Can light in the bedroom affect sleep without waking you?**

At room-light levels, yes. In a laboratory study, healthy young adults who slept one night with the overhead light on had a higher heart rate and a shift towards the body's alert state all night compared with sleeping in near-darkness, and spent less time in deep and REM sleep, without any increase in awakenings. They were also less sensitive to insulin the next morning. The light used was ordinary room lighting rather than a faint glow, melatonin was not measurably affected, and the study was small, so the effect of very dim sources such as standby lights is less certain.

Mason IC, Grimaldi D, Reid KJ et al. 2022, *PNAS* 119:e2113290119. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2113290119 (20 adults; one night at 100 lux vs under 3 lux)

**Why is temperature specifically "wired into the biology" of sleep?**

Temperature is embedded in sleep physiology in a way light and noise are not: the core
body temperature drop is part of the initiating mechanism of sleep, not merely a
condition to manage around it. Elevated ambient temperature increases wakefulness and
reduces both slow-wave and REM sleep, while the core temperature decline is most closely
aligned with NREM sleep onset. The chapter's other environmental factors — light and
noise — affect the sleep system primarily through signalling pathways; temperature
directly constrains the physiological conditions sleep requires to occur.

Harding EC, Franks NP, Wisden W 2019, *Front Neurosci* 13:336.
https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.00336
Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *J Physiol Anthropol* 31:14.
https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14

**What bedroom temperature do sleep sources recommend?**

Popular sleep sources commonly recommend a bedroom of around 18 °C, with ranges quoted from the mid-teens to about 20 °C. These figures are expert guidance rather than a precisely measured optimum, and they assume a typically covered sleeper; the comfortable room temperature shifts considerably with bedding and clothing, as the note on the 29 °C figure explains.

Sleep Foundation (US), *Best Temperature for Sleep* (updated 2025). https://www.sleepfoundation.org/bedroom-environment/best-temperature-for-sleep (recommends about 18.3 °C; doctors' range 18–20 °C)

**How much does body temperature drop at sleep onset?**

The physiology literature consistently places the core temperature decline at sleep
initiation at approximately 0.5–1 °C — the range the book gives, with "about one to two
degrees Fahrenheit" as the accurate conversion. The decline is not a single moment: core
temperature begins falling in the hour before sleep onset and continues through early
NREM sleep, with the steepest rate of change coinciding with the transition into
slow-wave sleep. Heat is shed outward through peripheral vasodilation — primarily via the
hands and feet — which is why cold extremities can slow sleep onset and why a warm bath
before bed (which drives peripheral vasodilation and accelerates the subsequent cooling)
can help initiate sleep.

Harding EC, Franks NP, Wisden W 2019, *Front Neurosci* 13:336.
https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.00336

**Does the body stop regulating its temperature during REM sleep?**

During REM sleep the brain's control of body temperature is largely suspended, so the body responds less to heat and cold and is more influenced by the room. This is well established in animal research; human studies are consistent with it, finding that a warm room increases wakefulness and reduces both REM and deep sleep. Applying the full mechanism to people is a reasonable extension rather than something measured directly.

Cerri M et al. 2017, *Front Physiol* 8:624. https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2017.00624 (review; evidence mainly from animal studies)
Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *J Physiol Anthropol* 31:14. https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14 (review of human studies on heat and sleep)

**Where does the 29 °C near-naked sleep figure come from?**

This figure traces to an early laboratory study in which six men slept in shorts only
with no bedding across five ambient temperatures; thermoneutrality — the least
disruptive condition — was around 29 °C. It is a small (n=6), dated, near-naked study,
useful for illustrating the principle that the body needs to shed heat, not for
prescribing a room temperature. Its insight is comparative: stripped of covers, the room
must supply all the warmth, so the comfortable point sits far above the 16–20 °C usually
quoted for a covered sleeper. The more robust finding from later work is that with
bedding, the "bed climate" against the skin stays roughly 32–34 °C across a wide range
of room temperatures — covering up matters more than room air temperature.

Haskell EH, Palca JW, Walker JM, Berger RJ, Heller HC 1981, *Electroencephalogr Clin
Neurophysiol* 51(5):494–501 (n=6, near-naked; illustrates the principle rather than
prescribing a room temperature).
https://doi.org/10.1016/0013-4694(81)90226-1
Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *J Physiol Anthropol* 31:14 (bed-climate stability
across room temperatures). https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14

**Do sudden noises disturb deep sleep without waking you?**

Often, yes. In a large laboratory study, recorded traffic noise during the night reduced deep sleep and increased brief arousals, which were far more common than full awakenings, so much of the disruption would go unnoticed. Louder and more abrupt noises had stronger effects, and although people partly adapted to the noise over several nights, their heart-rate responses to it did not fade.

Basner M, Müller U, Elmenhorst EM 2011, *Sleep* 34:11–23. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/34.1.11 (72 adults, 11 nights; arousals about 2.6 times as likely as awakenings; effects clearer at 55 dB and above)
WHO, *Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region*. https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/fact-sheets/item/noise

**Does a bedroom TV teach the brain that bed is for being awake?**

It can. Being awake and entertained in bed conditions the bed as a cue for alertness, which is why behavioural treatment for insomnia keeps the bed for sleep. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 17, in the note "Does CBT-i make getting out of bed when awake a central recommendation?"

**Is a TV's light less of a problem than its content?**

Probably. Expert consensus agrees that screen content harms sleep, but it did not agree on screen light. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 23, in the note "Does screen content matter more than screen light for delaying sleep?"

## Chapter 14 — Caffeine

**Does caffeine block the brain's sleep-pressure signal?**

Yes. Caffeine works mainly by blocking the brain's adenosine receptors, so the build-up of sleep pressure goes unread rather than cleared; the adenosine itself keeps accumulating. This mechanism is well established. Its effect on sleep can be large and easy to miss: in a controlled study, caffeine taken six hours before bed still shortened sleep by more than an hour, and participants did not notice.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-and-sleep
Drake C, Roehrs T, Shambroom J, Roth T 2013, *J Clin Sleep Med* 9:1195–1200. https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170 (12 healthy sleepers; 400 mg, about 2–3 cups; measured by home sleep monitor)

**Does caffeine wearing off make the afternoon slump worse?**

Partly. When caffeine wears off, the adenosine that built up behind it is felt again, so tiredness can return quickly. Regular users also develop tolerance, and a missed or delayed dose brings withdrawal fatigue and low alertness, which makes the next cup feel necessary. That the slump itself deepens over time is a reasonable reading of these effects rather than something measured directly.

Juliano LM, Griffiths RR 2004, *Psychopharmacology* 176:1–29. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-004-2000-x (review; fatigue and drowsiness are validated withdrawal symptoms, even after stopping 100 mg a day)
James JE, Rogers PJ 2005, *Psychopharmacology* 182:1–8. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-005-0084-6 (much of caffeine's apparent boost is reversal of withdrawal)

**What is caffeine's actual half-life?**

"Roughly five hours" is a reasonable estimate. The European Food Safety Authority's 2015
opinion gives approximately four hours as the average; a 2026 clinical pharmacokinetic
study measured it at 4.0 hours; the broader literature spans approximately 3–7 hours
depending on individual variation, medications (oral contraceptives and some SSRIs
prolong it notably), and pregnancy (which can extend it to 8–15 hours). The practical
implication of the calculation in this chapter — that caffeine from a mid-morning double
shot is still circulating at a meaningful level by evening — holds across the full range
of individual values, and is more pronounced for anyone toward the longer end of the
distribution.

Agboola OE et al. 2026, *Curr Ther Res Clin Exp* 104:100832.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.curtheres.2026.100832
EFSA 2015, *Scientific Opinion on the safety of caffeine*, *EFSA Journal* 13(5):4102.
https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine and Sleep*.
https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-and-sleep

**Does delaying the first coffee make caffeine work better?**

Waiting 60–90 minutes before the first cup is promoted on the grounds that drinking
during the natural cortisol-awakening surge and ongoing adenosine clearance wastes the
effect and builds tolerance faster. The morning cortisol-caffeine evidence base for this
specific recommendation is limited; no tracker-confirmed difference has been established.
It is a reasonable, low-confidence practical tip rather than established science.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine and Sleep*.
https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-and-sleep

**Does sleep loss reduce self-control?**

Sleep loss reliably impairs attention, decision-making and the ability to resist impulses, and sleep-deprived people are more likely to act on immediate urges.

Pilcher JJ, Morris DM, Donnelly J, Feigl HB 2015, *Front Hum Neurosci* 9:284. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00284 (review; evidence mostly from laboratory studies)

## Chapter 15 — Alcohol

**Is zero alcohol the recommended position for sleep?**

The zero recommendation the book references is the current general-health position —
major health bodies, including WHO, state that no amount of alcohol is safe for overall
health, and the book applies this direction to sleep. Sleep-specific guidance from sleep
foundations typically operationalises the same evidence as "drink as little as possible,
as infrequently as possible, finishing as early as possible" rather than a categorical
prohibition, because dose-dependent effects mean less is meaningfully better even when
zero is not the practical position. Both framings are defensible and consistent with the
evidence; the book presents both — the health-guidance floor, then the realistic
compromise.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*.
https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep
WHO (2023), *No level of alcohol consumption is safe for our health*.
https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/news/item/04-01-2023-no-level-of-alcohol-consumption-is-safe-for-our-health

**Does alcohol help you fall asleep, and is that real sleep?**

Alcohol does shorten the time it takes to fall asleep. It works as a sedative, though, and the sleep that follows is arranged differently from natural sleep: deep sleep is pulled into the early part of the night, REM is held back, and sleep becomes lighter and more broken later on.

Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549. https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006
Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Med Rev* 80:102030. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030

**Does alcohol affect deep sleep — and if so, how?**

Alcohol does not reduce slow-wave sleep; at most doses it increases it in the first half
of the night, and at higher doses the primary change is a reduced latency to deep sleep —
reaching it faster rather than getting more of it overall. This appears to be a sedative
artefact: alcohol's CNS depressant effects push the system into deeper non-REM states
earlier than it would naturally arrive. The cost comes in the second half of the night,
when the alcohol has metabolised and its suppression of REM sleep dominates. Early deep
sleep pulled forward by alcohol is not a bonus — it comes at the expense of the overall
architecture, displacing the REM that would have followed.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030
Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549.
https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006

**Does alcohol suppress REM and disrupt the second half of sleep?**

Alcohol reliably delays the onset of REM sleep and reduces its duration in a
dose-dependent manner, with disruption observed even at low doses (approximately two
standard drinks). In the second half of the night, alcohol causes increased sleep
disruption and more frequent awakenings. These two effects — REM suppression and
second-half fragmentation — are the robust and reproducible sleep costs of alcohol.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030
Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549.
https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006

major health bodies, including WHO, state that no amount of alcohol is safe for overall
health, and the book applies this direction to sleep. Sleep-specific guidance from sleep
foundations typically operationalises the same evidence as "drink as little as possible,
as infrequently as possible, finishing as early as possible" rather than a categorical
prohibition, because dose-dependent effects mean less is meaningfully better even when
zero is not the practical position. Both framings are defensible and consistent with the
evidence; the book presents both — the health-guidance floor, then the realistic
compromise.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*.
https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep
WHO (2023), *No level of alcohol consumption is safe for our health*.
https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/news/item/04-01-2023-no-level-of-alcohol-consumption-is-safe-for-our-health

**How much restorative sleep do one to four drinks cost?**

The dose-dependent effect is well supported. Even low doses — approximately two standard
drinks — produce measurable reductions in REM sleep, with disruption worsening at
approximately five standard drinks. A 2024 systematic review (Gardiner et al.) provides
the most comprehensive available quantification of these dose-response effects across
published studies. Specific per-drink minute estimates vary between studies and no single
authoritative pooled figure has been established, but the directional claim — that each
additional drink increases the cost, with a detectable effect starting as low as one to
two drinks — is robust.

Gardiner C et al. 2024, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 80:102030.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030
Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549.
https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006

**Why does alcohol wake you in the early hours?**

As alcohol is cleared during the night, its sedation lifts and the body swings the other way: the second half of the night after drinking brings more awakenings and lighter, broken sleep at every dose studied. Wearable studies also find a higher heart rate and lower heart-rate variability on nights after drinking, a sign the body is not settling into recovery, and this shows from the first hours of sleep rather than only at the rebound. "Metabolic rebound" is a descriptive label rather than a formal term, and when the wake comes depends on how much was drunk and when.

Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549. https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006
Pietilä J et al. 2018, *JMIR Ment Health* 5:e23. https://doi.org/10.2196/mental.9519 (large real-world sample of Finnish employees; heart rate up and heart-rate variability down in the first hours of sleep, dose-dependent)

**Does alcohol cause vivid dreams through REM rebound?**

At higher doses, the REM sleep held back early in the night tends to return later, as the alcohol is cleared, in a fragmented and easily interrupted form. Vivid dreams and nightmares after drinking are commonly reported and fit this pattern, though the link to dream intensity rests mainly on people's own reports rather than direct measurement.

Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549. https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep

**Does food slow how quickly alcohol is absorbed?**

Yes. Food in the stomach slows alcohol's passage into the small intestine, where most of it is absorbed, so blood alcohol rises more slowly and peaks lower than on an empty stomach. It does not reduce the total amount of alcohol the body has to process.

NIAAA, *Alcohol Metabolism*. https://www.niaaa.nih.gov/publications/alcohol-metabolism

## Chapter 16 — Food Timing

**Does digesting a meal work against the body's cooling for sleep?**

Digesting food raises the body's energy use for several hours, a process called diet-induced thermogenesis, and with it body temperature. Because a falling core temperature is part of how sleep begins, a substantial meal close to bed works against it.

Westerterp KR 2004, *Nutr Metab* 1:5. https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-7075-1-5 (review; the rise lasts several hours and is larger after protein; effect on pre-sleep cooling not measured directly)

**Does eating close to bed make sleep worse?**

The evidence points that way. In a randomised trial, young women who ate dinner one hour before bed rather than five had lower sleep efficiency and more time awake and briefly roused during the night. Large surveys link later last meals with poorer sleep, and eating within three hours of bed with more night waking.

Enomoto M et al. 2026, *J Physiol Anthropol* 45:12. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40101-026-00430-0 (13 young women, crossover; dinner 1 h vs 5 h before bed)
Chung N, Bin YS, Cistulli PA, Chow CM 2020, *Int J Environ Res Public Health* 17:2677. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17082677 (793 Sydney university students; cross-sectional)
Yan LM et al. 2024, *PLoS ONE* 19:e0308172. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0308172 (7,023 US adults; cross-sectional)

**Is three hours the right gap between dinner and bed?**

Sleep health guidance recommends allowing two to three hours between the last main meal and bed, and advises against going to bed either hungry or straight after eating; a small, light snack is fine. Three hours sits at the top of that range.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep

**Does hunger raise cortisol?**

Going without food raises cortisol, a clear finding across fasting studies, which is one reason sleep health guidance advises against going to bed hungry.

Nakamura Y et al. 2016, *Stress* 19:30–37. https://doi.org/10.3109/10253890.2015.1121984 (meta-analysis; studies involved fasting for a day or more)
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine, Food, Alcohol, Smoking and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-food-alcohol-smoking-and-sleep

**Does protein steady overnight blood sugar?**

Protein may help steady overnight blood sugar, particularly for people managing a glucose condition. Carbohydrates work differently: by raising insulin, they help tryptophan, a building block of melatonin, reach the brain, and a high-glycaemic evening meal shortened the time taken to fall asleep in one study. For most people, neither effect is large enough to make the choice of snack matter much.

Riley TM, Kris-Etherton PM, Hart TL, Petersen KS 2024, *J Nutr* 154:1219–1231. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tjnut.2024.01.021 (in healthy adults, snack type made little difference to overnight glucose)
Afaghi A, O'Connor H, Chow CM 2007, *Am J Clin Nutr* 85:426–430. https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.2.426 (small study; high-glycaemic meal four hours before bed)

## Chapter 17 — Can't-Sleep Routine

**Does CBT-i make getting out of bed when awake a central recommendation?**

Yes. Stimulus control, a core part of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, tells people to leave the bed when they cannot sleep, do something quiet elsewhere, and return only when sleepy, so the bed is kept for sleep. Repeated over weeks, this rebuilds the link between bed and sleep that nights of lying awake have weakened. Stimulus control is also recommended on its own as a treatment for chronic insomnia.

Edinger JD et al. 2021, *J Clin Sleep Med* 17:255–262. https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.8986 (AASM clinical practice guideline; stimulus control alone: conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence)
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Does lying awake frustrated keep you awake?**

Yes. Insomnia is understood as a state of heightened mental, emotional and physical arousal, and worry and frustration about not sleeping feed that arousal and keep the body alert.

Riemann D et al. 2010, *Sleep Med Rev* 14:19–31. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2009.04.002 (hyperarousal model, including raised stress-hormone activity)
Dressle RJ, Riemann D 2023, *J Sleep Res* 32:e13928. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13928 (updated review)

**What does ACT-i add to the advice to get up?**

Acceptance and commitment therapy for insomnia teaches people to accept being awake rather than fight it, because the struggle to sleep generates more arousal than the wakefulness itself. Reviews find it improves insomnia and sleep quality.

Salari N et al. 2020, *BMC Neurol* 20:300. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-020-01883-1 (systematic review of 19 studies)

**Why do mildly amusing shows work better than gripping ones?**

Predictable, low-stakes viewing gives the brain fewer reward signals to chase than gripping or interactive content. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 8, in the note "Is predictable TV genuinely less activating than interactive media?"

**Where do the lux figures in this footnote come from?**

Light's effect on melatonin follows a dose-response curve rather than a threshold. The
50% suppression point (ED50) across study populations is approximately 24–25 lux, the
group-level figure used in the footnote. Individual sensitivity ranges across more than
an order of magnitude: roughly 6–10 lux in the most sensitive people, up to around
350 lux in the least. The figures for a TV at three metres (~5 lux) and a phone held
close (~25–50 lux) apply the inverse-square principle and are illustrative rather than
directly measured at these exact positions. The practical conclusion — that a distant
screen is unlikely to affect even sensitive individuals, while a close-held phone sits
around the group-level ED50 — follows directly from these values.

Zeitzer JM et al. 2000, *J Physiol* 526:695–702.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10922269/
Phillips AJK et al. 2019, *PNAS* 116:12019–12024.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31138694/
Bauducco S et al. 2024, *Sleep Med Rev* 76:101933.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38657359/

**Does a TV across the room deliver far less light than a phone in your hand?**

A standard television at three metres delivers approximately 5 lux to the eye; a phone
held 30 cm away delivers approximately 25–50 lux. These are illustrative figures applying
the inverse-square principle, not pinned measurements — actual levels vary with screen
brightness, size, and ambient conditions. The underlying principle is well established: a
distant screen delivers far less light to the eye than a close-held one. (No primary
measurement study was identified for these exact values at these exact distances; the
figures are consistent with the light-sensitivity data in Zeitzer et al. 2000 and
Phillips et al. 2019, cited above.)

**How much does screen light delay sleep?**

In a controlled study, reading on a light-emitting tablet for four hours before bed, compared with a printed book, delayed sleep onset by about ten minutes, reduced REM sleep and left people groggier the next morning.

Chang AM, Aeschbach D, Duffy JF, Czeisler CA 2015, *PNAS* 112:1232–1237. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112 (12 adults; five nights in each condition)

**Does screen content matter more than screen light?**

Expert consensus agrees that pre-bed content harms sleep, but did not agree on screen light. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 23, in the note "Does screen content matter more than screen light for delaying sleep?"

## Chapter 18 — Morning Routine

**Is morning light the strongest cue for the body clock?**

Yes. Light reaching the eyes is the main signal the body clock uses to set itself to the day, and morning light shifts it earlier, so the time you wake and get light sets when melatonin rises that evening. Waking and getting light later pushes the whole cycle later.

Brown TM et al. 2022, *PLoS Biol* 20:e3001571. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571 (expert consensus recommendations on daytime and evening light)
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

**Is outdoor light much brighter than indoor light, even when it's overcast?**

Yes. Daylight outdoors is many times brighter than typical indoor lighting, even under cloud, and many indoor spaces fall short of the daytime light levels experts recommend for a well-set body clock.

Brown TM et al. 2022, *PLoS Biol* 20:e3001571. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571 (recommends bright daytime light at the eye; typical indoor levels often fall below it)

**Does morning movement help set the body clock alongside light?**

Morning exercise has modest circadian phase-shifting effects independent of light:
physical activity is recognised as a secondary zeitgeber — a time-of-day cue — that
reinforces the primary light signal. The book's framing ("its own gentle cue" that helps
alongside light) accurately reflects the evidence: movement acts as a complementary
zeitgeber rather than a measurable amplifier of the light response. The practical upshot
is the same: combining a morning walk with outdoor light provides two consistent
time-of-day signals rather than one.

Brown TM et al. 2022, *PLoS Biology* 20:e3001571.
https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571

**Can a light-therapy lamp stand in for morning sunlight?**

A bright light-therapy lamp used in the morning is an established treatment for body-clock and seasonal mood problems, and can stand in for outdoor light when getting outside isn't possible.

NCCIH, *Sleep Disorders and Complementary Health Approaches*. https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/sleep-disorders-and-complementary-health-approaches

**Does sleeping in throw off the body clock?**

Yes. Shifting your wake time shifts the timing of the evening melatonin rise. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 6, in the note "Does an irregular schedule throw off melatonin timing?"

**How long, and when, should a nap be?**

Short and not too late: about 20 minutes, well before the evening. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 21, in the note "When is it too late in the day to nap?"

## Chapter 19 — Evening Routine

**Are cortisol falling and melatonin rising by the evening?**

Yes. Cortisol declines through the day as melatonin begins to rise in the evening. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 7, in the note "Do cortisol and melatonin work against each other?"

**Does bright evening light push the sleep window later?**

Yes. Evening light delays the melatonin rise, and stimulating activity adds alertness on top. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 6, in the note "Does evening light delay melatonin?"

**Why keep the bed for sleep only?**

It strengthens the link between bed and sleep. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 17, in the note "Does CBT-i make getting out of bed when awake a central recommendation?"

**Does going to bed later than usual affect the next day?**

Yes. In a large study using wearable data, going to bed later than usual was linked to a higher resting heart rate through the night that carried into the following day, and the later the bedtime, the larger the effect. Going to bed earlier than usual did not show the same cost.

Faust L et al. 2020, *npj Digit Med* 3:39. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-020-0250-6 (557 university students, over 255,000 nights of Fitbit data; observational; the effect per night is small)

## Chapter 20 — Meditation

**Does regular meditation or slow breathing train the calming system?**

Yes. Regular slow-breathing practice makes the body's calming responses stronger over time: studies of heart-rate-variability training find the reflexes that steady heart rate become more responsive after weeks to months of practice. Mindfulness meditation also improves sleep quality in randomised trials.

Lehrer PM, Gevirtz R 2014, *Front Psychol* 5:756. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00756 (review of heart-rate-variability biofeedback)
Rusch HL et al. 2019, *Ann N Y Acad Sci* 1445:5–16. https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.13996 (meta-analysis of 18 randomised trials, 1,654 people; improved sleep quality compared with matched controls, and performed comparably to established sleep treatments)

**Are poor sleepers stuck in the activated state?**

Largely, yes. Insomnia is understood as a state of heightened arousal. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 17, in the note "Does lying awake frustrated keep you awake?"

**Does slow breathing work through the vagus nerve?**

Yes. The vagus nerve is the main pathway of the body's calming system, and slow breathing, especially a slow exhale, shifts the nervous system towards it, measurable as changes in heart rhythm. Breathing is the one automatic function that can also be taken over deliberately, which makes it the most direct lever on that system. Most of the vagus nerve's fibres carry information from the body up to the brain, so attending to bodily sensations engages it from that side as well.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817
Berthoud HR, Neuhuber WL 2000, *Auton Neurosci* 85:1–17. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1566-0702(00)00215-0 (about four-fifths of vagal fibres carry signals from body to brain)

**Does regular breathing practice train the vagus nerve to stay calmer?**

Slow breathing reliably shifts autonomic balance toward the parasympathetic (vagal) side —
a link well established in the HRV-biofeedback literature. The further step — that regular
practice keeps the cortisol response lower over time — is a reasonable inference from the
connection between vagal tone and stress regulation, but has not been directly
demonstrated. Read as "helps turn down" the stress response rather than "suppresses" it.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309.
https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817

**Does nasal breathing naturally slow the breath?**

Nasal breathing imposes higher airway resistance than mouth breathing, which reduces peak
airflow and naturally slows respiration rate. This mechanism is well established in
respiratory physiology.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309.
https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817

**Does a longer exhale slow the heart?**

Yes. Heart rate naturally rises on the in-breath and falls on the out-breath, a normal rhythm called respiratory sinus arrhythmia. Lengthening the exhale and pausing after it gives more of each breath to the slowing phase.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817

**Does the 528 Hz frequency itself aid sleep?**

A neutral cue repeated consistently during meditation can become a conditioned calm signal
through classical conditioning — that mechanism is sound. The separate claim that the
528 Hz frequency confers a physiological benefit beyond conditioning is supported only by
one small preliminary study: a crossover of nine participants found reduced salivary
cortisol and raised oxytocin at 528 Hz compared with 440 Hz. Given the very small sample,
single session, lower-tier journal, and absence of replication, this should be treated as
preliminary evidence only.

Akimoto K et al. 2018, *Health* 10:1159–1170 (SCIRP) — crossover, n=9. (Very small
sample, single session, lower-tier journal, unreplicated — treat as preliminary.)
https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperabs?paperid=87146

## Chapter 21 — Napping

**When is it too late in the day to nap?**

Standard guidance recommends avoiding naps within 7–8 hours of bedtime. The Sleep
Foundation (US) specifies "at least 8 hours before bedtime" and cautions against napping
after 3 p.m.; Mayo Clinic aligns with this. The Sleep Health Foundation (AU) endorses a
15–30 minute optimal nap duration.

Sleep Foundation (US), *Does Napping Impact Your Sleep at Night?* (rev. 2025).
https://www.sleepfoundation.org/how-sleep-works/does-napping-impact-sleep-at-night
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Napping*.
https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/napping
Mayo Clinic, *Napping: Do's and don'ts for healthy adults*.
https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/adult-health/in-depth/napping/art-20048319

**Do naps use up the sleep pressure you need for the night?**

Yes. Sleep of any kind, including a nap, clears some of the sleep pressure that has built up since waking. A short nap earlier in the day takes the edge off with little effect on the night, while a long or late nap leaves less pressure at bedtime and can make it harder to fall asleep.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Napping*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/napping

## Chapter 22 — Exercise

**Does exercise raise cortisol, and does it settle afterwards?**

Yes. Harder exercise raises cortisol, and in a well-recovered body it returns to baseline within hours. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 7, in the note "Does cortisol return to baseline once a demand passes?"

**Does hard exercise keep the body hot after you stop?**

Yes. Intense exercise raises core body temperature, and the body's ability to shed that heat stays reduced for a period after exercise ends, so it can take an hour or more to return to normal depending on how hard and how long the session was.

Kenny GP, McGinn R 2017, *J Appl Physiol* 122:933–944. https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00517.2016 (review of heat loss after exercise)

**Do sleep-deprived people recover more slowly?**

Sleep loss leaves cortisol higher the following evening, which adds to what exercise brings. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 7, in the note "Does a bad night raise cortisol the next day?"

**Does morning exercise suit the body's cortisol rhythm?**

Cortisol is naturally at its highest in the morning and falls through the day, so exercise early adds to a level that is already high, rather than raising it in the evening when it should be falling.

Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214 (daily cortisol rhythm; the timing benefit is inferred from the rhythm rather than tested directly)

**Does regular exercise lower cortisol and improve sleep quality?**

The book's claim is well supported. A systematic review and meta-analysis (De Nys et al.
2022) found regular physical activity reduces cortisol levels (standardised mean
difference −0.37) and improves sleep quality (SMD −0.30). The evidence base was
predominantly from breast-cancer patient populations, so the effect size for
otherwise-healthy people is less precisely characterised — but the directional finding,
that regular exercise supports healthier cortisol regulation and better sleep, is
consistent with the broader exercise-physiology literature.

De Nys L et al. 2022, *Psychoneuroendocrinology* 143:105843.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2022.105843

**How close to bedtime can you exercise vigorously?**

Research supports four hours as the evidence-based threshold: vigorous exercise ending
at least four hours before sleep onset is not associated with sleep changes, while
exercise within that window is linked to delayed sleep onset, shorter sleep, lower
quality, higher nocturnal heart rate, and lower HRV (from 14,689 active adults across
4,084,354 person-nights of wearable data). A personal margin of six hours is not
contradicted by the evidence — it simply exceeds the research floor — and is a
reasonable conservative choice for anyone sensitive to late training.

Leota J et al. 2025, *Nat Commun* 16:3297.
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58271-x
Stutz J, Eiholzer R, Spengler CM 2019, *Sports Med* 49:269–287.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-1015-0

**Do yoga and tai chi help sleep?**

Yoga and tai chi have been found to improve sleep quality in a number of studies. Both combine gentle movement with slow breathing and attention to the body, the same ingredients as the body scan. Gentle forms are low-intensity exercise, a level that does not raise cortisol, which makes them suited to the evening.

NCCIH, *Sleep Disorders and Complementary Health Approaches*. https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/sleep-disorders-and-complementary-health-approaches (evidence mostly from small studies)
Hill EE et al. 2008, *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587–591. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03345606 (cortisol rose at 60% and 80% of maximum aerobic capacity, not at 40%)

## Chapter 23 — Social Media

**Does screen content matter more than screen light for delaying sleep?**

On current evidence, yes. A 2024 National Sleep Foundation expert panel agreed that stimulating pre-bed screen content harms sleep but could not agree that screen light does, and blue-light filters address only the light. Screen light can delay sleep, but in controlled studies the effect is modest.

Hartstein LE et al. 2024, *Sleep Health* 10:373–384. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2024.05.001 (National Sleep Foundation consensus statement; focused on children and adolescents)
Chang AM et al. 2015, *PNAS* 112:1232–1237. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112 (four hours of tablet reading delayed sleep onset by about ten minutes)

**Do people react most strongly to provocative content?**

Yes. Across 17 countries, people showed stronger physical arousal when watching negative news than positive news, a consistent bias towards negative information.

Soroka S, Fournier P, Nir L 2019, *PNAS* 116:18888–18892. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1908369116 (measured skin conductance and heart rate while people watched news stories)

**Do social feeds serve more of what you react to?**

Yes. Feeds are built around unpredictable rewards and serve more of whatever holds attention. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 8, in the note "Are games and social feeds deliberately engineered for dopamine?"

## Chapter 24 — Hydration

**Does mild dehydration raise cortisol?**

Hypohydration is associated with significantly elevated cortisol (standardised mean
difference approximately 1.12) across 21 studies in systematic review and meta-analysis.
The strongest evidence comes from exercise- or heat-induced dehydration; the everyday
version — mild dehydration from simply not drinking enough — is a reasonable extension of
this finding rather than something directly demonstrated in passive-restriction studies
alone.

Zaplatosch ME, Adams WM 2020, *Nutrients* 12:2526.
https://doi.org/10.3390/nu12092526

**Does dehydration make it harder for the body to cool?**

Yes. Dehydration reduces sweating and blood flow to the skin, the body's main routes for shedding heat, so core temperature runs higher.

Sawka MN et al. (American College of Sports Medicine) 2007, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 39:377–390. https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597 (position stand; evidence from exercise and heat)

**Does cooling down help start sleep?**

Yes. A drop in core body temperature is part of how sleep begins. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 13, in the note "How much does body temperature drop at sleep onset?"

**Does cortisol make the bladder signal louder during lighter sleep?**

Nocturnal urine production is naturally suppressed by the overnight rise in vasopressin
(antidiuretic hormone), which peaks around midnight–4 a.m. During lighter or fragmented
sleep, the arousal threshold is lower, making the bladder signal more likely to break
through. The specific mechanism by which elevated cortisol contributes — by lightening
sleep — is a plausible inference but has not been directly demonstrated.

George CP, Messerli FH, Genest J, Nowaczynski W, Boucher R, Kuchel O, Rojo-Ortega JM
1975, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 41(2):332–338 — classic demonstration of the nocturnal
vasopressin rise. https://doi.org/10.1210/jcem-41-2-332
Andersson KE, Van Kerrebroeck P 2018, *Curr Urol Rep* 19:8.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s11934-018-0750-y

**Can you rely on thirst, and what does urine colour tell you?**

Thirst tends to lag behind the body's need for fluid, so waiting until you feel thirsty means falling slightly behind. Urine colour is a simple day-to-day guide: pale straw indicates good hydration and dark yellow indicates you need more.

NHS inform, *Hydration*. https://www.nhsinform.scot/campaigns/hydration/

**Is around two litres a day a sensible target?**

Yes. European dietary guidance sets adequate daily water intake at about two litres for women and two and a half for men, with roughly a fifth usually coming from food, which leaves around one and a half to two litres to drink. Needs rise with heat and activity.

EFSA 2010, *Dietary Reference Values for water*, *EFSA Journal* 8:1459. https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459

## Chapter 25 — Sugar and Food Sensitivities

**Does sleep loss reduce willpower?**

Yes. Sleep loss impairs the ability to resist impulses. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 14, in the note "Does sleep loss reduce self-control?"

**Does poor sleep make you hungrier, especially for sweet food?**

Yes. In a controlled study, two nights of short sleep raised the hunger hormone ghrelin, lowered the fullness hormone leptin, and increased appetite, most of all for calorie-dense, carbohydrate-rich foods such as sweets. Ghrelin also acts on the brain's reward system, increasing the pull of food cues.

Spiegel K et al. 2004, *Ann Intern Med* 141:846–850. https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-141-11-200412070-00008 (12 healthy young men; two nights of four hours in bed vs ten; later studies vary on leptin)
Malik S, McGlone F, Bedrossian D, Dagher A 2008, *Cell Metab* 7:400–409. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2008.03.007 (ghrelin increased brain reward-area responses to food pictures)

**Can a high-sugar diet affect the cortisol system over time?**

There are signs it can. A glucose drink before a stressful task amplifies the cortisol response, people who habitually eat more sugar show a weaker cortisol response to stress, and people with type 2 diabetes have a flatter daily cortisol rhythm with higher cortisol at bedtime. Together these suggest the blood-sugar and cortisol systems shape each other over time.

Gonzalez-Bono E et al. 2002, *Horm Behav* 41:328–333. https://doi.org/10.1006/hbeh.2002.1766 (37 fasted healthy men, 8–10 per group; glucose, not protein or fat, amplified the cortisol response to a public-speaking stress test)
Di Polito N et al. 2023, *Nutrients* 15:209. https://doi.org/10.3390/nu15010209 (54 adults; higher habitual sugar intake linked to a weaker cortisol response; self-reported diet)
Hackett RA, Steptoe A, Kumari M 2014, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 99:4625–4631. https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2014-2459 (Whitehall II, 3,508 adults; cross-sectional)

**Can an evening sugar hit cause a 2–3 a.m. wake?**

Reactive (postprandial) hypoglycaemia is a recognised phenomenon in which the insulin
response to a carbohydrate load overshoots, driving blood glucose below fasting baseline
roughly two to four hours after eating. The full evening-sugar-to-2 a.m.-waking chain is
built from established physiological parts — reactive hypoglycaemia is real, the brain
triggers a cortisol counter-regulatory response to falling glucose, and cortisol runs
higher in the small hours anyway — but the complete sequence has not been demonstrated
end-to-end in healthy people.

Stuart K, Field A, Raju J, Ramachandran S 2013, *Case Rep Med* 2013:273957.
https://doi.org/10.1155/2013/273957
Sweatt SK et al. 2026, *Nutrients* 18(5).
https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12986748
Verberne AJ, Sabetghadam A, Korim WS 2014, *Front Neurosci* 8:38.
https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2014.00038

**Do food sensitivities generate a background immune response?**

Non-IgE-mediated food disorders are clinically recognised, and diet-driven low-grade
systemic inflammation is established in the literature. The specific mechanism — that
individual food sensitivities sustain a persistent background immune response — is
contested and overlaps with the territory of food-specific IgG testing, which the same
chapter correctly identifies as scientifically disputed. The book's hedge ("what may be a
low-grade immune response") is appropriate.

Myszkowska D et al. 2021, *Medicina (Kaunas)* 57(11):1245 — non-IgE hypersensitivity.
https://doi.org/10.3390/medicina57111245
Minihane A-M, Vinoy S et al. (Calder PC) 2015, *Br J Nutr* 114(7):999–1012 — dietary
patterns and chronic low-grade inflammation.
https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114515002093

**Can food sensitivity cause disrupted sleep and brain fog?**

Food intolerance can produce disrupted sleep, fatigue, brain fog, bloating, and nasal
congestion (nasal congestion independently fragments sleep by impairing breathing). These
symptoms are real but highly nonspecific — many conditions produce the same picture.
Ruling out coeliac disease and other organic causes is the appropriate first step before
attributing them to food sensitivity.

NHS inform, *Food Intolerance*. https://www.nhsinform.scot/
Altinsoy C, Dikmen D 2025, *Medicina (Kaunas)* 61(2):344 — brain-fog severity correlated
with GI symptoms and mood; cross-sectional, not a causal demonstration.
https://doi.org/10.3390/medicina61020344

**Are gluten, dairy, eggs, and soy the most common triggers?**

Wheat (gluten), milk (dairy), eggs, and soy are all among the FDA's nine major food
allergens and consistently top clinical food-intolerance lists. Their prominence is
consistent across guidance sources, though reported frequency varies by population and by
how "reactivity" is defined.

NHS inform, *Food Intolerance*. https://www.nhsinform.scot/
Harvard Health, *Food Allergy vs. Food Intolerance*. https://www.health.harvard.edu/

**Is an elimination diet the best way to find a food sensitivity?**

Yes. Removing a suspected food for a few weeks and then reintroducing it is the standard clinical approach for identifying food intolerances, and no blood test replaces it.

Cleveland Clinic, *Elimination Diet*. https://health.clevelandclinic.org/elimination-diet

**Are IgG food-sensitivity tests reliable?**

Testing of IgG4 to foods is considered irrelevant for diagnosing food allergy or
intolerance and should not be performed, per the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical
Immunology Task Force. The American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology holds the
same position.

Stapel SO et al. (EAACI Task Force) 2008, *Allergy* 63:793.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1398-9995.2008.01705.x
American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (AAAAI).
https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/igg-food-test
