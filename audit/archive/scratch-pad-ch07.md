## Stage 2 — Six-Category Review

### Category 1 — Contradictions

**Contradiction 1 — Two cortisols: reactive vs proactive**

The new bridge passage introduces cortisol as a reactive hormone — it mobilises you for anything that feels urgent: "Cortisol mobilises you for anything that feels like it needs acting on right now." This frames cortisol as a demand-driven response.

Four paragraphs later, the Wake Executive framing presents cortisol differently: "Before you've even opened your eyes in the morning, it's already rising." This is proactive cortisol — it follows a built-in daily clock regardless of whether anything urgent is happening.

Both are true but the chapter presents them as one continuous story without acknowledging they describe two different cortisol functions: a baseline rhythm (rises and falls on a clock) and a reactive component (spikes in response to demand). A careful reader may notice the inconsistency — the bridge says cortisol responds to urgency, the Wake Executive says it runs before you even wake up.

*Proposed fix:* One bridging sentence connecting the two. Something the author needs to supply. Shape: cortisol has a rhythm AND a reactive layer — the rhythm is the floor, the spikes are the response. The spike-and-return paragraph already covers the reactive layer; the fix is just a cleaner handover from the bridge to the Wake Executive intro.

---

**Contradiction 2 — Caffeine timing: now largely resolved**

The earlier caffeine contradiction (CAR paragraph says "too early" is the problem; caffeine paragraph implied every cup) is largely resolved by the "too early or too late" edit. The CAR paragraph covers the morning end; the caffeine paragraph covers both ends. These are now consistent.

Residual flag only: "Replacing the body's own rise with a chemical one" specifically addresses the morning/too-early problem. The caffeine paragraph then covers the evening/too-late problem. The chapter presents these as separate paragraphs without explicitly linking them as two manifestations of the same issue. Low priority — the reader can connect them — but the two paragraphs would benefit from a one-clause bridge if the author wants to make the logic airtight.

### Category 3 — Weak Points (2+/5 only)

**Weak Point 1 — 2am re-onset mechanism absent [3/5]**

The chapter's central personal story is waking at 2am and being unable to return to sleep. The chapter explains the reactive cortisol spike (cortisol mobilises for anything urgent, including opportunities) and connects it to the 2am experience. It then states "The problem wasn't that cortisol was running. It was when."

But the handover paragraph ("Cortisol and melatonin should work in opposition — in the evening as cortisol falls, melatonin rises") describes the initial sleep-onset problem at bedtime, not the mid-night re-onset problem. The chapter never explicitly states why high cortisol at 2am makes it impossible to return to sleep. The implicit mechanism — a cortisol spike at 2am overrides the melatonin-dominant sleep state, collapsing the condition that was allowing sleep — is never stated.

The reader can infer it, but the chapter sets up "the problem was when" and the reader expects the follow-through to explain WHY 2am cortisol is the problem. The handover delivers the evening version of that explanation, not the 2am version.

*Proposed fix:* One sentence in or after the handover that bridges to the 2am situation. Shape: "The same logic applies at 2am — a cortisol spike there collapses whatever melatonin state was holding sleep in place." Author to supply in their own voice.

---

**Weak Point 2 — "harder to catch" ambiguous [2/5]**

Line 16: "A busy mind full of half-formed plans didn't have a solution. The feel-good ideas made it harder to catch."

"Catch" has no clear antecedent. Harder to catch the problem? The diagnosis? The applicability of insomnia advice? The author likely means: the productive framing made it easier to dismiss as not-insomnia, so the problem went unrecognised longer. But the reader has to work to get there — "catch" is doing quiet work that could easily be missed.

*Proposed fix:* Minor reword. Shape: "The feel-good ideas made it easier to explain away" or "made it harder to name as a problem." Author to supply.

---

**Weak Point 3 — Caffeine paragraph: mechanism mismatch for "too early" [2/5]**

"Every cup taken too early or too late is a mild cortisol stimulus — a small kick to a system that should be gradually falling toward evening."

The "gradually falling toward evening" framing describes the too-late mechanism correctly. But the too-early mechanism is different: the CAR paragraph explains it as substitution (replacing the body's own rise with a chemical one), not as a cortisol stimulus in the evening-decline sense. The sentence bundles both problems under one descriptor and one framing that only fits one of them.

A careful reader will notice that "gradually falling toward evening" doesn't apply to 7am coffee. The too-early mechanism needs its own phrase, or the two should be separated.

*Proposed fix:* Split into two clauses: "Every cup too early blunts the body's own rise. Every cup too late keeps the baseline higher than it should be." Author to adapt.
