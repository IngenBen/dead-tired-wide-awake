## STAGE 1 — Beat analysis

### Main messages of the chapter

1. Alcohol feels like it helps sleep, but it sedates rather than induces real sleep — the felt benefit ends the moment you fall asleep.
2. The mechanism, in three movements: first part of the night it suppresses REM and fragments deep sleep (cutting restorative sleep and adenosine clearance); second part it triggers metabolic rebound (the 4am wake-up); then REM rebound (vivid dreams).
3. The damage scales with your reserve — a rested sleeper absorbs it, someone in sleep debt doesn't.
4. The author's compromise: zero is optimal, but the honest, liveable position is a dry working week plus one to two drinks on weekends, early and with food.

The spine is sound and the three-movement mechanism is the strongest part of the chapter. Problems below are at the seams, not the structure.

**Beat finding 1 — REVISED after author challenge. Original cut withdrawn; downgraded to a minor phrasing echo.**

*Original finding (overruled):* I proposed cutting the good-sleeper/poor-sleeper contrast from l33 as a duplicate of l37. That was wrong. The l33 sentence (*"For a good sleeper the rebound might mean a patch of light sleep before drifting off again"*) is load-bearing — it sets up l35's opening, *"If you do sleep through the metabolic rebound, you hit REM rebound."* Cutting it strands that bridge.

*What's actually going on:* the two beats sit on different axes, so neither is redundant —
- **l33** — does the rebound *wake you*, or do you drift back through it? (mechanical; feeds the REM-rebound beat at l35)
- **l37** — does the lost sleep *matter* the next day? (reserve / sleep debt)

The only genuine overlap is surface phrasing: the *"For a good sleeper… / For someone already…"* two-part construction is used at both l33 and l37. Read aloud, the second instance can feel like a template reused. l37 also states the contrast twice within itself (open + "This is why some people…"). Fix at l37 only; leave l33 alone.

*Recommended change (l37):*

> If you do sleep through the metabolic rebound, you hit REM rebound. The earlier suppression of REM springs back often creating more intense, vivid, sometimes disturbing dreams. It happens in the early hours providing more disruption to your sleep. If you've ever woken from an unusually vivid dream after a night of drinking, that's what you were experiencing.

> ~~For a consistently good sleep – without sleep debt — one night of disrupted sleep may not matter much. For someone already running on empty, with little to no reserve, it's a substantial blow. This is why some people seem to wake up fine after a few drinks while others — and I am reliably in this camp — wake up feeling terrible.~~ **How much all this costs you depends on your reserve. With no sleep debt, one disrupted night may not matter much. Running on empty, it's a substantial blow. It's why some people wake up fine after a few drinks while others — and I'm reliably in this camp — wake up feeling terrible.**

> **What I actually do**
