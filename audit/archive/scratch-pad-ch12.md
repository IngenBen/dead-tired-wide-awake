## STAGE 1 — Beat analysis

### Main messages of the section
1. The headline "set your room to ~18 °C" advice is incomplete and, for the air-conditioner-less
   reader, impractical.
2. What your body actually responds to is the **bed climate** — the temperature against your
   skin under the covers — not the room air.
3. Practical upshot: keep the room at or below ~29 °C (almost any room qualifies) and do the
   real tuning with bedding and clothing.

The arc is sound and the personal grounding (no A/C, cotton layers, the fan) is present and
load-bearing — meets the explainer-register standard in author-style-notes.

### Problem beats (only problems reported)

**Beat problem 1 — the "room isn't the real lever" point is landed four times (B6, B10, B11,
B12).** This is the one genuine re-explaining issue. The idea that the precise room number
doesn't matter is stated as setup (B6), as the earned payoff (B10), as reassurance (B11: "the
room itself is rarely your real problem"), and again in the retained A/C line (B12: "the exact
temperature setting matters less than…"). B6 (setup) and B10 (payoff) are the two that earn
their place — B10 is the strongest because the 29-vs-18 reveal has just set it up. The
restatements in B11 and B12 should be trimmed so the point lands twice, not four times.

Fix — trim the restatement in B11 (keep the useful, non-duplicated reassurance that most rooms
already qualify; drop the explicit "room isn't your problem" echo):

Before (B10):
> Which leads to a conclusion that sounds wrong but isn't: a "perfect" 18-degree bedroom can
> give you worse sleep than a warm 29-degree one — if at 18 degrees you're buried under a duvet
> that's too thick. The room was never really the problem. The bed climate was.

Amended (B11):
> So the practical target is ~~refreshingly loose~~ **simple**: get your bedroom to roughly 29
> degrees or below — **which almost any room already manages.** ~~and most bedrooms already
> are, so the room itself is rarely your real problem.~~ From there, you tune the bed climate
> with the two things you can actually control: your bedding and what you wear.

After (B12, unchanged):
> If you're lucky enough to have air conditioning in the bedroom, this might be easier to
> manage. Same bedding each night, same thermostat temperature. The exact temperature setting
> matters less than simply not feeling too hot or too cold.

(Transition/repetition self-check on the fix: "which almost any room already manages" keeps the
reassurance without re-asserting "the room isn't the problem," so it no longer echoes B10; the
hand-off into B12's A/C paragraph is unchanged and still clean. Passes.)

Note for Stage 2: the fourth landing (B12's "exact temperature setting matters less than…") is
retained book prose; whether to trim it too is better judged in the Over-explaining category,
where the A/C paragraph can be weighed as a whole.

**Beat problem 2 (minor) — B1 pre-empts B8.** B1's "cool down to fall asleep *and to stay
there*" states the all-night idea that B8 is the dedicated beat for. Not a true duplication
(B1 is the compressed thesis; B8 does the work), but the phrase slightly spends the payoff
early. Low priority — flag only; the thesis framing is defensible and the author favours
landing a point then expanding it. No rewrite proposed unless you want the thesis tightened to
"cool down to fall asleep" and let B8 own the staying-asleep beat.

**Voice flag (carry into Stage 2 cadence) — "refreshingly loose".** Mild drift toward
wellness-brand polish; author-style-notes prefer plainness over polish. Folded into the Fix
above ("simple"). Confirm in cadence pass.

All other beats are relevant and pull their weight — no further beat-level issues.

## STAGE 2 — six-category review

Cross-chapter check completed: convergence-of-signals callback (B7) is consistent with its
origin in ch06 (which already names "core body temperature falling" as one of the converging
signals); ch15 carries no temperature/bed-climate content (no cross-chapter duplication of this
material); the REM-stops-regulating point appears only in ch12.

### 1. Contradictions

No logical contradictions. The apparent tension between "the specific room temperature isn't the
thing to chase" (B6) and "get the room to 29 or below" (B11) is not a contradiction — 29 °C is a
loose ceiling, not a precise target, and the text frames it that way. The 16–20 °C popular range
and the 29 °C study figure don't conflict either; the section explicitly explains the difference
(covered vs near-naked sleeper).

**Contradiction 1 (factual / unit nit).** "16 and 20 degrees Celsius (roughly 60 to 67 degrees
Fahrenheit)" — the conversion is slightly off. 16–20 °C is about 61–68 °F, not 60–67 °F. (The
60–67 °F figure is what the *Fahrenheit-native* sources quote, which converts to ~15.5–19.5 °C.)
Pick one anchor. Simplest fix — round the Fahrenheit to match the Celsius:

> I want to offer a more honest version of that argument. *(prior context — section opener)*

> Most popular sources quote an optimal range of somewhere between 16 and 20 degrees Celsius
> (roughly 60 to ~~67~~ **68** degrees Fahrenheit), with around 18 degrees — about 65 Fahrenheit
> — most often singled out as the target. I think that advice could be better, for two reasons.

> First, it says nothing about what you're wearing or sleeping under. *(following context)*

(Self-check: one-digit change, no transition or repetition effect. Passes.)

### 2. Cadence and flow

**Cadence 1.** B6 runs four dense sentences and buries the key term ("bed climate") at the end of
a long block — against the author's habit of frequent breaks and short, punchy landings. Split the
definition onto its own line so the new term lands. (Combined with Over-explaining 1 below, which
also trims B6's doubled opening.)

> So here's the version that actually helped me — the bit beyond the headlines. *(prior context)*

> ~~The specific room temperature isn't really the thing to chase. Getting the room temperature
> right is only one factor.~~ **The room itself is only one factor — and not the one to fixate
> on.** What matters is that the whole setup — room temperature, what you're wearing, your bed
> coverings — lets your body drop and maintain a comfortable skin temperature throughout the
> night.
>
> **The temperature that actually counts isn't the air in the room. It's the warm pocket trapped
> between your skin and your covers — your bed climate.**

> Before you fall asleep, your core body temperature begins to drop… *(following context)*

(Self-check: the standalone definition line is a short, emphatic landing in keeping with the voice;
hand-off into the core-temp paragraph is unchanged and clean. Passes.)

### 3. Weak points

**Weak point 1.** B11's "which almost any room already manages" quietly overclaims for the very
reader the section was built for — the no-air-conditioning reader in a hot climate, who may sit
*above* 29 °C on summer nights (which is exactly why the fan beat, B15, exists). Soften so it
doesn't contradict the section's own audience, and so it sets up B15:

> The room was never really the problem. The bed climate was. *(prior context — B10)*

> So the practical target is simple: get your bedroom to roughly 29 degrees or below — which
> ~~almost any room already manages~~ **most rooms manage most of the year**. From there, you tune
> the bed climate with the two things you can actually control: your bedding and what you wear.

> If you're lucky enough to have air conditioning… *(following context)*

(Self-check: "most rooms, most of the year" now foreshadows the hot-nights fan beat instead of
undercutting it. Passes.)

**Weak point 2.** B9 leans on a bare "studies of people sleeping near-naked… around 29 degrees"
with no attribution. The author's register tolerates plain science, but an unsourced "studies"
is the weak kind. The 29 °C figure is the Haskell-type thermoneutral point (tested floor 21 °C),
so it needs a note. Recommend a working endnote token after the sentence — ⟦EN:Ch12.12⟧ — sourcing
Haskell 1981 (and, if you want a second line, Okamoto-Mizuno 2012's bed-climate range). No prose
change required beyond the token; the in-text wording stays as is.

### 4. Over-explaining

**Over-explaining 1.** B6 opens with two sentences making the same point ("isn't really the thing
to chase" / "only one factor"). Handled in Cadence 1 above — the merge removes the duplicate
opening.

**Over-explaining 2.** B12 retains "The exact temperature setting matters less than simply not
feeling too hot or too cold" — the fourth statement of the don't-chase-the-number idea (B6 and B10
already own it). Cut it; the two short fragments before it land the paragraph better on their own:

> So the practical target is simple… your bedding and what you wear. *(prior context — B11)*

> If you're lucky enough to have air conditioning in the bedroom, this might be easier to manage.
> Same bedding each night, same thermostat temperature. ~~The exact temperature setting matters
> less than simply not feeling too hot or too cold.~~

> If you don't have air conditioning, and I didn't… *(following context)*

(Self-check: "Same bedding each night, same thermostat temperature." is a clean fragment-rhythm
landing and hands off to the no-A/C beat without a gap. Passes.)

### 5. Repetition

**Repetition 1.** "room temperature" appears three times inside B6 ("specific room temperature",
"Getting the room temperature right", "the air in the room"). The Cadence 1 / Over-explaining 1
rewrite already drops this to one use plus "the room itself" — resolved there.

**Repetition 2 (minor).** "too warm" sits in consecutive paragraphs — B7 ("conditions that are too
warm") and B8 ("Too warm and it struggles"). Low priority. Optional vary:

> …A falling core temperature is one of them. Your body needs to shed heat outward through your
> hands and feet, and conditions that are too warm prevent that from happening efficiently, making
> it harder to fall asleep. *(prior context — B7)*

> And the job isn't done once you're out. Your body has to hold that lower temperature all night.
> ~~Too warm and it struggles~~ **Overheat, and it struggles**, fragmenting sleep and pushing you
> toward awakenings.

> Especially during REM sleep… *(following context)*

(Self-check: removes the back-to-back "too warm"; no new repetition introduced. Passes. Optional.)

No cross-chapter repetition flagged: the convergence-of-signals callback (B7) deliberately echoes
ch06 and is signposted with "Remember…", which is the author's intended callback device, not
accidental repetition.

### 6. Abrupt transitions

**Abrupt transition 1.** B8 → B9 jumps from "a hot, stuffy night… vivid, restless dreams" straight
into "The clearest illustration comes from studies of people sleeping near-naked" — a pivot from
the maintenance/REM beat back to the room-number argument with no bridge. Add one short bridging
line that also voices the reader's natural question:

> …It's why a hot, stuffy night sometimes comes with vivid, restless dreams. *(prior context — B8)*

> **So how warm is too warm? It depends almost entirely on your covers.** The clearest illustration
> comes from studies of people sleeping near-naked — just shorts, no covers at all. For them, the
> ideal room temperature turned out to be around 29 degrees Celsius.⟦EN:Ch12.12⟧ Strip away the
> bedding and the room has to do all the work itself, so it has to be far warmer than the headline
> numbers suggest.

> Which leads to a conclusion that sounds wrong but isn't… *(following context — B10)*

(Self-check: the rhetorical question is used once and earns its place; it links "too warm" to the
covers theme that drives B9–B11. No new repetition. Passes.)

No other abrupt transitions: B5→B6, B10→B11, B11→B12, B13→B14 all read cleanly.
