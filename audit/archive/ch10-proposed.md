# PART 4 – The habits

Everything that follows in Part 4 of this book is based on the three jobs from the last chapter: training the body clock, building the calming system, and stopping activation at the wrong times. The habits come one at a time.

# Phase 1 – get set up
# Chapter 10 - Your Feedback Loop

A friend called me out of the blue one afternoon. A keen runner who tracks everything — "Do you still struggle with sleep? Interested in trying a tracker? I'll post it to you."

I'd thought about trackers before and talked myself out of it. I already knew I was tired. I didn’t need a device to tell me that. And the family budget had enough going on without adding another gadget to the pile. What was the point?

But since there was no cost, I thought *why not give it a try*.

On reflection, if I trace the chain of events that led to this book — the research, the habits, the program, the results — that phone call is where it all began. One small act of thoughtfulness from a friend.

What I didn't know at that time was how useful a tracker could be. I assumed it would just give me a few good-to-know numbers which showed how badly I slept. And that wouldn't change anything.

When the data started coming in, the first thing I noticed surprised me. Some nights I was getting more than five hours — fragmented, interrupted, but more than five hours nonetheless. But even on these 'good' nights, the tracker told me deep sleep was very low.

That sent me down a path I hadn't anticipated. All of a sudden, I had new questions to ask. Tiredness just tells you something is wrong. The data was giving me something specific to  look at. And something to act on: change one thing, then watch the next few nights to see if it moved.

That's what a sleep tracker is: a feedback loop. Clearly, its not going to fix your sleep. But over time it shows you whether the habits you're building are moving you in the right direction.

For some people having a tracker can make them obsessed and more anxious. Sometimes unnecessarily so, because the trackers aren't perfectly accurate night to night — a single bad reading may mean less than it seems.

If this is you, try to reframe you’re thinking - don’t look every morning for a pass or a fail from last nights sleep. Instead, take each day as an interesting data point. Add it to your cumulative learnings and look at trends over time.

This matters most at the beginning of the program, before you've changed anything. The tracker gives you a baseline — a picture of where you're starting from. As the habits build, it becomes the mechanism that tells you what's working. Without it, you're making changes in the dark… pun intended.

When I first opened the app, I tried to understand everything at once. There were dozens of metrics  — heart rate variability, respiratory rate, sleep stages, efficiency scores, strain, recovery — and I had no idea which ones mattered or in what order. It was overwhelming.

So here is what I'd tell someone starting out: ignore most of it, at least for now. There's a natural order to this, and it starts simple. The first things to look at are the basics:

How many hours are you actually sleeping?

Are you going to bed and waking up at roughly the same time each day?

What is your sleep efficiency?  (time in bed asleep vs awake - good target is above 85%)

These three measures — duration, consistency, efficiency — are a great place to begin when you’re first starting out. When you're well short of what you need, almost any habit lifts total duration first — so it's the number that moves soonest, and the right one to watch at the start.

Once those are in a reasonable range, you can start paying attention to quality, which matters just as much. It simply comes second. This is where sleep stages become useful — not as precise measurements, but as trends. The habits ahead are what move it; for now just focus on getting more hours.

Deep sleep is where the body does its physical repair work. REM sleep is central to memory consolidation and emotional processing. Together they're called restorative sleep; the rest of the night is lighter sleep. 

The exact numbers will vary night to night and the trackers aren't perfectly accurate on staging — more on that in a moment — but watching whether your deep sleep is trending up or down over weeks tells you something tiredness alone doesn’t.

This is also where your habit changes show up once the hours are steady. Adjusting when you have your last coffee, your last drink, how close to bed you exercise — these register in your quality metrics even if you don't always feel the difference.

The third layer — heart rate, heart rate variability, recovery scores — is for later. Heart rate variability (hrv) in particular is worth understanding: it reflects how well your nervous system is balancing its two modes – activated and calm (the names in this book, not the scientific names). A higher variability generally means better recovery and a more resilient system. But this is a refinement metric, not a starting point.

**What trackers actually get right**

Here's the thing about sleep tracker accuracy: the studies are all over the place. Some suggest the devices are unreliable. Others suggest they're close enough to be genuinely useful. Reading the research without context, you could talk yourself out of tracking altogether.

The evidence actually points to something more nuanced than either camp admits. There are two distinct things trackers measure, and they're not equally accurate. I’m generalising here to keep things simple.

The first is total sleep time. They typically get this right — with one exception. If you spend a lot of time lying very still while awake, some devices will count it as sleep. My WHOOP has occasionally logged me watching television on the couch as light sleep. The fix is straightforward: adjust your recorded bedtime to reflect when you actually got into bed. Once you do that, total sleep time is reliable.

The second is sleep stage classification — how much of your night was light, deep, or REM. Here the trackers are less precise. The validation studies I've seen put the nightly error around twenty to thirty minutes, narrowing to roughly ten once you average across a week. Which means the nightly number is noisy, and the weekly trend is more important. 

From my own experience, they're accurate enough to tell you whether a habit is working.

One other thing worth knowing: as of writing, the Oura Ring has the most published research behind it. That might mean it's the most accurate tracker on the market. Or it might simply mean it's the one that's invested most heavily in publishing its findings. Either way, if you want the most evidence-backed option, it's a reasonable place to start.

**What I use**

I use the WHOOP. Partly because it has a strong research reputation. But honestly, mostly because it's less bulky than the Apple Watch to wear to bed. That's the kind of consideration that doesn't make it into the product reviews but ends up mattering every single night.

At some point I'll test it side by side with the Oura Ring. When I do, I'll update the app.

What I can tell you is what it did for me in the early months of the program. I was experimenting with supplements — trying different combinations, different timings, on for a couple of nights, off for a couple of nights. It wasn't perfect science. Things don't always work in isolation, and there were other variables I couldn't fully control. But when I could see some kind of on/off correlation showing up in my total sleep time, that was good enough to count as a step forward. The tracker made that visible.

Even though it was the deep sleep number that first got me excited about tracking, once I got going, I paired back and I focused almost entirely on quantity. Total hours felt most urgent, so that's what I watched.

When you’re getting much less sleep than you need, all habits are going to help with total duration. Quality matters just as much (ie. deep and REM) but the comes later. Trying to optimise everything at once is too overwhelming. Start simple – just focus on total hours of sleep.

So here is what I'd suggest before you move on.

If you don't have a tracker, decide which one you're going to get. WHOOP, Oura, Apple Watch — any of them will do what you need. Pick one and order it.

If you already have one, open the app tonight. Ask the AI:

In the last 30 days, excluding outliers, what is my average total number of hours sleep per night?

Excluding outliers, what is the average of the bottom 20%?

Excluding outliers, what is the average of the top 20%?

This is your baseline. It tells you on average you sleep X hours per night. On bad nights its around Y and good nights around Z.

In the coming weeks or months, these will be a good reference to look back on.
