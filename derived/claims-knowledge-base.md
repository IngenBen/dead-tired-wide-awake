## Chapter 1 — The Weight of It

### Is poor sleep linked to serious disease?

Insufficient sleep is associated with increased risk of heart disease, diabetes, high blood pressure, stroke, depression and obesity. Long-term studies also link persistent short sleep in midlife to a higher risk of dementia later in life.

CDC, *Chronic Disease Indicators: Sleep* (2024). https://www.cdc.gov/cdi/indicator-definitions/sleep.html (names heart disease, depression and obesity among others)

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->

### Does dropping off instantly mean you're sleeping well?

Not usually. Most adults take about 10–20 minutes to fall asleep. On the standard daytime nap test, healthy people average about 10 minutes, and eight minutes or less marks excessive sleepiness, which can come from built-up sleep debt or a disorder such as narcolepsy. Very fast sleep onset is more often a sign of sleep debt than of good sleep.

Sleep Foundation, *Sleep Latency*. https://www.sleepfoundation.org/how-sleep-works/sleep-latency (US sleep-information site; summarises clinical nap-test norms)

## Chapter 2 — The Turning Point

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Can intense exercise produce worse sleep rather than better? -->
### Can hard training make sleep worse, not better?

High-intensity exercise raises cortisol substantially and produces prolonged post-exercise elevation in core body temperature, both of which can delay or disrupt sleep onset. Very hard training sessions, particularly late in the day, can worsen rather than improve sleep quality through this dual pathway.

Hill EE et al. 2008, *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587–591. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03345606 (cortisol rose by about 83% after exercise at 80% of maximum aerobic capacity and also rose at 60%, but not at 40%)
Kenny GP, McGinn R 2017, *J Appl Physiol* 122:933–944. https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00517.2016

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does daytime stress cause night-time sleep problems? -->
### Does daytime stress disrupt sleep at night?

When stress was applied in experiments, it consistently reduced slow-wave sleep, REM sleep and sleep efficiency while increasing awakenings. A systematic review of 63 studies supports the same picture: stress holds the brain in a lighter, more vigilant state at night.

Kim EJ, Dimsdale JE 2007, *Behav Sleep Med* 5:256–278. https://doi.org/10.1080/15402000701557383

## Chapter 3 — The Uncomfortable Truth

### Is poor sleep really linked to serious disease?

Insufficient sleep is associated with increased risk of heart disease, diabetes, high blood pressure, stroke, depression, and obesity. The association is recognised by CDC and NINDS (neither states long-term cognitive decline; checked 2026-10-14). Persistent short sleep (≤6 h) in midlife is linked to ~30% higher later dementia risk (Sabia 2021; observational).

CDC, *Chronic Disease Indicators: Sleep* (2024). https://www.cdc.gov/cdi/indicator-definitions/sleep.html
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Sabia S et al. 2021, *Nat Commun* 12:2289. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-22354-2

### Does poor sleep raise long-term health risks?

Yes. Insufficient sleep is associated with increased risk of heart disease, diabetes and depression, and persistent short sleep in midlife with a higher risk of dementia later in life. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 1, in the note "Is poor sleep linked to serious disease?"

### Is CBN really more sleep-targeted than CBD?

Not clearly. CBN (cannabinol), which forms as THC breaks down, has been tested in only a few trials. In the largest, a week of nightly CBN led to fewer night-time awakenings than placebo, with no extra benefit from adding CBD, and a review of cannabinoid trials found that products other than CBD alone improved self-reported sleep, while CBD alone showed no clear effect. But in another trial adding CBN to CBD made no difference, and in a sleep-lab study of people with insomnia a typical dose of CBN did not change measured sleep, though a much higher dose helped people fall asleep faster.

Bonn-Miller MO et al. 2024, *Exp Clin Psychopharmacol* 32(3):277–284. https://doi.org/10.1037/pha0000682 (293 adults with self-reported poor sleep, seven nights, sleep diaries; 20 mg CBN reduced night-time awakenings and overall sleep disturbance but not time to fall asleep, time awake or daytime tiredness; adding 10–100 mg CBD gave no extra benefit; sponsored and run by a cannabis company)
da Silva GHS et al. 2025, *Sleep Med Rev* 84:102156. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2025.102156 (meta-analysis of six randomised trials, 1,077 adults; self-reported sleep quality; the non-CBD products appear from the reference list to be THC-containing products and one CBN trial)
Saleska JL et al. 2024, *J Am Nutr Assoc* 43(1):1–11. https://doi.org/10.1080/27697061.2023.2203221 (1,793 US adults, four weeks, no placebo group; 15 mg CBD alone and 15 mg CBD plus 15 mg CBN improved self-reported sleep equally; run by a contract research company with the manufacturer's products)
Lavender IG et al. 2026, *J Sleep Res* 35(4):e70284. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.70284 (20 adults with insomnia disorder in Sydney; one night each of placebo, 30 mg and 300 mg CBN in a sleep lab; neither dose changed time awake during the night; 300 mg shortened time to fall asleep and improved rated sleep quality; 30 mg matches typical over-the-counter use; funded by a philanthropic university research centre)

### Can anyone truly function on little sleep?

Natural short sleeping exists but is rare. It has been traced to variants in genes such as DEC2 and ADRB1, each first identified in a single family. For most people, sleep restriction carries a cost they do not notice: performance keeps declining while the feeling of sleepiness levels off.

He Y et al. 2009, *Science* 325:866–870. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1174443 (DEC2 variant identified in one family: a mother and daughter)
Shi G et al. 2019, *Neuron* 103:1044–1055. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.07.026 (ADRB1 variant identified in one family)
Van Dongen HPA et al. 2003, *Sleep* 26:117–126. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117 (48 healthy adults across two experiments; 14 days at 4, 6 or 8 hours in bed; self-rated sleepiness rose only slightly while performance kept declining)

<!-- endnote -->

### Does sleep depend on hormone rhythms?

Sleep depends on several chemical signals, and calling all of them "hormones" is a simplification. Melatonin and cortisol are hormones that follow a circadian pattern of roughly 24 hours, and when that pattern is disrupted, the conditions for sleep degrade. Adenosine is classified as a neuromodulator and builds across waking hours before clearing during sleep. Dopamine acts mainly as a neurotransmitter. Grouping all four as hormones is a convenient shorthand for a wider picture, but the framing is well supported. All four respond to daily behaviour, which is the basis for the book's focus on habits. The individual habits, and the evidence for each, are covered in the chapters that follow.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31:e13597. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13597

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Is driving tired as dangerous as driving drunk? -->
### Is driving tired as risky as driving drunk?

Being awake for 17 hours produces impairment similar to a blood-alcohol level of 0.05%, and being awake for 24 hours to roughly 0.10%. The comparison rests on studies showing similar impairment of cognitive and physiological functioning from fatigue and from alcohol.

CDC/NIOSH, *Training for Nurses on Shift Work & Long Work Hours*, Module 3 (2020). https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod3/08.html

## Chapter 4 — Sleep Incorporated

### Does adenosine build sleep pressure from the moment you wake?

Adenosine accumulates as a byproduct of neural activity from waking onward, building "sleep pressure" across the day. The NINDS describes adenosine release from cells as a key mechanism that makes a person feel sleepy.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31:e13597. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13597

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- cross-reference note (added 2026-10-19, KB sync); substantiation in the entry above -->
### Does adenosine drive sleep pressure?

Yes. Adenosine builds across waking hours and clears during sleep, and it is a main driver of the pressure to sleep. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 5, in the note "What does "largely lifted" mean for adenosine by morning?"

### Is melatonin a timing signal rather than a sedative?

Melatonin is a hormonal timing signal that enables sleep by signalling darkness to the body; it does not directly force or sedate. The Sleep Health Foundation and NINDS both describe melatonin as a darkness/night signal, not a sleep-inducing drug.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- cross-reference note (added 2026-10-19, KB sync); substantiation in the entry above -->
### Does melatonin set the timing of sleep?

Yes. Melatonin rises in the evening and marks biological night, signalling when sleep should occur rather than forcing it. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 6, in the note "How does the sleep window line up with melatonin?"

### Is cortisol the body's daytime alertness and wake-driving hormone?

Cortisol follows a characteristic diurnal rhythm, rising through the late night and morning, peaking post-waking, and declining across the day. It is the primary hormonal driver of alertness and waking energy.

Fries E, Dettenborn L, Kirschbaum C 2009, *Int J Psychophysiol* 72:67–73. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2008.03.014
Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- cross-reference note (added 2026-10-19, KB sync); substantiation in the entry above -->
### Does cortisol supply waking energy?

Yes. Cortisol follows a daily rhythm that starts rising before waking and peaks in the morning, helping mobilise energy for the day. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 7, in the note "Does cortisol start rising before you open your eyes?"

### Does dopamine signal "reward" or "wanting"?

Dopamine encodes incentive salience, the motivational "wanting" or "worth pursuing" signal, rather than pleasure itself. It responds to opportunity and anticipation, driving approach behaviour. This distinction matters: a dopamine-driven state is activating regardless of whether the underlying urge is productive or threatening.

Berridge KC 2007, *Psychopharmacology* 191:391–431. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-006-0578-x

<!-- endnote -->

### Do sleeping pills fix the underlying problem?

Sleeping pills can give short-term relief, but they act on the symptom rather than on the habits and thought patterns that keep insomnia going. For that reason, the American College of Physicians recommends cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-i) as the first treatment for adults with chronic insomnia, with medication treated as a short-term addition when CBT-i alone hasn't worked.

Qaseem A et al. 2016, *Ann Intern Med* 165:125–133. https://doi.org/10.7326/M15-2175 (guideline recommendation; the medication advice is graded weak, based on low-quality evidence)

### Are adenosine and dopamine technically hormones?

Adenosine is classified as an endogenous sleep-regulatory substance and neuromodulator, not a hormone. Dopamine acts mainly as a neurotransmitter; in the motivation/reward role the book describes it works as a broad neuromodulator (diffuse projections setting overall brain state), which is the hormone-like function that justifies the book's shorthand. Calling both "hormones" is a simplification, conceded in Ch04 footnote 1 (proposal: audit/ch04-hormone-simplification-proposed.md, 2026-10-14). Dopamine-specific source still to add.

Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31:e13597. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13597

### Are adenosine and dopamine really hormones?

Not strictly. Adenosine is classified as a neuromodulator, and dopamine acts mainly as a neurotransmitter. In the roles this book describes, both behave in a hormone-like way: they act as broad signals that shift the overall state of the brain, rather than carrying single cell-to-cell messages. Grouping all four as hormones is a simplification. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 3, in the note "Does sleep depend on hormone rhythms?"

Liu C, Goel P, Kaeser PS 2021, *Nat Rev Neurosci* 22:345–358. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-021-00455-7 (review; dopamine is a typical neuromodulator: once released it spreads to act on many nearby target cells, in a more targeted way than once assumed)

### Does menopause sharply disrupt sleep?

Sleep complaints become more common through perimenopause and menopause. Falling oestrogen and progesterone in perimenopause and menopause are associated with increased sleep disturbance, supported by systematic review and meta-analysis.

Cintron D et al. 2017, *Endocrine* 55:702–711. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12020-016-1072-9

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Can HRT restore the conditions for sleep in menopause? -->
### Does menopause disrupt sleep, and can HRT help?

Sleep problems become more common through perimenopause and menopause, and the fall in oestrogen and progesterone is linked to the change, along with hot flushes and night sweats. Hormone therapy improves sleep quality on average, but the benefit appears in women who have hot flushes or night sweats; in women without them, studies show no clear improvement. This fits the idea that HRT helps most when menopause symptoms are the main thing disrupting sleep. The evidence does not show HRT fixing sleep problems that began before menopause or have other causes.

Cintron D et al. 2017, *Endocrine* 55:702–711. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12020-016-1072-9 (systematic review and meta-analysis; moderate-quality evidence; benefit limited to women with vasomotor symptoms)
The North American Menopause Society 2022, *Menopause* 29:767–794. https://doi.org/10.1097/GME.0000000000002028 (hormone therapy position statement; adds that oestrogen may have some effect on sleep independent of hot flushes, with some evidence for skin-patch oestrogen in perimenopause)

## Chapter 5 — Adenosine

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does deep sleep clear adenosine overnight, and how much lifts by morning? -->
### What does "largely lifted" mean for adenosine by morning?

By morning, the bulk of the sleep-pressure debt accumulated across the previous waking day has cleared, but adenosine clearance is gradual and progressive across the whole night rather than an overnight reset to a fixed baseline. Slow-wave sleep is most closely linked to this process; the homeostatic sleep literature tracks it through the slow-wave-activity curve, which reflects how much adenosine-related pressure has been discharged. The clearing is substantial but not necessarily complete, particularly after disrupted or shortened slow-wave sleep.

Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31:e13597. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13597
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

### Does poor overnight adenosine clearance explain morning tiredness?

See **Chapter 5: Does deep sleep clear adenosine overnight to near zero?** — insufficient deep sleep leaves adenosine insufficiently cleared, producing the tiredness that persists into the morning.

### Does stress hold the brain in a vigilant, lighter-sleep state?

Experimental stress consistently reduces slow-wave sleep, REM sleep, and sleep efficiency while increasing awakenings. Stress maintains a state of physiological hyperarousal that is incompatible with deep, restorative sleep.

Kim EJ, Dimsdale JE 2007, *Behav Sleep Med* 5:256–278. https://doi.org/10.1080/15402000701557383

<!-- endnote -->

### Does stress make sleep lighter?

Yes. Stress reduces deep and REM sleep and increases awakenings, holding the brain in a lighter, more vigilant state. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 2, in the note "Does daytime stress disrupt sleep at night?"

### Does warmth block the temperature drop needed for deep sleep?

Core temperature must decline for sleep onset and maintenance to occur. A warm room impairs this cooling process, with heat shed via peripheral vasodilation in the hands and feet. Elevated ambient temperature increases wakefulness and reduces both slow-wave and REM sleep.

Harding EC, Franks NP, Wisden W 2019, *Front Neurosci* 13:336. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.00336
Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *J Physiol Anthropol* 31:14. https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- cross-reference note (added 2026-10-19, KB sync); substantiation in the entry above -->
### Does a warm room reduce deep sleep?

Yes. Sleep depends on core body temperature falling, and a warm environment slows that fall, increasing wakefulness and reducing deep and REM sleep. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 13, in the note "Why is temperature specifically "wired into the biology" of sleep?"

### Does an irregular sleep schedule reduce deep sleep?

Not directly. Deep sleep comes mainly early in the sleep period, and how much a person gets depends mainly on how long they have been awake, not on clock time. An irregular schedule acts mainly on the body clock: people with irregular sleep times have a later melatonin rise and sleep rhythm, and often sleep more in the daytime. Daytime sleep uses up sleep pressure, and a nap in the early evening can reduce deep sleep the following night.

Dijk DJ 2009, *J Clin Sleep Med* 5(2 Suppl):S6–S15. https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.5.2S.S6 (review; forced-desynchrony and sleep-deprivation studies: deep sleep set mainly by time awake, almost independent of body-clock phase; REM strongly clock-dependent; an early-evening nap reduced deep sleep that night)
Phillips AJK et al. 2017, *Sci Rep* 7:3216. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-03171-4 (61 US undergraduates, 30 days of sleep diaries; the 12 most irregular sleepers had a melatonin rise about 2½ h later than the 12 most regular, the same total sleep, and more daytime sleep; deep sleep not measured)
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep (deep sleep comes in longer periods in the first half of the night)

See **Chapter 23: Does screen content matter more than screen light for delaying sleep?** — screen content (arousal, notifications, checking behaviour) fragments lighter sleep stages and eats into slow-wave sleep.

### Why doesn't alcohol appear in this chapter's list of deep sleep disruptors?

Alcohol is absent from this chapter's disruptors list because its effect on slow-wave sleep runs counter to the typical disruption pattern: most doses actually increase early-night deep sleep rather than reducing it. ### Is deep sleep front-loaded into the first part of the night?

Stage 3 non-REM (deep) sleep occurs in longer periods during the first half of the night. Starting sleep later reduces the total amount available, as the window for deep sleep is not evenly distributed across the night.

## Chapter 6 — Melatonin

### Does melatonin rise as evening light drops?

Melatonin secretion is gated by darkness; as evening light levels fall the brain begins releasing it. Both NINDS and the Sleep Health Foundation confirm that light exposure — particularly bright light — suppresses or delays melatonin production.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does melatonin lower core temperature and alertness as well as signalling night? -->
### Is melatonin a sleep signal rather than a sleeping pill?

The brain releases melatonin as light fades in the evening, marking the start of biological night. Alongside this timing role, it slightly lowers core body temperature and, separately, makes people feel sleepier, moving the body toward a state in which sleep can happen. It does not force sleep on its own.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Melatonin*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/melatonin (fact sheet; melatonin rises about two hours before sleep, with a slight fall in core temperature)
Cagnacci A et al. 1997, *J Biol Rhythms* 12:509–517. https://doi.org/10.1177/074873049701200604 (review; daytime melatonin lowers core temperature by about 0.3–0.4 °C, and blocking the night-time rise raises it by about as much)
Lok R et al. 2019, *J Pineal Res* 67:e12583. https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.12583 (10 young adults, 5 mg in the daytime; sleepiness rose without a measurable fall in core temperature)

### How does the sleep window line up with melatonin?

In the hours before habitual bedtime the body clock holds alertness up, a stretch researchers call the wake maintenance zone, when falling asleep is hardest. Melatonin starts to rise about two hours before habitual sleep onset, and the switch from that alert stretch to strong sleepiness, often called the sleep gate, closely tracks the start of the rise. Melatonin then climbs steeply for the next few hours, so habitual bedtime usually falls within its fastest climb, before it peaks in the middle of the night. The link between melatonin and the opening of the gate is an association: melatonin is proposed, not proven, to be what opens it.

Sletten TL et al. 2010, *Front Neurol* 1:137. https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2010.00137 (28 young adults on an early or late sleep schedule for at least 3 weeks; salivary melatonin onset about 2 hours before sleep onset in both groups, with wide individual variation)
Shochat T, Luboshitzky R, Lavie P 1997, *Am J Physiol* 273(1):R364–R370. https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.1997.273.1.R364 (6 healthy men given a 7-minute chance to sleep every 20 minutes for 29 hours; the night-time melatonin rise and the opening of the sleep gate kept a consistent timing; abstract only)
Lavie P 1997, *J Biol Rhythms* 12(6):657–665. https://doi.org/10.1177/074873049701200622 (review; proposes that melatonin opens the evening sleep gate by damping the brain's wakefulness system; abstract only)
Rajaratnam SMW et al. 2004, *J Physiol* 561(Pt 1):339–351. https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2004.073742 (8 healthy men given 16-hour sleep opportunities starting in the afternoon; 1.5 mg melatonin added about 2 hours of sleep in the first 8 hours; the introduction describes the evening 'wake maintenance zone' and the abrupt switch to high sleepiness as melatonin rises)
Zeitzer JM et al. 2007, *Sleep* 30(11):1437–1443. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/30.11.1437 (hourly-averaged plasma melatonin in 90 young men: a steep rise across the hours around lights out, peaking in the middle of the night)

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Why does missing the window keep you awake?

Lying awake gives worry room to work, and worry delays sleep. People who kept going over a stressful task took longer to fall asleep that night, and watching the clock while trying to sleep raised worry and lengthened the time taken to drop off. Leading accounts of insomnia describe a loop in which worry about sleep and about the day drives arousal that keeps the person awake, and people with insomnia carry raised arousal into the evening, including in the stress-hormone system. Treatment guidance advises getting up when sleep does not come rather than lying in bed awake. The pull to sleep itself does not fade after the window: once the sleep gate opens around the start of the melatonin rise, sleepiness stays high and sleep pressure keeps building. That the window seems to close because a thought takes hold and brings in the slower stress system is the book's reasoning, built from these findings.

Zoccola PM, Dickerson SS, Lam S 2009, *Psychosom Med* 71(7):771–775. https://doi.org/10.1097/PSY.0b013e3181ae58e8 (70 participants gave a stressful speech in the afternoon; those who ruminated more took longer to fall asleep that night, measured by wrist actigraph; no link with total sleep or night waking; abstract only)
Tang NKY, Schmidt DA, Harvey AG 2007, *J Behav Ther Exp Psychiatry* 38(1):40–55. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbtep.2005.07.004 (two experiments, 60 good and poor sleepers, then 38 people with insomnia; watching a clock while trying to sleep raised pre-sleep worry and lengthened the time taken to fall asleep; abstract only)
Harvey AG 2002, *Behav Res Ther* 40(8):869–893. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0005-7967(01)00061-4 (theoretical model with a review of early supporting evidence; worry about sleep drives arousal and distress, which keep the person awake; abstract only)

### Is overnight melatonin lower in people with insomnia?

Studies, mostly small, have found that people with long-term insomnia, especially those who struggle to stay asleep, produce less melatonin through the middle of the night than good sleepers. ### Does slow-release melatonin help you stay asleep?

Slow-release melatonin is designed to release over several hours rather than all at once, with the aim of holding the overnight signal steady; a 2 mg form is licensed in Europe for short-term insomnia in adults aged 55 and over. The trial evidence is modest: studies are relatively few, mostly in older adults, rely largely on self-reported sleep, and much of the research is linked to the manufacturer. The trials mainly measured how quickly people fell asleep, and the gain in total sleep time was small (around a quarter of an hour) and seen only in some groups.

### Does melatonin help once you've missed the window?

Taken at bedtime, melatonin shortens the time it takes to fall asleep by only a few minutes on average, and it has not been tested in people already lying awake past their usual bedtime, when the body's own melatonin is well into its evening rise. Pooled trials suggest the effect is several times larger when it is taken two to three hours before bed, while an hour before was not clearly better than at bedtime; these estimates come from a statistical model, and few trials tested earlier times. Taken around bedtime it has little effect on the body clock, and taken towards the end of the night it can push the clock later.

Zeitzer JM et al. 2007, *Sleep* 30(11):1437–1443. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/30.11.1437 (hourly-averaged plasma melatonin in 90 young men: a steep rise across the hours around lights out, peaking in the middle of the night)
Sletten TL et al. 2010, *Front Neurol* 1:137. https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2010.00137 (28 young adults; salivary melatonin onset about 2 hours before sleep onset, with wide individual variation)

### Does an irregular sleep schedule misplace the melatonin rhythm?

Irregular sleep and wake times remove the consistent light-dark cue the melatonin rhythm uses as a reference point. Without it, the rise can occur at the wrong time or flatten, explaining why irregular schedulers often feel neither tired at the right time nor alert in the morning.

Roenneberg T et al. 2012, *Curr Biol* 22:939–943 — social jetlag. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2012.03.038

### Does an irregular schedule throw off melatonin timing?

Yes. Without consistent sleep and wake times, the body clock loses its reference point, so the evening melatonin rise can arrive at the wrong time. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 5, in the note "Does an irregular sleep schedule reduce deep sleep?"

### Does cortisol and melatonin work in opposition across the 24-hour cycle?

Cortisol and melatonin oppose each other across the day-night cycle: cortisol peaks in the morning and declines through the day while melatonin is suppressed by light; by evening, as cortisol falls, melatonin begins to rise. Hyperarousal — elevated cortisol — can override the rising melatonin signal and delay the sleep window.

Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214

### Can stress override rising melatonin?

Yes. Cortisol and melatonin work in opposition, and a nervous system running hot can hold sleep off even as melatonin rises. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 7, in the note "Do cortisol and melatonin work against each other?"

### Does morning light plus a consistent bedtime set the circadian clock?

Light entering the eyes in the first 30–60 minutes after waking is the strongest single environmental input for setting the circadian clock. A consistent wake time paired with morning light exposure establishes the reference that sequences every subsequent rhythm, including the evening melatonin rise.

### Does evening light delay melatonin?

Melatonin release depends on darkness, so bright light in the evening tells the brain it is still daytime: the rise is delayed and the amount released is reduced. The light-sensing cells that drive this respond most to shorter (blue) wavelengths, so blue-enriched light, such as from many screens and LED lights, has a stronger effect, though brightness matters too. A clear shift in melatonin does not translate into an equally large delay in falling asleep. In the controlled study cited here, four hours of tablet reading at full brightness in an otherwise dim room moved the body clock later but added only about ten minutes to the time taken to fall asleep (see Chapter 17, "How much does screen light delay sleep?").

### How should melatonin be used for jet lag?

Melatonin taken at the new local bedtime for the first few nights reduces jet lag, with the clearest benefit after crossing five or more time zones, particularly flying east. Doses from about 0.5 to 5 mg work similarly. The higher dose helps people fall asleep somewhat faster, though by how much was not reported, and doses above 5 mg add nothing. Taken early in the local day, it can cause sleepiness and slow adjustment. Shifting the clock before an eastward flight, by getting up earlier each day into bright morning light with a small afternoon dose, has worked in the lab but has not been tested on real flights.

### Can melatonin help you move your bedtime earlier?

In people diagnosed with a body clock that runs late, who usually fell asleep around half past one, a small dose taken about an hour before an earlier bedtime they had chosen, with bed at that time on most nights, brought sleep about half an hour earlier than a dummy pill over four weeks. Most of the shift came from the earlier bedtime itself, most people did not fully reach their goal, and the body clock did not move significantly in that time. Getting up earlier each day into bright morning light moves the clock itself, and in laboratory studies a small evening dose moved it as far as a larger one. Whether the change lasts has not been tested; very late body clocks often run naturally long and tend to drift back, and a sleep specialist can confirm the diagnosis.

### Does melatonin help shift workers adjust?

Very few permanent night workers fully adjust their body clock to night work, and most adjust too little to benefit, largely because daylight on the way home and on days off keeps pulling the clock back. Sleep in the body's daytime tends to be cut short as the clock's alerting signal rises. Melatonin before daytime sleep may help that sleep last a little longer but does not help people fall asleep faster, and the evidence is weak. Managing light has better support as the main tool, with bright light early in a run of night shifts and light blocked on the commute home and in the bedroom, although most of the trials are small.

### What dose and timing work for circadian shifting with melatonin?

For circadian adjustment, daily doses of 0.5–5 mg appear similarly effective, with no advantage to higher doses. The Sleep Health Foundation recommends taking it about one hour before the target bedtime. A dose of 0.5–1 mg taken 30–60 minutes before the new target bedtime is consistent with this guidance.

### Do typical over-the-counter melatonin doses exceed natural production?

Most over-the-counter melatonin runs at doses far higher than what the body naturally produces each night. The Sleep Health Foundation confirms that low doses (0.5–5 mg) are as effective as higher ones, and that there is no advantage to taking more. The direction — that common OTC doses are supraphysiological — is well supported.

### Does a high melatonin dose carry over into the next morning?

Higher doses bring little extra benefit. For shifting the body clock, 0.5 mg and 3 mg move it about equally far when each is taken at its best time. For falling asleep, pooled trials suggest the effect grows slightly with dose up to about 4 mg and then levels off, but the gain is a few minutes, and when the dose is taken matters more. In jet-lag trials, 5 mg helped people fall asleep faster than 0.5 mg, by an unreported amount, with no gain above 5 mg, while in older adults who woke during the night 0.3 mg improved sleep more than 3 mg. Typical supplement doses raise blood melatonin far above the body's own night-time peak, and higher doses keep it raised for longer, in some people into the next morning, which may cause next-day drowsiness. In trials of 10 mg or more, drowsiness, headache and dizziness were somewhat more common. Cell and animal research shows the body's own nightly melatonin briefly dulls the brain clock's melatonin receptors, which recover within hours; a very high concentration dulls them for longer, which the researchers suggest could weaken the clock's response to later doses.

### Is melatonin for tonight or for good?

Melatonin has two uses that need different timing. As a sleep aid ("tonight"), it works only on the nights it is taken and does not move the body clock much; at a usual bedtime it works best taken two to three hours before bed (an hour before was not clearly better than at bedtime), while for someone moving to an earlier bedtime, about an hour before the new bedtime was the tested timing. For moving a bedtime earlier, a practical single plan is a dose about an hour before the new bedtime, kept every night with an earlier fixed wake time and bright morning light; this combines two tested findings (Sletten 2018 for the dose timing, Revell 2006 for morning light and earlier waking) but has not itself been tested, and in Sletten the clock did not shift significantly within four weeks. As a clock-shifter ("for good"), it is taken in the early evening, several hours before the body's own melatonin rise and timed to the current clock, with morning light and earlier waking, and it moves the clock itself; whether the shift lasts has not been tested, and late clocks tend to drift back. Jet lag draws on both; shift workers mainly use the first, with light managing the clock. The split itself is a synthesis of separate findings, not a single tested result.

### Does melatonin help more when the body clock is the problem than when stress is?

Probably. In pooled trials, melatonin shortened the time to fall asleep less in people with insomnia than in healthy volunteers, and the authors suggest the aroused state of insomnia interferes with its effect, as light and alerting activity do. In a trial for late body clocks, a third of people referred with the symptoms had no late clock and were excluded; whether melatonin helps them is unknown. ### Why does melatonin lengthen daytime sleep after a night shift but not speed up sleep onset?

The likely reason lies in how sleep pressure and the body clock's wake signal combine. Melatonin's direct effect is to damp the clock's wake signal, so it helps at whatever point that signal is the obstacle, and only while it is in the blood. At night, the obstacle is at the start, in the wake maintenance zone before habitual bedtime, so melatonin speeds sleep onset; later in the night the body's own melatonin is high and a quick-release dose has cleared, so it adds little to staying asleep except where natural overnight melatonin is low. After a night shift, sleep pressure is high enough that sleep starts easily, but the clock's daytime wake signal rises as the sleep goes on; with no natural melatonin in the body's daytime, a dose may hold sleep a little longer. Each step is sourced: the two-force framework (two-process model), the equal contribution of clock and sleep pressure to holding sleep together, and the shortening of sleep attempted in the body's daytime by the clock's alerting signal. Joining them into one explanation is reasoning rather than a tested finding.

Liira J et al. 2014, *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* (8):CD009776. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009776.pub2 (1–10 mg after a night shift may add about 24 min of daytime sleep; no faster sleep onset, from only 5 small trials; low-quality evidence)

## Chapter 7 — Cortisol

### Does cortisol start rising before you open your eyes?

Yes. Cortisol follows a daily rhythm that climbs through the second half of the night, so it is already rising before waking. The cortisol awakening response is a separate surge after waking, peaking within the first hour after waking, layered on top of that overnight rise.

Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214

### Does cortisol peak in the first hour after waking?

Cortisol peaks in the first hour or so after waking and then declines across the day in a characteristic diurnal curve. This post-waking surge (the cortisol awakening response) superimposed on the overnight circadian rise is well documented.

Fries E, Dettenborn L, Kirschbaum C 2009, *Int J Psychophysiol* 72:67–73. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2008.03.014

### Is fight or flight cortisol or adrenaline?

The body's stress response has two arms. A fast one, driven by adrenaline and noradrenaline, produces the immediate jolt of alertness and is short-lived. A slower one, a chain of signals from the brain that ends in cortisol, builds more gradually and can last much longer. Not every stressor brings in the slower arm to the same degree: in laboratory studies, cortisol rose most for tasks that felt out of the person's control or carried a risk of being judged, and took longest to settle afterwards. The book uses "cortisol" as shorthand for the whole slower system, because the evidence links that system to poor sleep more clearly than it links cortisol alone.

Godoy LD et al. 2018, *Front Behav Neurosci* 12:127. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2018.00127 (review; the fast sympathetic arm gives rapid, short-lasting responses, the hormonal arm both short- and long-term effects)
Dickerson SS, Kemeny ME 2004, *Psychol Bull* 130:355–391. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.355 (meta-analysis of laboratory stress studies)

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does cortisol respond to anything perceived as urgent — threat or productivity alike? -->
### Does cortisol respond to urgency, even when the thoughts are positive?

Cortisol responds to demands, not only to danger, and the demands that raise it most are those a person has to perform that feel out of their control (including working against the clock) or carry a risk of being judged. In keeping with a recognised theory that the morning cortisol rise prepares the body for the day ahead, it rises more after waking on days when a challenge is expected. It also rises with hard physical effort. But it does not respond to every kind of arousal: a large review of laboratory studies concluded that cortisol is not responsive to all types of stressors. No study has directly tested whether enthusiastic planning in the middle of the night raises cortisol; the link rests on the urgency those thoughts carry rather than on a direct measurement.

Dickerson SS, Kemeny ME 2004, *Psychol Bull* 130:355–391. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.355 (meta-analysis of 208 laboratory studies; how distressed people felt did not predict the rise)
Wetherell MA, Lovell B, Smith MA 2015, *Stress* 18:42–48. https://doi.org/10.3109/10253890.2014.993967 (one small study, 23 young adults; the anticipated challenge was an assessed, stressful task)
Fries E, Dettenborn L, Kirschbaum C 2009, *Int J Psychophysiol* 72:67–73. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2008.03.014 (review; proposes that anticipation of the coming day shapes the size of the cortisol rise after waking)
Hill EE et al. 2008, *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587–591. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03345606

### Does cortisol return to baseline once a demand passes?

Cortisol follows a daily baseline curve, and short-term demands produce temporary rises above it. In laboratory studies cortisol climbs during a stressful task and falls back afterwards, with the largest and slowest-to-settle responses coming from situations that feel uncontrollable and involve being judged by others. Physical demands work the same way: moderate-to-hard exercise raises cortisol, while light exercise does not.

Dickerson SS, Kemeny ME 2004, *Psychol Bull* 130:355–391. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.355 (meta-analysis of 208 laboratory studies; back to pre-task levels within about an hour)
Hill EE et al. 2008, *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587–591. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03345606 (cortisol rose at 60% and 80% of maximum aerobic capacity, not at 40%)

### Does high cortisol actually keep you awake?

Possibly, especially if cortisol is taken to mean the whole slower stress system, as this book does, rather than the hormone alone. That system is a chain of signals. It starts with CRH, a signal in the brain, which prompts the pituitary gland to release ACTH, which in turn prompts the adrenal glands to release cortisol. Technically, CRH, at the start of the chain, has the stronger evidence for causing wakefulness. Given just after falling asleep, it increased wakefulness and reduced deep sleep in middle-aged men, though not in young men. People with chronic insomnia also run the chain higher. On average they have moderately higher cortisol, most of all in the evening and the first half of the night, and in one small study ACTH, the middle link, was clearly raised while cortisol was only borderline. Long-term excess, from steroid medicines or Cushing syndrome, goes with disturbed sleep that partly recovers once the excess is treated. In the one small trial of a drug that blocks cortisol's action, insomnia improved, although sleep-lab measures changed less clearly. Against a simple cause-and-effect reading, a night-time dose of cortisol in healthy young men deepened sleep rather than disturbing it, possibly because it damps the brain's own CRH. Sleep loss also raises cortisol the next evening, so higher cortisol may be partly a result of poor sleep as well as a contributor. So there are plausible mechanisms and some studies linking cortisol to poor sleep, and the link is firmer when cortisol is taken as the whole chain than as the hormone alone, as set out earlier in this chapter.

Dressle RJ et al. 2022, *Sleep Med Rev* 62:101588. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2022.101588 (meta-analysis of 20 case-control studies, 449 people with insomnia and 357 good sleepers; moderately higher cortisol, larger in blood samples; studies with a larger cortisol difference tended to show more sleep loss, not significant; abstract only)
Vgontzas AN et al. 2001, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 86(8):3787–3794. https://doi.org/10.1210/jcem.86.8.7778 (11 young adults with insomnia and 13 controls; 24-hour ACTH significantly higher and cortisol higher at borderline significance, most of all in the evening and first half of the night; the more disturbed sleepers secreted more cortisol; abstract only)
Huscher D et al. 2009, *Ann Rheum Dis* 68(7):1119–1124. https://doi.org/10.1136/ard.2008.092163 (1,066 people with rheumatoid arthritis; self-reported sleep disturbance rose steadily with glucocorticoid dose; observational; abstract only)
Ismailogullari S et al. 2021, *Horm Metab Res* 53(9):608–615. https://doi.org/10.1055/a-1542-8816 (34 people with Cushing syndrome and 23 controls, sleep-lab recordings; less deep and REM sleep, with deep sleep partly recovering 12 months after treatment; abstract only)
Born J et al. 1989, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 68(5):904–911. https://doi.org/10.1210/jcem-68-5-904 (10 healthy men given overnight cortisol infusions: more deep sleep and less REM sleep than on placebo; abstract only)
Friess E et al. 2004, *Neuropsychopharmacology* 29(3):598–604. https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.npp.1300362 (10 healthy young men given repeated hydrocortisone injections at night: more deep, slow-wave sleep; the authors propose it works by damping the brain's own CRH; abstract only)

### Do cortisol and melatonin work against each other?

Cortisol and melatonin follow roughly opposite daily rhythms. Cortisol peaks in the morning and declines through the day to its lowest point around the start of the night, while melatonin is low by day and rises in the evening. When cortisol stays high into the evening, as it does under stress, its alerting effect can override melatonin's signal, much as a stimulant can mask tiredness: melatonin may still be rising, but the body stays too activated for sleep to take hold. The two rhythms are well established; the override fits the hyperarousal model of insomnia rather than having been measured directly as an interaction between the two hormones.

Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214

Riemann D et al. 2010, *Sleep Med Rev* 14:19–31. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2009.04.002 (hyperarousal model of insomnia)

### Does cortisol start rising in the early hours?

Cortisol reaches its lowest point around midnight and begins climbing in the early hours, roughly 2 to 3 a.m., on its way to a morning peak. This falls in the second half of the night, when melatonin is past its peak and sleep is lighter. The timing makes raised cortisol a plausible reason people wake at this hour, but that has not been directly shown.

Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

### Does repeated stimulation raise the cortisol baseline over time?

Repeated stimulation and stress raise baseline cortisol over time, shallowing the evening decline that is needed for melatonin to rise. This is supported both by the caffeine-cortisol literature and by the sleep-deprivation-cortisol literature.

Lovallo WR et al. 2005, *Psychosom Med* 67:734–739. https://doi.org/10.1097/01.psy.0000181270.20036.06

### Does constant stimulation keep cortisol higher?

Partly. Provocative content brings in the stress system as well as dopamine, but the idea that everyday stimulation steadily raises baseline cortisol has not been directly measured. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 8, in the note "Does provocative content add cortisol on top of dopamine?"

### Does late caffeine raise cortisol?

Caffeine raises cortisol, and more so in people who rarely drink it than in daily drinkers. In people who had stopped caffeine for several days, doses through the day kept cortisol raised into the evening. In daily drinkers the response was smaller and shorter, though an early-afternoon dose still raised it for some hours. ### Does a bad night raise cortisol the next day?

Sleep loss acts as a stressor. After a night of partial or total sleep deprivation, cortisol the following evening was higher than normal and its evening decline was slower, leaving the next night starting from a worse position.

Leproult R et al. 1997, *Sleep* 20:865–870. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/20.10.865 (evening cortisol up 37% after partial and 45% after total sleep loss; healthy young men)

Behavioural treatment for insomnia advises keeping the bed for sleep and leaving it when awake, rather than working or scrolling, so that bed and night become cues for sleep. ### Does dopamine respond to opportunity?

Yes. Dopamine signals that something is worth pursuing, a motivational "wanting" rather than pleasure itself. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 4, in the note "Does dopamine signal "reward" or "wanting"?"

### Can looking forward to something keep you awake?

Probably, but it has not been tested directly. Dopamine signals that something is worth pursuing, and its activity rises when a reward is signalled and builds while the outcome is awaited. In mice, silencing the dopamine-producing cells that drive this reduced wakefulness, and stimulating them woke the animals and kept them awake. In people, the wake-promoting drug modafinil raises dopamine in the brain's reward centre. Before an important competition, most elite athletes surveyed had slept worse at least once, mainly through trouble falling asleep, and most put it down to thinking about the event; the survey did not ask about excitement separately from nerves. No study has measured dopamine in people lying awake looking forward to something, so the link is the book's reasoning from these findings.

### Do screens recalibrate the brain's reward system?

The evidence points more to a stronger pull than to a duller response. Variable, unpredictable rewards, of the kind found in gambling and many digital products, engage dopamine learning processes that build "wanting", the drive toward a reward. A review of brain-imaging studies found stronger reward-centre activity in response to social media cues in people with problematic use, and in a three-year study of adolescents, habitual social media checkers' brain responses to anticipated social feedback grew stronger over time. Whether ordinary rewards come to feel less satisfying is less clear: two very small studies of people with internet addiction found fewer dopamine receptors and transporters in the striatum, but it is not known whether heavy use causes this or the difference comes first.

### Does provocative content add cortisol on top of dopamine?

Curiosity-driven content engages the dopamine system, which signals that something is worth pursuing. ### Does the brain respond to novelty and unpredictability?

The brain's dopamine system responds strongly to things that are new or uncertain. A sustained build-up of dopamine activity is greatest when it is most unclear whether a reward will arrive, and new images on their own activate the brain's dopamine centres. These findings come from laboratory tasks rather than studies of scrolling, so applying them to social feeds is a reasonable extension.

### Is predictable TV genuinely less activating than interactive media?

Low-stakes, predictable content produces fewer unpredictable dopamine-reward events than interactive games or algorithmically curated feeds. This is a reasonable extension of the variable-reward and novelty-seeking literature, though no direct head-to-head experimental comparison exists.

### Are games and social feeds deliberately engineered for dopamine?

Variable-ratio reinforcement, in which rewards are unpredictable, maximises dopamine-driven approach behaviour. Games and social-media feeds exploit this mechanism by design. The neuroscience of variable reward is well established; the description of design intent as deliberate is as much a design-ethics observation as a neuroscience claim.

## Chapter 11 — Your Feedback Loop

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: What sleep efficiency should you aim for? -->
### Is above 85% a good target for sleep efficiency?

Sleep efficiency is the share of time in bed actually spent asleep. A National Sleep Foundation expert panel identified 85% or higher as a marker of good sleep quality across adult age groups. It is a guide rather than a hard line, and trackers' estimates of time spent awake in bed are among their least accurate measures.

Ohayon M et al. 2017, *Sleep Health* 3:6–19. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2016.11.006 (expert consensus panel; 85% or more rated good for all adult age groups)

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does deep sleep do the body's physical repair work? -->
### Is deep sleep when the body does its physical repair?

Deep (slow-wave) sleep is the stage most closely associated with physical restoration and with waking refreshed. Describing it as the body's repair time is a fair simplification: restorative processes are not confined to a single stage, and much of the evidence links deep sleep to recovery rather than measuring repair directly.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does HRV reflect how well the nervous system balances its two modes? -->
### Does HRV measure how well the nervous system balances its two modes?

Heart rate variability measures autonomic regulation; higher HRV is associated with greater parasympathetic (vagal) tone and better recovery capacity. "Balancing two modes" is a useful simplification: the calming and activating contributions to HRV are not a simple see-saw, and higher is not always better (some heart-rhythm problems raise HRV).

Shaffer F, Ginsberg JP 2017, *Front Public Health* 5:258. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2017.00258

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Is REM sleep where memory consolidation and emotional processing happen?

REM sleep is central to memory consolidation and emotional processing, though consolidation most likely requires both non-REM and REM. Attributing it solely to REM oversimplifies the process. The amygdala is markedly active during REM, and REM sleep is strongly associated with emotional regulation.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: How accurate are consumer sleep trackers at staging? -->
### How accurate are consumer sleep trackers?

Consumer trackers are most accurate for total sleep time and least accurate for sleep stages. Averaged across groups of people, the better devices estimate total sleep close to laboratory measurement, though any single night can be out by considerably more. Long stretches of lying still while awake are often recorded as sleep, and devices make further errors when they guess bedtime for themselves; correcting the recorded bedtime is the book's practical advice. For light, deep and REM sleep, a single night's figure can be out by up to an hour, and some devices consistently over- or under-count a particular stage. Averaging several nights does not remove all of this error, because part of it is consistent for the same person on the same device. That same consistency is why the trend over weeks on one tracker is more informative than any single night, although trend accuracy itself has not been tested directly.

Chinoy ED et al. 2021, *Sleep* 44:zsaa291. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsaa291 (seven devices, 34 healthy young adults over three nights; deep-sleep limits of agreement roughly ±55–75 min for the better devices; accuracy worse on disrupted nights; flags errors from automatic bedtime detection)
Svensson T et al. 2024, *Sleep Med* 115:251–263. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2024.01.020 (Oura Gen 3, 96 adults; up to three nights averaged per person; no average difference from the sleep lab for deep sleep; mainly funded by Oura)
Robbins R et al. 2024, *Sensors* 24:6532. https://doi.org/10.3390/s24206532 (Oura, Fitbit, Apple Watch, 35 adults, one night; Apple undercounted deep sleep by 43 min on average; funded by Oura)
Schyvens AM et al. 2025, *Sleep Adv* 6:zpaf021. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleepadvances/zpaf021 (six devices; WHOOP 4.0 overcounted deep sleep by about 30 min on average; single night)
Kainec KA et al. 2024, *Sensors* 24:635. https://doi.org/10.3390/s24020635 (five devices; all poor at detecting wake after sleep onset)

### Can trackers miscount still wakefulness as sleep?

Trackers measure total sleep time fairly reliably (high epoch sensitivity) but they can miscount lying very still while awake as sleep, because consumer-grade accelerometry is poor at detecting quiet wakefulness. Stage-by-stage classification accuracy is lower.

Chinoy ED et al. 2021, *Sleep* 44:zsaa291. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsaa291

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->

### Can sleep tracking increase anxiety about sleep?

For some users, sleep-tracker data drives obsessive monitoring and heightened anxiety about sleep quality, a pattern termed "orthosomnia". The appropriate response for users who find tracking counterproductive is to set the tracker aside. The clinicians who described the pattern suggest bringing tracker data into cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-i) rather than letting it guide sleep on its own.

Baron KG et al. 2017, *J Clin Sleep Med* 13:351. https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/10.5664/jcsm.6472 (case reports; the authors propose incorporating sleep trackers into CBT-i)

<!-- endnote -->
### Does the Oura Ring have the most published validation research?

The Oura Ring is among the most studied consumer wearables. A 2024 sleep-lab comparison study validated the Gen 3 device, and in a 2024 multi-device study it had the highest agreement in four-stage sleep staging, narrowly ahead of the Apple Watch. Oura funded both studies, so the volume of published research may partly reflect how much the company invests in publishing.

Svensson T et al. 2024, *Sleep Med* 115:251–263. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2024.01.020 (96 adults, 421,045 thirty-second epochs compared with sleep-lab recording; mainly funded by Oura)
Robbins R et al. 2024, *Sensors* 24(20):6532. https://doi.org/10.3390/s24206532 (35 adults, one night; Oura had the highest agreement in four-stage staging, 76%, narrowly ahead of Apple at 75%; funded by Oura)

## Chapter 12 — Supplements

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does a supplement's specific form or extract matter more than the generic name? -->
### Does the form of a supplement matter?

The same nutrient can come in different chemical forms, and they are not absorbed equally. For magnesium, organic forms such as citrate are generally absorbed better than inorganic forms such as oxide, which is the cheapest and most common. A systematic review supports this broad pattern, though the studies are small and measure absorption in different ways, and it has not been directly proven that better absorption leads to better sleep.

Lindberg JS et al. 1990, *J Am Coll Nutr* 9:48–55. https://doi.org/10.1080/07315724.1990.10720349 (citrate more soluble and better absorbed than oxide)
Pardo MR et al. 2021, *Nutrition* 89:111294. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nut.2021.111294 (systematic review)

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does phosphatidylserine blunt the evening cortisol response? -->
### Does phosphatidylserine reduce the evening cortisol response?

Phosphatidylserine (PS) at 800 mg a day blunted the ACTH and cortisol response to physical exercise in a controlled trial. The evidence is specific to exercise-induced cortisol, not the evening pre-sleep cortisol profile, so the application to sleep is a reasonable extension. Soy- or sunflower-derived PS is the recommended current form (the brain-cortex source used in the original trial is no longer available). Around 400 mg taken 30–60 minutes before bed is the book's suggestion.

Monteleone P et al. 1992, *Eur J Clin Pharmacol* 42:385–388. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00280123 (9 healthy men, 800 mg a day of brain-cortex PS for 10 days; ACTH and cortisol responses to exercise blunted)

### What is the right dose and form of phosphatidylserine for sleep support?

Soy- or sunflower-derived phosphatidylserine is the recommended form (brain-cortex PS is no longer available). Around 400 mg taken 30–60 minutes before bed is consistent with clinical dosing, though the original cortisol-blunting trial used 400–800 mg of brain-cortex PS under exercise-stress conditions, not as a pre-sleep dose. The before-bed timing is practical rather than something those trials established directly.

Monteleone P et al. 1992, *Eur J Clin Pharmacol* 42:385–388. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00280123

<!-- endnote -->

### Does ashwagandha reduce stress and help sleep?

Standardised ashwagandha root extracts have reduced self-reported stress and improved sleep in several small randomised trials, and a meta-analysis found a modest overall benefit for sleep, clearer at higher daily doses and over eight weeks or more. KSM-66 is the most widely studied extract, including a trial that found lower morning cortisol after two months, but other standardised extracts have trials of their own and none has been shown to outperform the others head to head. Trials usually split the dose across the day, so no best time of day has been established. Many trials were small, and the extract was often supplied by its manufacturer.

Cheah KL et al. 2021, *PLoS One* 16:e0257843. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0257843 (meta-analysis; effect clearer at 600 mg/day or more and 8 weeks or longer)
Chandrasekhar K, Kapoor J, Anishetty S 2012, *Indian J Psychol Med* 34:255–262. https://doi.org/10.4103/0253-7176.106022 (KSM-66, 300 mg twice daily, 64 adults with chronic stress, 60 days; morning serum cortisol fell 28% vs 8% on placebo)
Langade D et al. 2019, *Cureus* 11:e5797. https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.5797 (KSM-66, 300 mg twice daily, 60 adults with insomnia, 10 weeks; faster sleep onset and better sleep efficiency; extract supplied by manufacturer)

### What dose and timing are recommended for ashwagandha?

A dose of 300–600 mg is consistent with the meta-analytic evidence, with 600 mg/day or above showing clearer effects in subgroup analysis. Late-afternoon or evening timing is the practical recommendation, though the meta-analysis does not specify timing.

Cheah KL et al. 2021, *PLoS One* 16:e0257843. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0257843

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->

### Does magnesium L-threonate cross into the brain more effectively?

Magnesium L-threonate is often recommended on the basis that it crosses the blood–brain barrier more readily than other forms, an advantage shown mainly in animal studies. Across all forms, the evidence that magnesium improves sleep is low quality. The most direct human evidence on how it might work comes from a small trial in older adults with insomnia, where magnesium improved sleep measures and lowered cortisol; this may reflect correcting a shortfall common at that age.

Mah J, Pitre T 2021, *BMC Complement Med Ther* 21:125. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-021-03297-z
Abbasi B et al. 2012, *J Res Med Sci* 17:1161–1169. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23853635/ (46 older adults with insomnia, 500 mg/day for 8 weeks; better sleep efficiency and sleep onset, lower serum cortisol, higher melatonin; total sleep time unchanged)

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: What dose of magnesium should you take in the evening? -->
### How much elemental magnesium is in a standard evening dose?

Around 150–300 mg of elemental magnesium in the evening is within the range studied in insomnia trials, which gave 320–729 mg a day across two or three doses. Time to fall asleep was shorter after magnesium than after placebo, though the evidence quality is low. When taking magnesium L-threonate, the label refers to the whole compound: a 2 g (2,000 mg) compound serving delivers about 144 mg of elemental magnesium, as printed on product labels. Citrate and glycinate forms are typically labelled by elemental content and are directly comparable to the studied range.

Mah J, Pitre T 2021, *BMC Complement Med Ther* 21:125. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-021-03297-z (meta-analysis of three trials, 151 older adults; time to fall asleep about 17 minutes shorter than on placebo; low to very low quality evidence)
Liu G et al. 2016, *J Alzheimers Dis* 49(4):971–990. https://doi.org/10.3233/JAD-150538 (magnesium L-threonate compound, 1.5–2 g a day by body weight, in adults aged 50–70 with cognitive impairment; sleep effects could not be separated from a strong placebo response; manufacturer-funded; 144 mg per 2 g serving is the product label figure)

## Chapter 13 — Your Sleep Environment

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does night light in the bedroom register as a wake signal? -->
### Can light in the bedroom affect sleep without waking you?

At room-light levels, yes. In a laboratory study, healthy young adults who slept one night with the overhead light on had a higher heart rate and a shift towards the body's alert state all night compared with sleeping in near-darkness, and spent less time in deep and REM sleep, without any increase in awakenings. They were also less sensitive to insulin the next morning. The light used was ordinary room lighting rather than a faint glow, melatonin was not measurably affected, and the study was small, so the effect of very dim sources such as standby lights is less certain.

Mason IC, Grimaldi D, Reid KJ et al. 2022, *PNAS* 119:e2113290119. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2113290119 (20 adults; one night at 100 lux vs under 3 lux)

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Why is temperature specifically "wired into the biology" of sleep?

Temperature is embedded in sleep physiology in a way light and noise are not: the core body temperature drop is part of the initiating mechanism of sleep, not merely a condition to manage around it. Elevated ambient temperature increases wakefulness and reduces both slow-wave and REM sleep, while the core temperature decline is most closely aligned with NREM sleep onset. The chapter's other environmental factors, light and noise, affect the sleep system primarily through signalling pathways; temperature directly constrains the physiological conditions sleep requires to occur.

Harding EC, Franks NP, Wisden W 2019, *Front Neurosci* 13:336. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.00336
Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *J Physiol Anthropol* 31:14. https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: What is the commonly cited optimal bedroom temperature range? -->
### What bedroom temperature do sleep sources recommend?

Popular sleep sources commonly recommend a bedroom of around 18 °C, within a range of about 18–20 °C. These figures are expert guidance rather than a precisely measured optimum, and they assume a typically covered sleeper; the comfortable room temperature shifts considerably with bedding and clothing, as the note on the 29 °C figure explains.

Sleep Foundation (US), *Best Temperature for Sleep*. https://www.sleepfoundation.org/bedroom-environment/best-temperature-for-sleep (recommends about 18.3 °C; doctors' range 18–20 °C)

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Where does the "around 29 °C" near-naked sleep figure come from? -->
### Where does the 29 °C near-naked sleep figure come from?

This figure traces to an early laboratory study in which six men slept in shorts only with no bedding across five ambient temperatures; thermoneutrality, the least disruptive condition, was around 29 °C. It is a small, dated, near-naked study, useful for illustrating the principle that the body needs to shed heat, not for prescribing a room temperature. Its insight is comparative: stripped of covers, the room must supply all the warmth, so the comfortable point sits far above the 18–20 °C usually quoted for a covered sleeper. The more robust finding from later work is that with bedding, the "bed climate" against the skin is generally around 32–34 °C when sleep is normal, so covering up matters more than room air temperature.

Haskell EH, Palca JW, Walker JM, Berger RJ, Heller HC 1981, *Electroencephalogr Clin Neurophysiol* 51(5):494–501. https://doi.org/10.1016/0013-4694(81)90226-1 (six men, near-naked; illustrates the principle rather than prescribing a room temperature)
Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *J Physiol Anthropol* 31:14. https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14 (review; bed climate generally around 32–34 °C when sleep is normal)

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: How much does core body temperature drop during sleep onset? -->
### How much does body temperature drop at sleep onset?

Core temperature falls by about 1 °C (2 °F) over the night. The fall begins up to about two hours before sleep, as warm hands and feet release heat, and sleep is most likely to begin when it is falling fastest. Because heat is shed mainly through the hands and feet, cold extremities can slow sleep onset, and a warm bath before bed, which drives this heat loss and speeds the cooling that follows, can help initiate sleep.

Harding EC, Franks NP, Wisden W 2019, *Front Neurosci* 13:336. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.00336 (review; the fall begins up to 2 h before sleep; sleep onset most likely when core temperature falls fastest)
Sleep Foundation (US), *Best Temperature for Sleep*. https://www.sleepfoundation.org/bedroom-environment/best-temperature-for-sleep (core temperature varies by about 2 °F through the night; the drop starts about two hours before sleep)

### Does a warm room fragment sleep, especially REM?

Elevated ambient temperature increases wakefulness and decreases both REM and slow-wave sleep. These effects are directly linked to thermoregulation. A too-warm environment is a well-documented cause of sleep fragmentation.

Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *J Physiol Anthropol* 31:14. https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does the body largely suspend thermoregulation during REM? -->
### Does the body stop regulating its temperature during REM sleep?

During REM sleep the brain's control of body temperature is largely suspended, so the body responds less to heat and cold and is more influenced by the room. This is well established in animal research; human studies are consistent with it, finding that a warm room increases wakefulness and reduces both REM and deep sleep.

Cerri M et al. 2017, *Front Physiol* 8:624. https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2017.00624 (review; the evidence comes from animal studies, chiefly cats and rats)
Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K 2012, *J Physiol Anthropol* 31:14. https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14 (review of human studies on heat and sleep)

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Do sudden noises pull you from deep sleep and lower quality? -->
### Do sudden noises disturb deep sleep without waking you?

Often, yes. In a large laboratory study, recorded traffic noise during the night reduced deep sleep and increased brief arousals, which were far more common than full awakenings, so much of the disruption would go unnoticed. Louder and more abrupt noises had stronger effects, and although people partly adapted to the noise over several nights, their heart-rate responses to it did not fade.

Basner M, Müller U, Elmenhorst EM 2011, *Sleep* 34:11–23. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/34.1.11 (72 adults, 11 nights; noise-triggered arousals about 2.6 times as common as noise-triggered awakenings; effects clearer at 55 dB and above)

### Does watching TV in bed teach the brain that bed is for wakefulness?

The brain conditions stimulus-response associations between the sleep environment and sleep or wakefulness. Activities in bed that involve wakefulness — including watching TV, using a phone, or worrying — condition the bed as a cue for arousal rather than for sleep. This is the stimulus-control rationale underpinning CBT-i.

AASM, *Behavioral and Psychological Treatments for Insomnia* clinical practice guideline (2020). https://aasm.org/new-guideline-supports-behavioral-psychological-treatments-for-insomnia/
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

<!-- endnote -->

It can. Being awake and entertained in bed conditions the bed as a cue for alertness, which is why behavioural treatment for insomnia keeps the bed for sleep. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 17, in the note "Does CBT-i make getting out of bed when awake a central recommendation?"

Probably. An expert panel agreed that screen content harms sleep in children and adolescents, but could not agree that screen light does at any age. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 23, in the note "Does screen content matter more than screen light for delaying sleep?"

## Chapter 14 — Caffeine

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: How does caffeine keep you awake? -->
### Does caffeine block the brain's sleep-pressure signal?

Yes. Caffeine works mainly by blocking the brain's adenosine receptors, so the build-up of sleep pressure goes unread rather than cleared; the adenosine itself keeps accumulating. Its effect on sleep can be large and easy to miss: in a controlled study, caffeine taken six hours before bed still shortened sleep by more than an hour, and participants did not notice.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep (adenosine helps make you feel sleepy; caffeine blocks its actions)
Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31(4):e13597. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13597 (review; at everyday doses caffeine acts by blocking adenosine receptors; evening caffeine delays sleep and lightens deep sleep)
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-and-sleep
Drake C, Roehrs T, Shambroom J, Roth T 2013, *J Clin Sleep Med* 9:1195–1200. https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170 (12 healthy sleepers; 400 mg, about 2–3 cups; measured by home sleep monitor)

### Is caffeine the single biggest sleep disruptor for a significant number of people?

Caffeine can have a significant impact on falling asleep and staying asleep. Among afternoon caffeine consumers, a high proportion report at least one sleep problem. For this subgroup, caffeine is likely the primary habit disrupting sleep.

Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Caffeine and Sleep*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/caffeine-and-sleep

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does caffeine wearing off worsen the afternoon slump? -->
### Does caffeine wearing off make the afternoon slump worse?

Partly. When caffeine wears off, the sleep pressure it was masking is felt again; in animal studies, regular caffeine raised adenosine levels and the number of its receptors, which may make that return stronger. Regular users also develop tolerance, and a missed or delayed dose brings withdrawal fatigue and low alertness, which makes the next cup feel necessary; in studies lasting several days, daily caffeine did not leave people less sleepy than placebo. That this deepens the afternoon slump is the book's reasoning.

Reichert CF, Deboer T, Landolt HP 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31(4):e13597. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13597 (review; in rats and mice, regular caffeine raised adenosine levels and receptor numbers, and sleep increased once caffeine cleared; in people, several days of caffeine did not reduce sleepiness compared with placebo, and some studies found more, possibly reflecting withdrawal)
Juliano LM, Griffiths RR 2004, *Psychopharmacology* 176:1–29. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-004-2000-x (review; fatigue and drowsiness are validated withdrawal symptoms, which can occur even after stopping as little as 100 mg a day)
James JE, Rogers PJ 2005, *Psychopharmacology* 182:1–8. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-005-0084-6 (much of caffeine's apparent boost is reversal of withdrawal)

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: What is caffeine's half-life? -->
### What is caffeine's actual half-life?

"Roughly five hours" sits within the usual range. The European Food Safety Authority puts the average at about four hours, varying from about two to eight hours between healthy adults, and a 2026 re-analysis of published data put it at 4.0 hours. It is longer in women taking oral contraceptives, with some medicines, and in pregnancy, reaching three to four times the usual figure by the end of pregnancy. At a typical half-life, caffeine from a mid-morning double shot is still circulating in the evening, and more so for anyone at the longer end of the range.

EFSA 2015, *Scientific Opinion on the safety of caffeine*, *EFSA Journal* 13(5):4102. https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102 (scientific opinion; average half-life about four hours, about two to eight hours in healthy adults; longer with oral contraceptives, 15.5 vs 5.6 h in one small study; 6–16 h in pregnancy)
Agboola OE et al. 2026, *Curr Ther Res Clin Exp* 104:100832. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.curtheres.2026.100832 (re-analysis of published blood-level data from 37 people given 100–400 mg)

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does delaying the first coffee by 60-90 minutes help caffeine work better? -->
### Does delaying the first coffee make caffeine work better?

Waiting 60–90 minutes after waking before the first coffee is the book's own practice, popularised by the neuroscientist Andrew Huberman, who argues that caffeine taken straight after waking blocks adenosine that has not yet cleared, leading to an afternoon slump. An expert review found no basis for it: adenosine starts to build again within minutes of waking, and in people who drink coffee daily, morning caffeine no longer adds to the body's own cortisol rise.

Huberman Lab podcast, *Using Caffeine to Optimize Mental & Physical Performance* (5 Dec 2022). https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/using-caffeine-to-optimize-mental-and-physical-performance (podcast; segment on delayed caffeine intake and the afternoon crash; no study cited for the delay)
Fries E, Dettenborn L, Kirschbaum C 2009, *Int J Psychophysiol* 72:67–73. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2008.03.014 (review; the cortisol awakening response peaks within the first hour after waking)
Lovallo WR et al. 2005, *Psychosom Med* 67:734–739. https://doi.org/10.1097/01.psy.0000181270.20036.06 (in daily caffeine users the morning cortisol response to caffeine disappeared)
Antonio J et al. 2024, *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 21(1):2323919. https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2024.2323919 (expert review, International Society of Sports Nutrition; addresses the delay advice directly: caffeine still raises cortisol if delayed, the effect is blunted in habitual users, adenosine rises within minutes of waking; no basis found for the afternoon-crash rationale)

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does sleep deprivation reduce self-control? -->
### Does sleep loss reduce self-control?

Sleep loss impairs attention and decision-making, and laboratory studies show it measurably weakens inhibitory control, the ability to stop an impulsive response. A night without sleep also makes the brain's emotional centre react more strongly to upsetting images, with weaker control from the front of the brain.

Choong SY et al. 2025, *Sleep Med Rev* 80:102042. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102042 (meta-analysis of 24 laboratory studies, 712 healthy adults; moderate effect on two standard impulse-control tasks; mostly after a full night without sleep)
Yoo SS et al. 2007, *Curr Biol* 17(20):R877–R878. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2007.08.007 (26 healthy adults aged 18–30 after about 35 hours awake; amygdala response to negative images about 60% greater, with weaker links to the medial prefrontal cortex)
Pilcher JJ, Morris DM, Donnelly J, Feigl HB 2015, *Front Hum Neurosci* 9:284. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00284 (mini-review; the wider link with everyday self-control is less studied)

## Chapter 15 — Alcohol

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does alcohol speed sleep onset but sedate rather than induce natural sleep? -->
### Does alcohol help you fall asleep, and is that real sleep?

Alcohol can shorten the time it takes to fall asleep, and people often feel they drop off faster, but in sleep-lab studies the measured effect was small and clear only at higher doses. It works as a sedative, though, and the sleep that follows is arranged differently from natural sleep: deep sleep is pulled into the early part of the night, REM is held back, and sleep becomes lighter and more broken later on.

Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549. https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006 (qualitative review)
Gardiner C et al. 2025, *Sleep Med Rev* 80:102030. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030 (meta-analysis of 27 lab studies; about 2 min faster on average; clearly shorter only from about five standard drinks)

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does alcohol affect deep sleep — and if so, how?

Alcohol does not reduce slow-wave sleep; at most doses it increases it in the first half of the night, and at higher doses people reach deep sleep sooner, while findings for deep sleep across the whole night are mixed. This appears to be a sedative artefact: alcohol's depressant effect on the brain pushes the system into deeper non-REM states earlier than it would naturally arrive. The cost comes in the second half of the night, when the alcohol has been broken down and its suppression of REM sleep dominates. Early deep sleep pulled forward by alcohol is not a bonus: it comes at the expense of the overall architecture, displacing the REM that would have followed.

Gardiner C et al. 2025, *Sleep Med Rev* 80:102030. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030 (meta-analysis; whole-night deep sleep unchanged on average; more in the first three hours and less in the second half in two dose studies; deep sleep reached about 3 min sooner, clearly only at about six drinks)
Ebrahim IO et al. 2013, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37:539–549. https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006 (qualitative review; more first-half deep sleep at all doses; whole-night deep sleep raised at high doses)

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does eating with alcohol slow absorption and soften the peak? -->
### Does food slow how quickly alcohol is absorbed?

Yes. Food in the stomach slows alcohol's passage into the small intestine, where most of it is absorbed, so blood alcohol rises more slowly and peaks lower than on an empty stomach. The body also breaks alcohol down somewhat faster after a meal, whatever the meal's make-up.

Cederbaum AI 2012, *Clin Liver Dis* 16(4):667–685. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cld.2012.08.002 (review; food slows stomach emptying and so absorption, the main factor in how fast alcohol is absorbed; fat, carbohydrate and protein meals similar; alcohol broken down faster in the fed state)

### Is zero alcohol the recommended position for sleep?

The World Health Organization's European office states that no level of alcohol is safe for health. Sleep guidance focuses on timing rather than a ban: avoid alcohol for at least three to four hours before bed, stopping earlier if sensitive or taking medicines that interact with it, as even modest amounts can worsen snoring and sleep apnoea. Applying the general-health advice to sleep, the book takes zero as its starting point.

### Does alcohol suppress REM and disrupt the second half of sleep?

Alcohol reliably delays the onset of REM sleep and reduces its duration in a dose-dependent manner, with disruption observed even at low doses (approximately two standard drinks). In the second half of the night, alcohol causes increased sleep disruption and more frequent awakenings. These two effects, REM suppression and second-half fragmentation, are the robust and reproducible sleep costs of alcohol.

The dose-dependent effect is well supported. ### Why does alcohol wake you in the early hours?

As alcohol is cleared during the night, its sedation lifts and the body swings the other way: the second half of the night after drinking brings more awakenings and lighter, broken sleep at every dose studied. Wearable studies also find a higher heart rate and lower heart-rate variability on nights after drinking, a sign the body is not settling into recovery, and this shows from the first hours of sleep rather than only at the rebound. "Metabolic rebound" is a descriptive label rather than a formal term, and when the wake comes depends on how much was drunk and when.

### Does alcohol cause vivid dreams through REM rebound?

At higher doses, the REM sleep held back early in the night tends to return later, as the alcohol is cleared, in a fragmented and easily interrupted form. Vivid dreams and nightmares after drinking are commonly reported and fit this pattern, though the link to dream intensity rests mainly on people's own reports rather than direct measurement.

### Does digesting a meal work against the body's cooling for sleep?

Digesting food raises the body's energy use and heat production for several hours, a process called diet-induced thermogenesis. Because a falling core temperature is part of how sleep begins, the extra heat from a substantial meal close to bed working against that fall is a reasonable inference that has not been measured directly.

### Does eating close to bed make sleep worse?

The evidence points that way. In a randomised trial, young women who ate dinner one hour before bed rather than five had lower sleep efficiency and more time awake and briefly roused during the night. Surveys link later last meals with poorer sleep, and eating within three hours of bed with more night waking.

Eating close to bedtime may ease sleep onset — the post-meal metabolic state can promote drowsiness — but the quality and architecture of sleep suffer, with fragmentation more likely in the second half of the night. This parallels the alcohol pattern.

### Does protein steady overnight blood sugar?

A protein-rich, low-carbohydrate snack is gentler on overnight blood sugar than a carbohydrate snack, but no bedtime snack has been shown to steady blood sugar compared with having none. In people with type 2 diabetes, two eggs at bedtime led to lower overnight and morning blood sugar than a yoghurt with the same protein and calories but more carbohydrate; a review of bedtime-snack studies in type 2 diabetes and prediabetes found that no snack did better than eating nothing; and in healthy older adults a protein drink before bed did not change next-morning blood sugar. Morehen S et al. 2019, *Nutrients* 12(1):90. https://doi.org/10.3390/nu12010090 (12 healthy adults aged about 70 in the UK; 40 g protein drink vs a carbohydrate drink vs water before bed; next-morning glucose and insulin did not differ)
Afaghi A, O'Connor H, Chow CM 2007, *Am J Clin Nutr* 85:426–430. https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.2.426 (small study of 12 healthy men; high-glycaemic meal four hours before bed)

Dietary carbohydrate intake raises the plasma concentration of tryptophan, a precursor of serotonin (and downstream, melatonin). ## Chapter 17 — Can't-Sleep Routine

### When the mind races, does getting out of bed help?

See Does CBT-i make getting out of bed when awake a central recommendation? (below) — the stimulus-control instruction to leave the bed when unable to sleep applies equally to mind-racing. Getting up and sitting on the couch until sleepy is an acceptable implementation.

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does lying frustrated in bed raise cortisol and prevent sleep? -->
### Does lying awake frustrated keep you awake?

Yes. Insomnia is understood as a state of heightened mental, emotional and physical arousal, and worry and frustration about not sleeping feed that arousal and keep the body alert.

Riemann D et al. 2010, *Sleep Med Rev* 14:19–31. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2009.04.002 (hyperarousal model, including raised neuroendocrine (hormonal) activity)
Dressle RJ, Riemann D 2023, *J Sleep Res* 32:e13928. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13928 (updated review; physical arousal shows most clearly in hormonal and brain-activity measures rather than heart rate)

<!-- endnote -->
### Does CBT-i make getting out of bed when awake a central recommendation?

Yes. Stimulus control, a core part of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, tells people to leave the bed when they cannot sleep, do something quiet elsewhere, and return only when sleepy, so the bed is kept for sleep. Repeated over weeks, this rebuilds the link between bed and sleep that nights of lying awake have weakened. Stimulus control is also recommended on its own as a treatment for chronic insomnia.

Edinger JD et al. 2021, *J Clin Sleep Med* 17:255–262. https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.8986 (AASM clinical practice guideline; stimulus control alone: conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence)
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

### Does repeating the routine rewrite the bed-sleep association over weeks?

Stimulus control — consistently leaving the bed when awake and returning only when sleepy — gradually reconditions the bed-sleep association. The CBT-i literature and the conditioning principle underlying it confirm that reconditioning takes repeated nights and typically several weeks.

AASM, *Behavioral and psychological treatments for insomnia* clinical practice guideline (2020). https://aasm.org/new-guideline-supports-behavioral-psychological-treatments-for-insomnia/

### What does ACT-i add to the advice to get up?

Acceptance and commitment therapy for insomnia (ACT-i) is described as teaching people to accept being awake rather than fight it, because the struggle to sleep generates more arousal than the wakefulness itself. A systematic review found it improved insomnia and sleep quality.

Salari N et al. 2020, *BMC Neurol* 20:300. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-020-01883-1 (systematic review of 19 studies)

### Where do the lux figures in this footnote come from?

Light's effect on melatonin follows a dose-response curve rather than a threshold. In one study the average person's melatonin was half suppressed at around 25 lux, the group-level figure used in the footnote, while an earlier study put that halfway point nearer 100 lux. People differ widely: each person's own halfway point ranged from about 6 lux in the most sensitive to around 350 lux in the least, so the most sensitive show some suppression below 6 lux. The figures for a TV at three metres (about 5 lux) and a phone held close (about 25–50 lux) apply the inverse-square principle and are illustrative rather than measured at these exact positions. On these figures, a distant screen sits well below the level that affects most people, though the most sensitive may still respond, while a close-held phone sits around the group-level halfway point.

Phillips AJK et al. 2019, *PNAS* 116:12019–12024. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31138694/ (55 adults aged 18–30; group halfway point about 25 lux; individual halfway points from 6 to 350 lux)

A standard television at three metres delivers approximately 5 lux to the eye; a phone held 30 cm away delivers approximately 25–50 lux. These are illustrative figures applying the inverse-square principle, not measurements, and actual levels vary with screen brightness, size and ambient conditions. The underlying principle is well established: a distant screen delivers far less light to the eye than a close-held one.

## Chapter 18 — Morning Routine

### Does shifting wake time shift the melatonin countdown later?

Moving wake time shifts the cortisol-awakening response and adjusts when the evening melatonin countdown begins, pushing the sleep window correspondingly later. Light and wake time together are the primary anchors of the circadian clock.

NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep

### Does sleeping in throw off the body clock?

Yes. Shifting your wake time shifts the timing of the evening melatonin rise. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 5, in the note "Does an irregular sleep schedule reduce deep sleep?"

<!-- endnote -->

### Is morning light the strongest cue for the body clock?

Yes. Light reaching the eyes is the main signal the body clock uses to set itself to the day, and morning light shifts it earlier, so the time you wake and get light sets when melatonin rises that evening. Waking and getting light later pushes the whole cycle later.

Brown TM et al. 2022, *PLoS Biol* 20:e3001571. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571 (expert consensus; morning and evening light have opposite effects on the clock)
NINDS, *Brain Basics: Understanding Sleep*. https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep (light described as perhaps the greatest influence on the sleep-wake cycle)

### Does 5-15 minutes of morning sun usually suffice, and does overcast beat indoor light?

Five to fifteen minutes of direct sun is usually enough for the circadian signal; overcast outdoor light is still far brighter than typical indoor environments (10,000-100,000 lux outdoors versus 100-500 lux indoors), so time outside on a grey day still meaningfully outperforms staying inside. The brightness contrast is uncontested; the 5-15 minute dose is a practical figure rather than a hard-cited threshold.

Brown TM et al. 2022, *PLoS Biology* 20:e3001571 — expert-consensus light recommendations. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Is natural light dramatically brighter than indoor lighting, even on an overcast day? -->
### Is outdoor light much brighter than indoor light, even when it's overcast?

Yes. Daylight outdoors is several times brighter than typical indoor lighting, even under cloud, and many indoor spaces fall short of the daytime light levels experts recommend for a well-set body clock.

Brown TM et al. 2022, *PLoS Biol* 20:e3001571. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571 (indoor lighting guidelines sit at least ten times below outdoor light; most people get less than the recommended daytime level)
Daugaard S et al. 2019, *Ann Work Expo Health* 63(6):651–665. https://doi.org/10.1093/annweh/wxy110 (light recorded for 7 days across the year in 509 Danish workers, 55–56°N; indoor workers' daytime exposure never exceeded 1,000 lux in winter, while most outdoor workers exceeded 1,000 lux in winter and 2,500 lux in summer)

### Does morning movement help set the body clock alongside light?

Yes. Exercise is a time cue for the body clock in its own right: in a laboratory study, an hour of moderate exercise early in the morning moved the clock earlier, and exercise in the late evening moved it later. Light remains the main cue, and a morning walk outdoors gives both at once.

Youngstedt SD, Elliott JA, Kripke DF 2019, *J Physiol* 597(8):2253–2268. https://doi.org/10.1113/JP276943 (99 adults, 1 h of moderate treadmill exercise at one of eight times for 3 days, in dim light; 7 a.m. and 1–4 p.m. shifted the clock earlier, 7–10 p.m. later; shifts comparable to bright light of equal duration; combined effect with light not tested)

<!-- endnote -->

### Can a light-therapy lamp stand in for morning sunlight?

Bright light-therapy lamps are an established treatment for seasonal low mood and are recommended for some body-clock disorders, timed to the problem. They are the usual substitute when outdoor light isn't available, though no study has compared them directly with morning sunlight for everyday sleep timing.

Auger RR et al. 2015, *J Clin Sleep Med* 11(10):1199–1236. https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.5100 (American Academy of Sleep Medicine guideline; light therapy suggested, as a weak recommendation, for advanced sleep timing in adults, delayed sleep timing in children and adolescents with behavioural treatment, and older people with dementia; evidence rated low to very low quality)

### Does a consistent wake time and bedtime anchor the circadian clock?

Holding a consistent wake time — even after a bad night — and keeping bedtime within approximately 30 minutes anchors the circadian system. Going to bed even 30 minutes later than usual is associated with a significantly higher resting heart rate throughout sleep that persists into the following day.

Faust L et al. 2020, *npj Digital Medicine* 3:39. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-020-0250-6

Short naps of approximately 20 minutes (within a 15-30 minute window) are optimal — short enough to avoid slow-wave sleep and the subsequent grogginess of sleep inertia, and not so late that they spend down the adenosine pressure needed for night sleep.

## Chapter 20 — Meditation

### Do the ANS's two modes — sympathetic activating and parasympathetic calming — govern arousal?

The autonomic nervous system operates in two broad modes: sympathetic (activating, mobilising) and parasympathetic (calming, restorative). These modes are well established in basic physiology. Most people with persistent sleep problems spend too much time in the sympathetically dominant, activated state — the hyperarousal model of insomnia.

Riemann D et al. 2010, *Sleep Med Rev* 14:19–31 — hyperarousal model. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2009.04.002
Dressle RJ et al. 2023, *J Sleep Res* 32:e13928. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jsr.13928

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- cross-reference note (added 2026-10-19, KB sync); substantiation in Chapter 17 -->
### Are poor sleepers stuck in the activated state?

Largely, yes. Insomnia is understood as a state of heightened arousal. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 17, in the note "Does lying awake frustrated keep you awake?"

<!-- endnote -->

### Does slow breathing work through the vagus nerve?

Yes. The vagus nerve is the main pathway of the body's calming system, and slow breathing, especially a slow exhale, shifts the nervous system towards it, measurable as changes in heart rhythm. Breathing is the one automatic function that can also be taken over deliberately, which in the book's view makes it the most direct lever on that system. Most of the vagus nerve's fibres carry information from the body up to the brain, so attending to bodily sensations engages it from that side as well.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817 (review; slow breathing raised vagal activity in healthy people, balancing rather than switching off the stress side)
Berthoud HR, Neuhuber WL 2000, *Auton Neurosci* 85:1–17. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1566-0702(00)00215-0 (about four-fifths of vagal fibres carry signals from body to brain)

### Is the vagus nerve bidirectional, with most fibres running body-to-brain?

The vagus is a mixed nerve composed of approximately 80% afferent (sensory, body-to-brain) and 20% efferent fibres. Attending to body sensations therefore works the nerve from the sensory side — a bottom-up mechanism that gives behavioural access to the calming system.

Berthoud HR, Neuhuber WL 2000, *Auton Neurosci* 85:1–17 — primary. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1566-0702(00)00215-0 Physiopedia, Vagus Nerve (secondary summary). https://www.physio-pedia.com/Vagus_Nerve

### Are the lungs uniquely controllable off autopilot?

Breathing is the one autonomic function under reliable voluntary control, which is why it serves as the primary lever for deliberate autonomic regulation. This point is well supported by the slow-breathing and HRV-biofeedback literature.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817

### Is nasal breathing better for meditation?

Probably, for two reasons, though both rest on small studies. First, the nose passes less air than the mouth, which limits how quickly air can be drawn in: people switch to the mouth once they need a lot of air, and during exercise nose-only breathing gave slower, deeper breaths than mouth breathing. At rest it did not change breathing rate by itself, but a deliberately slow, deep breath takes longer to draw in through the nose, which makes a slow pace easier to hold; this last step follows from the airflow findings and has not been tested in meditation. Second, the route itself seems to matter. Breathing at the same pace, people at rest had slightly lower blood pressure and a heart rhythm tilted further towards the body's calming side when breathing through the nose rather than the mouth, and experienced meditators reported less anxiety, more joy and a deeper meditative state after slow nose breathing than after the same breathing through the mouth, although their heart-rhythm measures did not differ. One proposed explanation is that air moving through the nose drives a rhythm in the brain's smell, emotion and memory areas that fades when breathing switches to the mouth; it has not been directly proven that this causes the calming effect.

Niinimaa V et al. 1981, *Respir Physiol* 43(1):69–75. https://doi.org/10.1016/0034-5687(81)90089-X (30 healthy adults during graded exercise; 20 switched from nose-only to nose-and-mouth breathing at about 35 litres of air a minute, while 5 stayed nose-only and 4 breathed through the mouth as a habit)
Eser P et al. 2024, *Front Physiol* 15:1380562. https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2024.1380562 (Switzerland; 15 young and 12 older healthy adults and 30 cardiac patients cycling at half of peak power; nose-only breathing gave a lower breathing rate and ventilation and larger breaths than mouth-only breathing in every group; exercise, not rest)
Mapelli M et al. 2025, *PLoS One* 20(7):e0326661. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0326661 (Italy; 12 healthy adults; at rest, breathing rate, breath volume and ventilation did not differ between nose-only, mouth-only, normal and one-nostril breathing; at peak effort, nose-only breathing gave a lower breathing rate and ventilation)
Watso JC et al. 2023, *Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol* 325(6):R797–R808. https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00148.2023 (USA; 20 healthy young adults, randomised crossover; five minutes each of nose-only and mouth-only breathing at rest, paced at the same rate; nose breathing lowered diastolic blood pressure by about 4 mmHg and raised the calming (parasympathetic) share of heart-rate variability; no such benefit during light cycling)
Zaccaro A et al. 2022, *Front Syst Neurosci* 16:803904. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnsys.2022.803904 (Italy; 12 experienced meditators, randomised crossover; 15 minutes of breathing at 2.5 breaths a minute through the nose or through the mouth with the nose clipped; after nose breathing, lower anxiety, more joy, stronger sense of an altered state and more slow-wave brain activity; heart-rate variability did not differ; small sample, and the two conditions could not be hidden from participants)
Zelano C et al. 2016, *J Neurosci* 36(49):12448–12467. https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2586-16.2016 (USA; brain recordings in 7 epilepsy patients plus behavioural tests in healthy adults; activity in the brain's smell, emotion and memory areas rose and fell with nasal breathing and faded when breathing was switched to the mouth; did not measure calm, anxiety or sleep)

### Does a slow exhale mechanically activate the vagus nerve?

Slow breathing produces significant effects on the autonomic nervous system, including changes in heart-rate variability, respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and sympathovagal balance. These findings support the claim that a slow exhale shifts autonomic balance toward the parasympathetic (vagal) side.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817

### Does regular breathing practice train the vagus nerve to stay calmer?

Slow breathing reliably shifts the nervous system toward its calming (vagal) side, and with regular practice the reflexes that steady heart rate become more responsive. It has not been directly proven that regular practice also keeps the stress response lower over time, though that fits the link between vagal activity and stress regulation.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817 (review; slow breathing raised vagal activity in healthy people, balancing rather than switching off the stress side)
Lehrer PM, Gevirtz R 2014, *Front Psychol* 5:756. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00756 (review; with twice-daily home practice over about three months, the resting heart-rate reflex (baroreflex) strengthened)

### Does a longer exhale slow the heart?

Yes. Heart rate naturally rises on the in-breath and falls on the out-breath, a normal rhythm called respiratory sinus arrhythmia. Lengthening the exhale and pausing after it gives more of each breath to the slowing phase.

Russo MA, Santarelli DM, O'Rourke D 2017, *Breathe* 13:298–309. https://doi.org/10.1183/20734735.009817 (review; heart rate rises on the in-breath and falls on the out-breath; some studies find variation is largest with equal in- and out-breaths)

<!-- endnote -->

### Does regular meditation or slow breathing train the calming system?

Yes. Regular slow-breathing practice makes the body's calming responses stronger over time: studies of heart-rate-variability training find the reflexes that steady heart rate become more responsive after a few months of regular practice. Mindfulness meditation also improves sleep quality in randomised trials.

Lehrer PM, Gevirtz R 2014, *Front Psychol* 5:756. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00756 (review; with twice-daily home practice over about three months, the resting heart-rate reflex (baroreflex) strengthened)
Rusch HL et al. 2019, *Ann N Y Acad Sci* 1445:5–16. https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.13996 (meta-analysis of 18 randomised trials, 1,654 people; improved sleep quality in people with sleep problems compared with matched controls; the comparison with established sleep treatments rests on weaker evidence)

### Does the 528 Hz frequency itself aid sleep?

A neutral cue repeated consistently during meditation can become a conditioned calm signal through classical conditioning; that mechanism is sound. The separate claim that the 528 Hz frequency confers a physiological benefit beyond conditioning rests on one small, preliminary study, which found salivary cortisol fell and oxytocin rose after 528 Hz music, with no change after 440 Hz; sleep was not measured.

## Chapter 21 — Napping

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Do naps clear adenosine, and do long or late naps spend the resource? -->
### Do naps use up the sleep pressure you need for the night?

Yes. Sleep of any kind, including a nap, clears some of the sleep pressure that has built up since waking, though naps clear it less effectively than a night's sleep. A short nap earlier in the day takes the edge off with little effect on the night, while a long or late nap leaves less pressure at bedtime: after an early-evening nap, healthy young men took longer to fall asleep and had less deep sleep early in the night.

Tarokh L et al. 2021, *J Sleep Res* 30(5):e13295. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13295 (46 healthy people aged 10–23; six 90-minute naps over 24 hours after a short night cleared less sleep pressure than a 9-hour night's sleep, which the authors attribute to the body clock holding sleep off at some times of day; multiple naps rather than a single nap)
Werth E, Dijk DJ, Achermann P, Borbély AA 1996, *Am J Physiol* 271(3):R501–R510. https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.1996.271.3.R501 (healthy young men; after an early-evening nap, sleep onset took longer and early-night deep-sleep activity was lower than on a baseline night)
Dijk DJ 2009, *J Clin Sleep Med* 5(2 Suppl):S6–S15. https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.5.2S.S6 (review; naps in the late afternoon or early evening reduced deep sleep early in the following night)
Sleep Health Foundation (AU), *Napping*. https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/napping (fact sheet; long or late naps can make it harder to fall asleep at the usual time; 15–30 minutes best)

<!-- endnote -->

Keeping naps seven to eight hours or more before bedtime is the book's advice. The Sleep Foundation (US) suggests at least eight hours and cautions against napping after 3 p.m., and Mayo Clinic gives similar advice. The Sleep Health Foundation (AU) puts the best nap length at 15–30 minutes.

Sleep Foundation (US), *Does Napping Impact Your Sleep at Night?* (updated 22 June 2026). https://www.sleepfoundation.org/how-sleep-works/does-napping-impact-sleep-at-night

## Chapter 22 — Exercise

### Does exercise spike cortisol, and does sleep deprivation slow its clearance?

Moderate-to-high intensity exercise raises cortisol substantially (approximately 40-83% at 60-80% of VO2max; low-intensity exercise actually lowers it). A recovered body clears this cortisol within a few hours. Sleep-deprived bodies recover more slowly from the same exercise stress.

Hill EE et al. 2008, *J Endocrinol Invest* 31:587. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18787373/

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- cross-reference note (added 2026-10-19, KB sync); substantiation in Chapter 7 -->
### Does exercise raise cortisol, and does it settle afterwards?

Yes. Harder exercise raises cortisol, and in a well-recovered body it returns to baseline within hours. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 7, in the note "Does cortisol return to baseline once a demand passes?"

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- cross-reference note (added 2026-10-19, KB sync); substantiation in Chapter 7 -->
### Do sleep-deprived people recover more slowly?

Sleep loss leaves cortisol higher the following evening, which adds to what exercise brings. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 7, in the note "Does a bad night raise cortisol the next day?"

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- added 2026-10-19, KB sync: Notes-only note -->
### Does morning exercise suit the body's cortisol rhythm?

Cortisol is naturally at its highest in the morning and falls through the day, so exercise early adds to a level that is already high, rather than raising it in the evening when it should be falling.

Chan S, Debono M 2010, *Ther Adv Endocrinol Metab* 1:129–138. https://doi.org/10.1177/2042018810380214 (daily cortisol rhythm; the timing benefit is inferred from the rhythm rather than tested directly)

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: How long does intense exercise elevate body temperature? -->
### Does hard exercise keep the body hot after you stop?

Yes. Intense exercise raises core body temperature, and the body's ability to shed that heat stays reduced for a period after exercise ends, so it can take an hour or more to return to normal depending on how hard and how long the session was.

Kenny GP, McGinn R 2017, *J Appl Physiol* 122:933–944. https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00517.2016 (review of heat loss after exercise)

### Do both exercise intensity and timing affect sleep?

Both the intensity and timing of exercise affect sleep. Vigorous exercise close to bedtime impairs sleep; moderate exercise at appropriate times supports it. The key variables interact — a moderate walk in the evening is typically benign; a hard session within the four-hour window before bed is not.

Stutz J, Eiholzer R, Spengler CM 2019, *Sports Med* 49:269–287. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-1015-0

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does regular exercise lower cortisol and improve sleep quality?

A systematic review and meta-analysis found that regular physical activity lowers cortisol and improves sleep quality. Most of the trials were in women with breast cancer, so the size of the effect in otherwise healthy people is less certain, but the direction is consistent with the broader exercise-physiology literature.

De Nys L et al. 2022, *Psychoneuroendocrinology* 143:105843. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2022.105843 (meta-analysis of 10 randomised trials, 756 adults, 90% women, six trials in breast cancer, none over 65; cortisol standardised mean difference −0.37, moderate certainty; sleep quality −0.30, low certainty; no trial linked the cortisol and sleep changes)

<!-- endnote -->

### How close to bedtime can you exercise vigorously?

A large wearable study found exercise ending at least four hours before sleep was not linked to sleep changes, while later high-strain sessions were linked to later and shorter sleep, poorer sleep quality, a higher night-time heart rate and lower heart-rate variability. A meta-analysis of laboratory studies found evening exercise generally did not harm sleep, with possible problems only after vigorous exercise ending within an hour of bed. The six-hour margin is the book's own, more cautious choice.

Leota J et al. 2025, *Nat Commun* 16:3297. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58271-x (14,689 wearable users, about 4 million nights; observational)
Stutz J, Eiholzer R, Spengler CM 2019, *Sports Med* 49:269–287. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-1015-0 (23 studies of a single evening session in healthy good sleepers; the one-hour finding rested largely on one study)

### Is finishing vigorous exercise six hours before bed a safe personal margin?

The research supports four hours as the threshold at which vigorous exercise is not associated with sleep changes. An individual choosing six hours as a personal conservative margin is not contradicted by the evidence — it simply exceeds the research floor. The book frames this explicitly as personal self-experimentation, which is appropriate.

Leota J et al. 2025, *Nat Commun* 16:3297. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58271-x

### Do yoga and tai chi help sleep?

Yoga and tai chi have been found to improve sleep quality in a number of studies. Both combine gentle movement with slow breathing and attention to the body, the same ingredients as the body scan. Gentle forms are low-intensity exercise, a level that does not raise cortisol, which makes them suited to the evening.

## Chapter 23 — Social Media

### Can you not blue-light-filter your way out of screen-related sleep disruption?

The NSF expert panel reached consensus that screen content impairs sleep; it did not reach consensus on screen light as the mechanism. Blue-light filtering addresses only the light component, not the stimulating or emotionally engaging content that is the more reliable disruptor.

Hartstein LE et al. 2024, *Sleep Health* 10:373–384. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2024.05.001

<!-- endnote -->
### Does screen content matter more than screen light for delaying sleep?

Probably. Blue-light filters help little: a 2024 National Sleep Foundation expert panel agreed that pre-bed screen content harms sleep in children and adolescents but could not agree that screen light does at any age, and in young adults phone light lowers melatonin before bed yet changes sleep only slightly. The adult picture is less settled: the same panel reached no agreement for adults, and a home study found short, focused use did no harm, while long or multitasking sessions were linked to later bedtimes and less sleep.

Hartstein LE et al. 2024, *Sleep Health* 10:373–384. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2024.05.001 (National Sleep Foundation expert consensus; votes for children, adolescents and adults; content agreed for children and adolescents, light for no age group, nothing for adults; blue-light filters showed minimal benefit)
Höhn C et al. 2024, *Brain Commun* 6(3):fcae173. https://doi.org/10.1093/braincomms/fcae173 (35 young men in Austria reading the same stories for 90 min on a phone, a filtered phone or a book; melatonin still lower at bedtime after the phone; slightly less deep sleep in the first two hours; real-world content not tested)
Chang AM et al. 2015, *PNAS* 112:1232–1237. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112 (four hours of tablet reading delayed sleep onset by about ten minutes)
Ellithorpe ME et al. 2022, *J Sleep Res* 31(5):e13551. https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13551 (58 US adults; home EEG and media diaries; deep and REM sleep unaffected)

## Chapter 24 — Hydration

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
### Does mild dehydration raise cortisol?

A systematic review of experimentally induced dehydration found that cortisol rises when the body is short of water. Mild dehydration from simply not drinking enough is a reasonable extension of this finding rather than something directly demonstrated.

Zaplatosch ME, Adams WM 2020, *Nutrients* 12:2526. https://doi.org/10.3390/nu12092526 (systematic review and meta-analysis, 21 articles in the review overall; cortisol higher with dehydration, standardised mean difference about 1.12)

### Does mild dehydration register as a low-level stress state?

See Chapter 24: Does mild dehydration raise cortisol? — the cortisol elevation associated with hypohydration is the physiological basis for describing dehydration as registering as a low-level stress state.

<!-- endnote -->

### Does dehydration make it harder for the body to cool?

Yes. Dehydration reduces sweating and blood flow to the skin, the body's main routes for shedding heat, so core temperature runs higher.

Sawka MN et al. (American College of Sports Medicine) 2007, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 39:377–390. https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597 (position stand; evidence from exercise and heat; not tested at rest or before sleep)

### Does cooling down help trigger sleep onset?

A falling core temperature is a key trigger for sleep onset; NREM onset is most closely aligned with the steepest rate of core-temperature decline. Anything that impairs the body's ability to cool — including dehydration and a warm room — can delay or disrupt sleep onset.

Harding EC, Franks NP, Wisden W 2019, *Front Neurosci* 13:336. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.00336

### Does cooling down help start sleep?

Yes. A drop in core body temperature is part of how sleep begins. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 13, in the note "How much does body temperature drop at sleep onset?"

### Does cortisol make the bladder signal louder during lighter sleep?

Urine production normally falls at night, partly because the hormone vasopressin rises overnight. Deep sleep is the hardest stage to wake from, so in lighter or broken sleep a filling bladder is more likely to wake a person, and sleep problems are a recognised cause of waking to urinate. That raised cortisol adds to this by lightening sleep is the book's reasoning.

### Can you rely on thirst, and what does urine colour tell you?

Most everyday drinking happens before thirst sets in. Thirst is a less reliable guide in older people and during exercise, so drinking regularly through the day is the usual advice. Urine colour is a simple guide: pale yellow is healthy, while dark, strong-smelling urine is a sign to drink more.

NHS inform, *Hydration*. https://www.nhsinform.scot/campaigns/hydration/ (urine colour chart; drink regularly through the day)

Yes. European dietary guidance sets adequate daily water intake at about two litres for women and two and a half for men, with roughly a fifth usually coming from food, which leaves around one and a half to two litres to drink. Needs rise with heat and activity.

Urine colour is a readily available, zero-cost hydration marker: pale straw indicates good hydration; dark yellow indicates dehydration. NHS hydration guidance uses urine colour as the simplest monitoring tool.

### Does sleep deprivation reduce self-control and willpower?

Sleep deprivation measurably reduces capacity for self-control. A sleep-deprived individual who has expended self-regulatory resources is at increased risk of succumbing to impulsive desires, impaired attention, and compromised decision-making. See Does sleep deprivation reduce self-control? (Chapter 14, same source).

Pilcher JJ, Morris DM, Donnelly J, Feigl HB 2015, *Front Hum Neurosci* 9:284. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00284

### Does sleep loss reduce willpower?

Yes. Sleep loss impairs the ability to resist impulses. The evidence and sources are in Chapter 14, in the note "Does sleep loss reduce self-control?"

<!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does poor sleep raise ghrelin and lower leptin? -->
### Does poor sleep make you hungrier, especially for sweet food?

Yes. In a controlled study, two nights of short sleep raised the hunger hormone ghrelin, lowered the fullness hormone leptin, and increased appetite, most of all for calorie-dense, carbohydrate-rich foods such as sweets. Ghrelin also acts on the brain's reward system, increasing the pull of food cues.

Spiegel K et al. 2004, *Ann Intern Med* 141:846–850. https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-141-11-200412070-00008 (12 healthy young men; two nights of four hours in bed vs ten; later studies vary on leptin)
Malik S, McGlone F, Bedrossian D, Dagher A 2008, *Cell Metab* 7:400–409. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2008.03.007 (ghrelin increased brain reward-area responses to food pictures)

<!-- endnote tag removed 2026-10-19 (KB sync): merged into the Notes entry 'Does poor sleep make you hungrier, especially for sweet food?' -->
### Does ghrelin tilt cravings toward sweet or fatty foods?

Sleep restriction is associated with increased appetite specifically for calorie-dense, high-carbohydrate foods (increases of 33-45%). The ghrelin-driven appetite shift tilts cravings toward sweet and starchy foods in particular; the extension to fatty foods is a reasonable inference supported by later research on ghrelin and the reward system.

Spiegel K et al. 2004, *Ann Intern Med* 141:846–850. https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-141-11-200412070-00008

<!-- endnote -->
### Can a high-sugar diet affect the cortisol system over time?

There are signs it can. A glucose drink before a stressful task amplifies the cortisol response, people who habitually eat more sugar show a weaker cortisol response to stress, and people with type 2 diabetes have a flatter daily cortisol rhythm with higher cortisol at bedtime. Together these suggest the two systems are linked, but no study has yet followed people over time to show that a high-sugar diet changes the cortisol system.

Gonzalez-Bono E et al. 2002, *Horm Behav* 41:328–333. https://doi.org/10.1006/hbeh.2002.1766 (37 fasted healthy men, 8–10 per group; glucose, not protein or fat, amplified the cortisol response to a public-speaking stress test)
Di Polito N et al. 2023, *Nutrients* 15:209. https://doi.org/10.3390/nu15010209 (54 adults; higher habitual sugar intake linked to a weaker cortisol response; self-reported diet)
Hackett RA, Steptoe A, Kumari M 2014, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 99:4625–4631. https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2014-2459 (Whitehall II, 3,508 adults; cross-sectional)

### Does the brain run on glucose and treat a glucose drop as an emergency?

The brain normally relies on circulating blood glucose as a primary energy source, accounting for 60-80% of total body glucose uptake. When blood glucose falls, the brain registers this as an emergency and triggers counter-regulatory hormones.

Verberne AJ, Sabetghadam A, Korim WS 2014, *Front Neurosci* 8:38. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2014.00038

### Does cortisol mobilise energy to correct a glucose drop?

Cortisol is a counter-regulatory hormone that activates hepatic gluconeogenesis to raise plasma glucose and reduces insulin-dependent glucose uptake. It is one of several hormones the body uses to correct hypoglycaemia.

Verberne AJ, Sabetghadam A, Korim WS 2014, *Front Neurosci* 8:38. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2014.00038

<!-- contested --> <!-- endnote -->
<!-- was: Does a sugar crash cause glucose to drop below baseline? -->
### Can an evening sugar hit cause a 2–3 a.m. wake?

Reactive (postprandial) hypoglycaemia is a recognised phenomenon in which the insulin response to a carbohydrate load overshoots, driving blood glucose below fasting baseline roughly two to five hours after eating. The full evening-sugar-to-2 a.m.-waking chain is built from established physiological parts: reactive hypoglycaemia is real, the brain triggers a counter-regulatory response to falling glucose, and cortisol runs higher in the small hours anyway. In that response, glucagon and adrenaline act first, and cortisol follows over several hours. It has not been directly proven that the whole sequence happens, start to finish, in healthy people.

Stuart K, Field A, Raju J, Ramachandran S 2013, *Case Rep Med* 2013:273957. https://doi.org/10.1155/2013/273957 (case report of two patients)
Sweatt SK et al. 2026, *Nutrients* 18(5):822. https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18050822 (69 non-diabetic adults with obesity; defines reactive hypoglycaemia from glucose curves after a liquid meal; sleep not measured)
Verberne AJ, Sabetghadam A, Korim WS 2014, *Front Neurosci* 8:38. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2014.00038 (review; glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol and growth hormone all take part in the counter-regulatory response)
Pruessner JC, Meier M 2026, *Compr Psychoneuroendocrinol* 27:100374. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpnec.2026.100374 (narrative review; when blood glucose falls, glucagon and adrenaline act immediately, while cortisol and growth hormone act over several hours and matter most in prolonged low glucose)

### Does a late sugar hit trigger a cortisol surge that disrupts the 2-3am handover?

The mechanism — late sugar load to postprandial glucose spike to reactive insulin overshoot to glucose drop to cortisol counter-regulatory surge to disruption of the 2am melatonin-cortisol handover — is built from established physiological parts, each of which is individually sourced. The full nocturnal chain from evening sugar to a 2-3am waking has not been demonstrated end-to-end in healthy people. The book already hedges this appropriately.

Stuart K, Field A, Raju J, Ramachandran S 2013, *Case Rep Med* 2013:273957. https://doi.org/10.1155/2013/273957
Sweatt SK et al. 2026, *Nutrients* 18(5). https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12986748
Verberne AJ, Sabetghadam A, Korim WS 2014, *Front Neurosci* 8:38. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2014.00038

### Do food sensitivities generate a background immune response?

Some food reactions that do not involve allergy antibodies are clinically recognised, though hard to diagnose, and diet is linked to low-grade inflammation, a link that is widely studied though hard to measure reliably in people. It has not been directly proven that individual food sensitivities keep a low-grade immune response running in the background. Blood tests for food-specific IgG4 antibodies do not identify which foods to avoid.

Myszkowska D et al. 2021, *Medicina (Kaunas)* 57(11):1245. https://doi.org/10.3390/medicina57111245 (case study of four women; raised food IgG4 and cytotoxic test results not clinically relevant and not a basis for cutting foods)
Minihane AM et al. 2015, *Br J Nutr* 114(7):999–1012. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114515002093 (dietary patterns and chronic low-grade inflammation)

### Can food sensitivity cause disrupted sleep and brain fog?

Possibly, though the link is indirect. Food intolerances are recognised causes of gut symptoms, headaches and fatigue, and natural food chemicals called amines can trigger nasal congestion in some people; nasal congestion is a common cause of poor sleep, and treating it improves sleep. Brain fog is not among the symptoms of food intolerance listed by the Australasian clinical body. Because these symptoms have many possible causes, the usual advice is to have a doctor check for coeliac disease and other conditions before putting them down to a food.

Australasian Society of Clinical Immunology and Allergy (ASCIA), *Food Intolerance*. https://www.allergy.org.au/patients/food-other-adverse-reactions/food-intolerance (peak clinical body for Australia and New Zealand; intolerance symptoms include gut upset, headaches, fatigue and a runny nose; food amines can trigger nasal congestion in some people; check for other conditions first, then a supervised temporary elimination diet)
Lunn M, Craig T 2011, *Sleep Med Rev* 15(5):293–299. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2010.12.001 (review of allergic rhinitis; nasal congestion is thought to be a key cause of poor sleep, and treatments that relieve it improve sleep and daytime tiredness)

### Are gluten, dairy, eggs, and soy the most common triggers?

Wheat (gluten), milk (dairy), eggs and soy are among the most common food allergens: they are among the eight foods that account for most food allergies, and the same foods are often behind intolerance in people who are not allergic to them. How often each causes problems varies by population and by how "reactivity" is defined.

Cleveland Clinic, *Elimination Diet*. https://health.clevelandclinic.org/elimination-diet (eight foods account for about 90% of food allergies and are often behind intolerance too; a dietitian's guidance, no references)

### Is an elimination diet the best way to find a food sensitivity?

Yes. Removing a suspected food for a few weeks and then reintroducing it is the standard clinical approach for identifying food intolerances, and no blood test replaces it.

Cleveland Clinic, *Elimination Diet*. https://health.clevelandclinic.org/elimination-diet
American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (AAAAI). https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/igg-food-test (IgG food panels do not diagnose food allergy or intolerance)

### Are IgG food-sensitivity tests reliable?

IgG food-sensitivity tests are widely available. They are sold directly to the public and offered by many naturopaths and integrative practitioners, and many people report that the tests helped them find the foods behind ongoing symptoms. A small number of controlled trials back up that experience: in people with irritable bowel syndrome, and separately in people with migraine, cutting out the foods an IgG test flagged reduced symptoms more than a comparison diet did. The major allergy bodies in Australia, Europe and the United States do not recommend the tests. Raised food IgG is common in healthy people and may only show that a food is eaten often. The trials were small and have not been widely repeated, so the results work best as a starting point for an elimination diet with reintroduction, which is still the standard way to confirm a sensitivity.

Atkinson W, Sheldon TA, Shaath N, Whorwell PJ 2004, *Gut* 53(10):1459–1464. https://doi.org/10.1136/gut.2003.037697 (150 adults with IBS, 12 weeks; the IgG-guided diet reduced symptom scores about 10% more than a sham diet, and about 26% more in people who kept to it fully)
Alpay K et al. 2010, *Cephalalgia* 30(7):829–837. https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102410361404 (30 adults with migraine, crossover design; fewer headache days and attacks on the IgG-guided elimination diet; the authors call for larger studies)
Stapel SO et al. (EAACI Task Force) 2008, *Allergy* 63:793. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1398-9995.2008.01705.x
American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (AAAAI). https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/igg-food-test
Australasian Society of Clinical Immunology and Allergy (ASCIA), *Evidence based versus non evidence based tests and treatments*. https://www.allergy.org.au/patients/allergy-testing/evidence-based-versus-non-evidence-based-tests-and-treatments (lists food IgG testing as not evidence-based, citing the risk of misdiagnosis and unnecessary diet restrictions)
