# Author Style Notes
*Derived from sample chapter — first-person sleep/health memoir*
*Updated to reflect confirmed three-register editorial structure*

---

## Book Structure — Editorial Decision

This book operates in three registers, confirmed at editorial review:

- **Chapters 1–3: Memoir register.** These chapters carry the concentrated personal narrative — the backstory, the years of wrong thinking, the suffering, and the decision to change. They establish the author as narrator-as-patient-zero and build the trust the rest of the book depends on. Chapter 1a is the voice baseline for the entire manuscript.

- **Chapters 4–9: Explainer-with-personal-grounding.** The author explains science, habits, and frameworks — but is never absent. Every chapter requires at least one specific, named personal moment that anchors the explanation in lived experience. This is not memoir volume; it is memoir presence. A sentence of throat-clearing ("as someone who struggled with sleep...") does not meet this standard. A concrete, named moment does.

- **Part Four onward: Habit chapters.** These chapters carry the doing work of the book. They operate in the explainer-with-personal-grounding register.

- **Personal thread recurrence.** The author's experience should resurface at structural inflection points across the manuscript — when a new section begins, when a major idea lands, and fully in the closing chapter. The arc from Ch. 1a to the close must feel inhabited, not just framed.

- **Chapter 1 is the voice baseline, not the voice standard for every chapter.** It represents peak memoir register. All chapters are assessed against its quality of voice, specificity, and honesty — not its depth of personal narrative.

---

## Voice & Persona

- **Narrator-as-patient-zero.** The author writes from lived experience. Claims, observations, and insights are grounded in their own body, struggle, and eventual recovery. There is no detached expert voice — only a peer who has been through it. In Chs. 3 onward, this grounding appears at minimum once per chapter as a specific personal moment; it does not need to sustain throughout.
- **Glass-half-full self-awareness.** The author acknowledges being an optimist by nature, which makes the depth of their suffering more credible, not less. They do not wallow or catastrophise; they report.
- **Self-deprecating but not self-pitying.** Humour and honesty about personal failings (low willpower, wrong assumptions, years wasted) are used to build trust, not sympathy. The tone is "I got this wrong, here's why, and here's what actually worked."
- **Reluctant protagonist.** The author did not fix their sleep through iron discipline — they crawled through it, grudgingly, one small step at a time. This is a deliberate part of the voice and must not be softened into a tidy hero's journey.

---

## Tone

- Warm, direct, and conversational — never clinical or preachy.
- Honest to the point of being unflattering about oneself.
- Occasional dry humour (e.g., "The only downside? Admitting my wife was right all along.").
- Never hyperbolic about results — claims are measured and qualified ("most nights of the week", "much less often").
- Empathetic toward the reader without being patronising. The author speaks *to* the reader, not *at* them.

---

## Sentence & Paragraph Structure

- **Short sentences are the default.** Long sentences are used sparingly, usually to mirror complexity or accumulation. Many paragraphs are a single sentence.
- **Lists within prose.** The author uses sentence fragments and stacked short clauses to create list-like rhythm without bullet points (e.g., "Tired but wired. A terrible sleeper. Constantly lacking energy."). Fragments are a deliberate emphasis tool, not a default rhythm — reserve them for moments that warrant it. A single flowing sentence is often preferable to staccato fragments that perform energy rather than earn it.
- **Repetition for emphasis.** Key words and phrases recur deliberately — "willpower", "holistic", "incremental", "stick" — to reinforce the core argument without stating it abstractly.
- **Rhetorical questions used sparingly** to voice the reader's likely internal objection before answering it.
- **Paragraph breaks are frequent.** The author rarely runs more than three or four sentences in a single block. White space is part of the rhythm.

---

## Structural Patterns

- **Open with symptoms, not diagnosis.** Chapters and sections tend to begin with felt experience before introducing the explanatory framework. This applies in explainer chapters as much as memoir chapters — the personal moment that grounds each chapter should come early, not be appended at the end.
- **Name the wrong thinking before correcting it.** The author consistently structures around a flawed assumption they held, then shows why it was wrong. Subheadings like "Thinking it was just me" and "Looking for the silver bullet" are the wrong belief — the section then dismantles it.
- **Science as validation, not authority.** When scientific concepts appear (prefrontal cortex, amygdala), they are introduced to explain and vindicate the author's experience, not to establish credibility through jargon. Technical terms are immediately translated into plain language.
- **Concrete specifics over generalities.** Abstract advice is grounded in personal example wherever possible (the coffee habit, the WHOOP tracker, the five-to-ten minute increments). In explainer chapters, at least one such specific is required per chapter; not every claim needs personal grounding.
- **Show the emotional mechanism, don't name the feeling.** When writing personal moments, the author describes the behaviour that reveals the emotional state rather than labelling it. "The machine was how I instantly and reliably swapped out that feeling" is preferred over "it was a deeply embedded habit." The reader infers the psychology from the action.
- **Results are earned late.** The payoff — what actually changed — comes near the end of a section or chapter, after the struggle has been fully laid out.

---

## Personal Grounding — Minimum Standard for Explainer Chapters

Each chapter from Ch. 3 onward must contain at minimum one personal moment that meets all three of these criteria:

1. **Specific** — a named detail, not a general observation (e.g., "my 3 pm coffee" not "I used to drink too much coffee").
2. **Load-bearing** — it illuminates the chapter's core idea, not just colours the tone.
3. **In the author's voice** — not throat-clearing, not a parenthetical aside, not a generalised admission.

Where a chapter currently lacks this, the author needs to supply the raw material. It cannot be invented or implied.

---

## Language Preferences

- Plain, conversational English throughout. No jargon without immediate plain-language translation.
- Contractions used naturally ("I'm", "it's", "didn't", "wasn't").
- Australian/British spelling conventions (no "z" in words like "realise", "organisation").
- Colloquial phrases that feel lived-in rather than performed (e.g., "a tonne of bricks", "the dreaded afternoon dip", "pat on the back").
- Avoids passive voice. Agency is always assigned — usually to the author themselves.
- Numbers under ten are written as words in narrative prose; specific measurements and timeframes use numerals (e.g., "4–5 hours", "9+ hours", "5–10 minutes").

---

## What to Preserve

- The author's willingness to admit failure, confusion, and wasted years — this is central to the credibility of the eventual solution.
- Qualified claims. Do not amplify results beyond what the author states. "Most nights" should stay "most nights."
- The personal anecdotes (the coffee habit, the wife remark, the WHOOP tracker) — these are not padding; they are the proof.
- The structural move of naming a wrong belief as a subheading before dismantling it.
- Short, punchy paragraph endings that land a point and move on.

---

## What to Avoid

- Do not smooth the voice into generic self-help register — the author is not a wellness brand. The author's editing instinct is toward plainness, not polish — when in doubt, cut the elaboration rather than refine it. Implied meaning is preferred over stated meaning; trust the reader to fill in what's obvious.
- Do not add hedging language the author has not used (e.g., "you might find that...", "some people experience...").
- Do not inflate the science. If the author mentions a brain region, keep it brief and immediately translated.
- Do not restructure the symptom-first pattern into a conclusions-first pattern — the author's readers need to see themselves in the struggle before they trust the solution.
- Do not use bullet points in place of the author's natural prose lists — the stacked fragment style is intentional.
- Do not treat the absence of sustained memoir in Chs. 3 onward as a licence to remove personal voice entirely. Explainer register and personal absence are not the same thing.
- Do not substitute generalised first-person commentary ("I found this interesting", "I learned a lot about this") for the specific named personal moments the structure requires.
- Do not open a chapter with administrative throat-clearing or a chapter-contents summary.
