# Study Summary Template

This file holds two templates:

- **Study template:** for original research (trials, cohort studies, meta-analyses).
- **Reputable body template:** for guidelines and statements from bodies such as the CDC, NINDS, WHO or AASM.

Every summary in either template must include a working link to the source. A summary without a link is incomplete.

---

# Study template

Every study summary has a heading, then two parts: a study summary written in plain spoken English, and quick facts covering anything the summary didn't already include.

---

## Heading

State the main takeaway in 10 words or fewer, as a headline.
> Example: Six-Hour Nights Impair You Like All-Nighters, Without You Noticing

---

## Part 1: Study Summary

**1. Opening**
> In a [year] study at [institution] in [country], researchers [did what] to [number] [descriptor]…

The descriptor names the group a listener would picture: age band, sex mix, and anything notable. Examples: "healthy young men and women", "older adults with insomnia", "male night-shift workers". Give no numbers for the split.

**2. Method in practice**
Describe what participants actually did and experienced:
- Where they stayed and for how long
- How the intervention worked, and who got what (groups or conditions)
- What they could and couldn't do (naps, caffeine, schedule)
- How they were tested, described from the participant's side

**3. Results**
Say what happened in plain terms. Use real-world effects ("as impaired as an all-nighter"), not statistics. Include any surprising or human detail.

**4. The main takeaway**
State the study's central conclusion in one or two sentences, written so a listener could repeat it to a friend.

---

## Part 2: Quick facts

Include only fields not already covered in Part 1:
- **Authors**
- **Link (required):** DOI link preferred (https://doi.org/...), otherwise PubMed or the publisher's page
- **Published in:** journal, and whether it was peer reviewed or a preprint
- **Study type:** RCT, controlled lab trial, observational, cohort, meta-analysis, etc.
- **Age range:** exact, if known
- **Measures:** what was measured and how
- **Funding:** include only if relevant, e.g. industry-funded
- **Caveat:** main limitations and who the results may not apply to
- **Replication:** whether later work replicated or contradicted the findings
- **Evidence:** Strong / Moderate / Preliminary, with a short reason

## Worked example

### Six-Hour Nights Impair You Like All-Nighters, Without You Noticing

In a 2003 study at the University of Pennsylvania in the USA, researchers moved 48 healthy young men and women into a sleep lab for two weeks and controlled exactly how long each person could be in bed. Each person was put in one group: 4 hours a night, 6 hours, or a normal 8. A separate group was kept awake for three days straight. No naps, no caffeine, lights and schedule controlled.

Every two hours while awake, participants did a simple reaction test. They watched a screen and hit a button the moment a light appeared, for 10 minutes. Slow responses and missed lights counted as lapses. They also rated how sleepy they felt.

The 6-hour group got worse every single day. By day 14 they were as impaired as people who'd pulled an all-nighter, but they *felt* only a bit tired.

**The main takeaway:** Regularly sleeping six hours or less steadily erodes your performance as much as skipping entire nights of sleep. Because you stop feeling much sleepier, you can't rely on how you feel to judge how impaired you are.

**Quick facts**

- **Authors:** Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117
- **Published in:** *Sleep* (peer reviewed)
- **Study type:** Controlled laboratory trial
- **Age range:** 21–38
- **Measures:** Psychomotor vigilance task, working memory tests, self-rated sleepiness
- **Caveat:** Young, healthy sample in a controlled lab, so results may differ for older adults or people with sleep disorders
- **Evidence:** Strong (widely cited, consistent with later work)

# Reputable body template

For guidelines, position statements and fact pages from health bodies. These restate evidence rather than produce it, so there is no method paragraph, and the Basis field must say where the evidence actually comes from.

## Heading

State the main takeaway in 10 words or fewer, as a headline.

## Summary

Write two or three sentences:

> In a [year] guideline, [body] in [country] [stated/recommended what]…

Follow with the key figures or recommendation, then the basis in plain words.

## The main takeaway

State the body's central position in one or two sentences.

## Quick facts

- **Issued by:** the body, and what kind of organisation it is
- **Title:** the page or document title, with the date published or last updated
- **Link (required):** the direct URL to the page or document
- **Source type:** guideline, position statement, consensus statement, fact page, etc.
- **Basis:** the studies, consensus statements or reports the body relies on
- **Data sources:** include only if the body reports its own data
- **Caveat:** limitations the body states, plus whether its claims show association or cause
- **Evidence:** Strong / Moderate / Preliminary, with a short reason

## Worked example

### A Third of Adults Sleep Too Little, Raising Disease Risk

In a 2024 guideline, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in the USA defined short sleep as under 7 hours a night for adults, and set age-based targets for children and teens. Drawing on national surveys, it reported that about a third of US adults and children fall short, along with more than three in four high school students. The CDC links insufficient sleep to obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke, anxiety and depression, basing this on expert consensus from the leading US sleep medicine bodies.

**The main takeaway:** The CDC treats short sleep as a widespread public health risk tied to several major chronic diseases.

**Quick facts**

- **Issued by:** Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (US government agency)
- **Title:** *Chronic Disease Indicators: Sleep* (first published June 2024)
- **Link:** https://www.cdc.gov/cdi/indicator-definitions/sleep.html
- **Source type:** Public health guideline, not an original study
- **Basis:** AASM/SRS adult sleep consensus statement (Watson et al., 2015) and an Institute of Medicine report (2006)
- **Data sources:** BRFSS (adults), YRBSS (high school), National Survey of Children's Health (children)
- **Caveat:** Based on self-reported and parent-reported hours of sleep, without measuring sleep quality or sleep disorders. The disease links are associations, not proven cause and effect.
- **Evidence:** Strong as a statement of expert consensus. The underlying causal evidence is in the studies it cites.
