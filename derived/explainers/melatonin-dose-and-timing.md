## Core explanation
- Two forces: sleep pressure (adenosine, Ch5) and the body clock's wake signal, which peaks in the wake maintenance zone before habitual bedtime until the body's own melatonin rise opens the sleep gate (Rajaratnam 2004; Lavie 1997; Shochat 1997).
- Melatonin's direct effect is to damp the clock's wake signal. It helps when that signal is the obstacle, and only while it is in the blood (quick-release 0.5 mg half-life about 45 min, per Sletten 2018; 1–5 mg back to baseline in 4–8 h, Tordjman 2017).
- Night owl / slow sleep onset: obstacle is at the start of the night, so it helps onset (Sletten 2018 authors' explanation).
- Waking 2–3 am: own melatonin already high, dose gone; cause is falling sleep pressure plus arousal (Ch7). Exception: low overnight melatonin (Zhdanova 2001; slow-release trials).
- Night shift: high sleep pressure makes onset easy; later the daytime wake signal wakes them; melatonin in the body's day holds sleep a little longer (Liira 2014: +24 min, no faster onset; consistent with Rajaratnam 2004, Cruz-Sanabria 2024).
- Stress / hyperarousal: smallest benefit. Cruz-Sanabria 2024 found a smaller sleep-onset effect in insomnia vs healthy volunteers; the authors attribute it to arousal (fits Riemann 2010; Dressle 2022; Ch6 lines 43–45). Sletten 2018: a third of people referred with night-owl symptoms had no late clock.
- Book line: melatonin helps most when your body clock is the problem, least when stress is.
- Sourced 2026-10-28: two-process model (Borbély 2016); clock and sleep pressure contribute about equally to holding sleep together, and their normal timing consolidates night sleep (Dijk & Czeisler 1994); sleep attempted in the body's daytime is shorter and poorer because the clock's alerting signal competes with it (Sack 2007, AASM review); shortened sleep is a main acute problem of shift work (Åkerstedt 2003). The night/day synthesis joining these is reasoning, not tested.

## Two uses
- Tonight (sleep aid): works only on nights taken; doesn't move the clock much. Two timings, for two situations (corrected 2026-10-28):
  - At your usual bedtime, slow to fall asleep: 2–3 h before bed. At 1 h before, no clear gain over taking it at bedtime (Cruz-Sanabria 2024 Tables 4–5).
  - Moving your bedtime earlier: about 1 h before the new bedtime (Sletten 2018: bed about 2½ h earlier than habitual; dose about 1.4 h before the body's melatonin onset). Relative to the body clock, both timings land around or a little before the body's own melatonin rise (inference).
- Moving a bedtime earlier, practical single plan (book framing, 2026-10-28): dose about 1 h before the new bedtime, every night, with an earlier fixed wake time and bright morning light. Combines Sletten (dose timing; ~34 min benefit beyond the earlier bedtime alone; clock not significantly shifted in 4 weeks) and Revell 2006 (morning light and earlier waking move the clock). Untested as a combination; durability untested. Framed as moving towards a regular bedtime, not as a work-night workaround (author decision 2026-10-28).
- For good (clock shifter): early evening, several hours before the body's melatonin rise, timed to the current clock, with morning light and earlier waking (Burgess 2010; Revell 2006; Mundey 2005). Durability untested; maintenance needs fixed wake time, morning light, dim evenings (Micic 2016: late clocks run long and relapse).
- Jet lag uses both. Shift workers use only "tonight"; light handles the clock, aiming for a compromise (Folkard 2008: under 3% fully adjust; Smith 2009; Lowden 2019).
- Organising idea: when the world has moved, melatonin helps you catch up; when it hasn't, you move the clock yourself, gradually.

## Dose
- Clock shift: 0.5 mg ≈ 3 mg (Burgess 2010; Revell 2006).
- Sleep onset: grows to about 4 mg then flat; 1 mg about 7 min, 3–4 mg about 12 min faster than placebo (derived from Cruz-Sanabria 2024 Table 2); timing matters more than dose.
- Night waking: 0.3 mg beat 3 mg in older insomniacs (Zhdanova 2001; small).
- Jet lag: 0.5–5 mg; 5 mg faster onset, size unreported (Suhner 1998; Herxheimer 2002).
- Carryover: 3–4 mg raised about 10 h (Zhdanova 2001; Gooneratne 2012).
- "Sleeping longer" at night is mostly the faster start (Ferracioli-Oda 2013: 7 min onset, 8 min total).
- Practical rule: start 0.5 mg, time it right, increase toward 3–4 mg only if still slow to fall asleep; stay low for night waking.

## Timing
- At bedtime about 3–4 min faster; 2–3 h before up to about 20 min (Cruz-Sanabria 2024 Table 5).
- Clock shift: count back from current sleep time; 0.5 mg best about 5–6 h before it; 3 mg about 1–2 h earlier (Burgess 2010).
- Pace: about 1 h/day at best with morning light (Revell 2006); 2 h/day too fast (Eastman 2005); about 30 min/day reasonable for a genuine night owl.
- Avoid morning or end-of-sleep dosing (can delay the clock; Burgess 2010; Herxheimer 2002).

## Scenarios
- Jet lag: local bedtime, first few nights; not earlier in the local day. Pre-flight: earlier waking, morning light, afternoon melatonin (lab only).
- Roster: before day sleep, maybe +20–25 min via longer sleep (weak); light management first.
- Night owl, work night (Sletten 2018): habitual bed about 1 am (sleep onset about 1:20–1:30 am), desired about 10:30 pm, bed moved about 2½ h earlier in one jump on work nights; sleep onset about 1 h 50 earlier on melatonin vs 1 h 20 on placebo (the 34-min result); most still short of goal; clock shift not significant; 4 weeks, no week-by-week trajectory reported.
- Night owl, lasting change: stepped early-evening plan with morning light; very late clocks (asleep after 3–4 am) to a sleep clinic (Auger 2015 endorses timed melatonin).
- Why not the jet-lag jump at home: nothing in the environment shifts, a 10 pm bed lands in the night owl's wake maintenance zone, and it amounts to self-inflicted eastward jet lag.
