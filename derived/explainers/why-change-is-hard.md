# Explainer: why change is hard (a deeper view of willpower)

Background reading behind the book's willpower thread. Living document: update in place, don't duplicate. First written 2026-11-03 from a chat question: at a biological level, why do people want things to change but not change the things that would get them there?

**Status: not in the book.** None of these claims appear in the manuscript, so there are no KB entries for them and nothing here feeds `/book/endnotes.md`. Entries use the house "summary plus sources" format so any of them can be lifted into the KB as a chapter note if the book later goes this deep. Sources are S214–S239 in /derived/sources/ (status COLLECTED, background reading; see catalogue.md).

How to read it: each entry is a question heading, a neutral summary, and the source lines beneath (hard numbers and caveats are in the parentheticals). Anything marked as reasoning is not a sourced claim. Wiki pages [[Sleep loss and self-control]] and [[Incremental habit change]] point here.

## Where this touches the book
- Ch02: willpower "running on empty" as the reason the program is incremental.
- Ch14: cold turkey fails because sleep loss reduces self-control (KB note sourced to Pilcher 2015).
- Ch23: later habits are easier once some willpower is restored.
- Ch25: willpower as "a battery that poor sleep never fully recharges"; cravings up, willpower down.
- Ch26: the reader's starting belief that the fix is better willpower.

**Flag for a future audit (no proposal written):** the battery metaphor in Ch14 and Ch25 echoes ego-depletion theory, which large replications have not supported (see 'Is willpower a battery that runs down?'). The book's actual claim, that sleep loss reduces self-control, is sourced separately and is not affected. The author may want to check that the metaphor reads as a figure of speech rather than as the mechanism.

**Possible bridge to sleep (hypothesis):** the lead author of the glutamate study (Wiehler 2022) has said there is good evidence glutamate is cleared from synapses during sleep. If that holds, it links the cost of overriding habits to sleep restoring the capacity to do it, which fits Ch14 and Ch25 better than the battery image. Not tested in that study.

**Fits the book's method:** the habit-formation findings (gradual automaticity over weeks to months; a missed day doesn't reset it) and the if-then planning evidence support the book's incremental, one-habit-at-a-time design.

## Entries

### Why do people want change but not act on it?

Wanting to change and changing are only loosely linked. Reviews of many studies find that intentions turn into action only about half the time, and that when an intervention genuinely shifts what people intend, their behaviour shifts much less. Most of the gap is made up of people who mean to act but don't: they forget, miss the moment, put it off, lose track of progress, or are pulled back by habit. This is psychological evidence; it describes the gap rather than explaining its biology.

Sheeran P, Webb TL 2016, *Soc Personal Psychol Compass* 10:503–518. https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12265 (review; intentions turn into action about half the time, and a medium-to-large change in intention produces only a small-to-medium change in behaviour)

### Why does overriding a habit feel draining?

Repeated actions in a stable setting are gradually handed over to habit circuits in the basal ganglia and become the default way of responding. Habits then run with little attention: in hourly diaries, people's thoughts were on something else during about half of their habitual actions, and they rated habits as easier and less demanding than non-habitual ones. Doing something different means deliberately overriding that default, and studies consistently find that this kind of deliberate control is costly, in the specific sense researchers use: people avoid it, give up reward to escape it, find it unpleasant, and slip back to habit when their capacity is stretched. Why it is costly is less settled; the leading explanation is that deliberate control can only serve a few things at once, so the brain weighs whether a task is worth it, and one early study points to a chemical build-up in the brain's planning region after a long day of hard mental work.

**How researchers define and measure the cost**
- **By what people choose.** Offered two tasks that pay the same, people pick the less demanding one about two-thirds of the time, even when it is no quicker or more accurate and even when they haven't noticed the difference (Kool 2010).
- **By what people will give up.** People accept less money to do an easier version of a memory task; the amount they forgo is the measured cost, and it rises with difficulty (Westbrook 2013). This is called effort discounting.
- **By how it feels.** Effortful tasks raise blood pressure, sweating and pupil size, bring frowning and frustration, and track activity in the anterior cingulate cortex (Inzlicht 2018). The same review notes the paradox that people also value what effort earns.
- **By what gives way under load.** With a demanding second task in the lab, people's choices shift from deliberate planning to repeating what worked before (Otto 2013). In real life, students fell back more on their strong habits, good and bad, during exam weeks (Neal 2013).
- **As a calculation.** The leading theory treats deliberate control as worth applying only when its expected payoff outweighs its cost, weighed by the anterior cingulate cortex (Shenhav 2013); on one account, the felt strain reflects what else that limited capacity could be doing (Kurzban 2013).
- **As a possible chemical cost.** After about six hours of hard mental work, people leaned towards easier, sooner rewards and had more glutamate in the lateral prefrontal cortex than people who did easy work; the visual cortex showed no difference (Wiehler 2022). It is one small study, and the group difference came mainly from an unexpected fall in the easy group.

Graybiel AM 2008, *Annu Rev Neurosci* 31:359–387. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.neuro.29.051605.112851 (review; habits and routines form through learning-driven changes in basal ganglia circuits; largely animal research)
Wood W, Rünger D 2016, *Annu Rev Psychol* 67:289–314. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-122414-033417 (review; habits form as goals are pursued by repeating the same action in the same setting, and habits are the efficient default way of responding)
Wood W, Quinn JM, Kashy DA 2002, *J Pers Soc Psychol* 83:1281–1297. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.83.6.1281 (two hourly-diary studies, 70 and 209 students; a third to half of daily behaviours were habits, and during habits people's thoughts were on something else about half the time; habits were rated as needing less attention and less effort)
Kool W, McGuire JT, Rosen ZB, Botvinick MM 2010, *J Exp Psychol Gen* 139:665–682. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020198 (six experiments, about 270 young adults in total; offered two tasks with the same reward, people chose the less demanding one about 60–73% of the time, even when it was not faster or more accurate and when they had not noticed the difference)
Westbrook A, Kester D, Braver TS 2013, *PLoS One* 8:e68210. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0068210 (young and older adults chose between less money for an easier memory task and more money for a harder one; the reward they gave up measures the cost of effort, which rose with difficulty and differed with age and with how much people enjoy effortful thinking)
Inzlicht M, Shenhav A, Olivola CY 2018, *Trends Cogn Sci* 22:337–349. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2018.01.007 (review; people and animals avoid effort and discount rewards that require it, and effort raises blood pressure, sweating and pupil size and brings negative feelings; yet effort can also make outcomes feel more valuable)
Otto AR, Gershman SJ, Markman AB, Daw ND 2013, *Psychol Sci* 24:751–761. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612463080 (lab experiment; a demanding second task shifted people's choices from deliberate planning towards habit-like responding, trial by trial; sample size not saved)
Neal DT, Wood W, Drolet A 2013, *J Pers Soc Psychol* 104:959–975. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0032626 (five studies; in a 10-week field study of 65 students, exam weeks made people fall back more on their strong habits, good and bad, such as their usual breakfast; weak habits were unaffected; some studies used ego-depletion tasks, which have not replicated well)
Shenhav A, Botvinick MM, Cohen JD 2013, *Neuron* 79:217–240. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.07.007 (theory paper; proposes the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex weighs the payoff from deliberate control against its effort cost)
Kurzban R et al. 2013, *Behav Brain Sci* 36:661–679. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X12003196 (theory paper; argues the feeling of mental effort reflects what else the brain could be doing, not a physical fuel running down)
Wiehler A et al. 2022, *Curr Biol* 32. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.07.010 (40 young adults, about six hours of hard or easy mental work; the hard group shifted towards easier, sooner rewards and had higher glutamate in the lateral prefrontal cortex by the end of the day, not in the visual cortex; small study, and the group difference came mainly from an unexpected fall in the easy group)

### Does the brain favour rewards that come sooner?

Yes. People reliably value a reward less the longer they have to wait for it, and brain-imaging studies show the brain's valuation areas tracking this markdown. One influential study found reward areas responding especially to rewards available now, with planning areas more active when people chose to wait; a later study argued instead for a single system that marks down the future. Either way, the costs of change usually arrive now and its rewards later. The idea that steep discounting evolved because the ancestral future was unpredictable is a plausible argument that these studies do not test.

McClure SM et al. 2004, *Science* 306:503–507. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1100907 (brain-imaging study; reward areas responded most to options available now, while planning areas were engaged by all choices; small sample)
Kable JW, Glimcher PW 2007, *Nat Neurosci* 10:1625–1633. https://doi.org/10.1038/nn2007 (brain-imaging study; reward-area activity tracked each person's own discounted value of delayed money, suggesting one valuation system rather than separate 'now' and 'later' systems)

### Is uncertainty more stressful than a known bad outcome?

In a laboratory study using electric shocks, people felt most stressed, and their bodies reacted most, when they could not predict whether a shock was coming, more than when a shock was almost certain. Reviews of anxiety research reach a similar view: uncertainty about a possible threat is a central driver of anxiety. These findings come from short-term threats; no study here shows that the uncertainty of a life change triggers the same response, so applying them to change is a reasonable extension rather than a demonstrated fact.

de Berker AO et al. 2016, *Nat Commun* 7:10996. https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10996 (45 healthy adults; a computer game with electric shocks; stress ratings, pupil size and sweating were highest when a shock was least predictable, not when it was certain)
Grupe DW, Nitschke JB 2013, *Nat Rev Neurosci* 14:488–501. https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn3524 (review; uncertainty about a possible threat makes it harder to avoid or soften, and so produces anxiety)

### Do losses weigh more than equal gains?

On average, yes. A large pooled analysis of studies, mostly of choices about money, found that people weigh a loss about twice as heavily as an equal gain, and brain-imaging work shows reward areas reacting more sharply to possible losses than to possible gains. Some researchers argue that loss aversion is not a general rule and depends on the situation. Whether giving up a comfort or a routine counts as a loss in the same way as losing money has not been tested directly.

Brown AL, Imai T, Vieider FM, Camerer CF 2024, *J Econ Lit* 62:485–516. https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.20221698 (meta-analysis of 607 estimates from 150 articles, 1992–2017; losses weighed on average about twice as much as equal gains, 95% interval about 1.8 to 2.1)
Tom SM et al. 2007, *Science* 315:515–518. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1134239 (brain-imaging study of 50/50 gambles; reward areas rose with possible gains and fell more steeply with possible losses, and the brain response predicted each person's loss aversion; small sample)
Gal D, Rucker DD 2018, *J Consum Psychol* 28:497–516. https://doi.org/10.1002/jcpy.1047 (critical review; argues the evidence does not show losses are generally more powerful than gains, and that the effect depends on context)

### Does stress push people back to old habits?

Possibly, but the human evidence is weaker than it is often presented. Stress quickly impairs the prefrontal cortex, the brain's planning region, which is shown mostly in animals, and an influential 2009 experiment found that stressed people kept repeating an action for a food they no longer wanted. Two careful exact repeats of that experiment could not test the effect, because unstressed people did not behave in the expected goal-directed way either, and other attempts to create habits in the laboratory have failed. Falling back on old patterns under stress is a common experience, but the mechanism behind it has not been firmly established in people.

Arnsten AF 2009, *Nat Rev Neurosci* 10:410–422. https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2648 (review; even mild uncontrollable stress can rapidly impair the prefrontal cortex, the brain's planning area; much of the evidence is from animals)
Schwabe L, Wolf OT 2009, *J Neurosci* 29:7191–7198. https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0979-09.2009 (67 adults; after ice-water stress, people kept performing an action for a food they had already eaten their fill of, a sign of habit; exact replications in 2023 could not test this, see S228)
Smeets T et al. 2023, *Neurobiol Stress* 23:100528. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ynstr.2023.100528 (two preregistered exact replications of Schwabe & Wolf 2009 and 2010, planned samples of about 90 each; stress worked, but unstressed people did not show goal-directed behaviour either, so the shift to habit could not be tested)
de Wit S et al. 2018, *J Exp Psychol Gen* 147:1043–1065. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000402 (five experiments; extra training did not make people's lab behaviour more habit-like, unlike in rats, which raises questions about how habits are measured in human lab studies)

### Can imagining the goal sap the drive to reach it?

Some evidence suggests so. In four small experiments with university students, vividly imagining an ideal outcome slightly lowered blood pressure and self-reported energy, and in one study people who had done so got less done the following week. The effects were small and the samples small and mostly female. Why it happens is not established: the idea that the brain partly enjoys the reward in advance is an explanation, not something these studies measured. Pairing the imagined outcome with a clear look at the obstacle, the approach called mental contrasting, has a modest benefit across many studies.

Kappes HB, Oettingen G 2011, *J Exp Soc Psychol* 47:719–729. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2011.02.003 (four small experiments with university students; imagining an ideal outcome lowered blood pressure and self-reported energy compared with more questioning or neutral thoughts, and in one study people got less done the following week)
Cross A, Sheffield D 2019, *Health Psychol Rev* 13:209–225. https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2019.1594332 (meta-analysis, 1,528 participants; mental contrasting improved health behaviours with a small-to-moderate effect up to three months; adding if-then plans did not clearly add more)

### Does self-identity hold behaviour in place?

There is some support for the idea that identity matters. A pooled analysis found that how strongly people see a behaviour as part of who they are adds modestly to whether they intend to do it, beyond their attitudes, what others expect of them and what they have done before. Those studies are correlational and measured intentions rather than behaviour. Comparing identity to the body's homeostasis, and the idea that repetition updates the brain's model of the self, are analogies, not tested biology.

Rise J, Sheeran P, Hukkelberg S 2010, *J Appl Soc Psychol* 40:1085–1105. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1559-1816.2010.00611.x (meta-analysis; how strongly people see a behaviour as part of who they are added about 6% to the prediction of their intentions, beyond attitudes, norms, sense of control and past behaviour; correlational)

### Is willpower a battery that runs down?

The evidence does not support the simple battery version. Two very large preregistered tests, run across dozens of laboratories, one of them led by the theory's own supporters, found that using self-control on one task did not measurably weaken it on the next. The idea that self-control is fuelled by blood sugar has not held up either. An alternative view is that feeling mentally drained reflects the brain weighing what else it could be doing. This is separate from the book's claim that sleep loss reduces self-control, which rests on sleep research rather than on these depletion experiments.

Hagger MS et al. 2016, *Perspect Psychol Sci* 11:546–573. https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873 (23 labs, 2,141 participants, preregistered; a tiring self-control task did not measurably weaken self-control on the next task, effect close to zero)
Vohs KD et al. 2021, *Psychol Sci* 32:1566–1581. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797621989733 (36 labs, 3,531 participants, preregistered and led by proponents of ego depletion; no reliable depletion effect, d = 0.06)
Vadillo MA, Gold N, Osman M 2016, *Psychol Sci* 27:1207–1214. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616654911 (analysis of the studies behind the idea that self-control uses up blood glucose; the evidence was weak and possibly inflated by publication bias)
Kurzban R et al. 2013, *Behav Brain Sci* 36:661–679. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X12003196 (theory paper; argues the feeling of mental effort reflects what else the brain could be doing, not a physical fuel running down)
Pilcher JJ, Morris DM, Donnelly J, Feigl HB 2015, *Front Hum Neurosci* 9:284. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00284 (already in the KB; Chapter 14 note on sleep loss and self-control)

### What helps close the gap?

Several findings line up with the book's incremental approach. Deciding in advance exactly when and how to act, in an if-then form, makes follow-through considerably more likely; picturing the goal and then the obstacle adds a modest benefit; and regularly checking progress helps. New habits become automatic gradually, typically over about two months but anywhere from days to most of a year, and missing an occasional day does not reset the process.

Gollwitzer PM, Sheeran P 2006, *Adv Exp Soc Psychol* 38:69–119. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1 (meta-analysis of 94 studies; if-then plans improved goal attainment with a medium-to-large effect, d = 0.65; figures as reported in Sheeran & Webb 2016)
Sheeran P, Webb TL 2016, *Soc Personal Psychol Compass* 10:503–518. https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12265 (review; intentions turn into action about half the time, and a medium-to-large change in intention produces only a small-to-medium change in behaviour)
Wang G, Wang Y, Gai X 2021, *Front Psychol* 12:565202. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202 (meta-analysis of 21 studies, 15,907 participants; picturing the goal, then the obstacle, then an if-then plan, helped people reach goals with a small-to-moderate effect, possibly overstated by publication bias)
Lally P et al. 2010, *Eur J Soc Psychol* 40:998–1009. https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674 (96 volunteers repeated a chosen daily behaviour for 12 weeks; reaching near-full automaticity took from 18 to 254 days, and missing a single day did not derail the process)
Singh B et al. 2024, *Healthcare* 12:2488. https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare12232488 (systematic review of 20 studies, 2,601 people; median time to form a health habit about two months, 59–66 days, with a range of 4 to 335 days; most studies at high risk of bias)
