## The three concepts

Four questions, three concepts. Each concept answers a different question.

| Question | Answer | Concept |
|---|---|---|
| What makes sleep? | A company. Dozens of hormones doing different jobs, and no one thing that can make them all behave. You focus on the four at the top, the executives. | **Company** (the model) |
| What has to happen? | The executives clock on and off at the right times, with a clean handover between the day shift and the night shift. | **Company** (its shifts) |
| "Well, how do I do that?" | You build habits that send regular signals, activating or calming. Activating brings the day shift on. Calming hands over to the night shift. | **Signals** (the CEO's lever) |
| How does that change anything? | The executives are good staff who have never been well trained. Send the right signals every day and they learn their shifts. Everything else falls into place. | **Training** (the change process) |

**Bridge:** repeated signals are the training. One signal changes little. The same signals, every day, teach the shifts.

**Model sentence (working draft):**

> You can't run the company by hand. What you can do is send it signals, activating or calming. One signal changes little. But send the right ones at the right times, every day, and the executives learn their shifts: clocking on, clocking off, and handing over cleanly at night. Then everything else falls into place.

## Pub version (two layers)

**Purpose:** a plain explanation of the model for the knowledge base, not book text. Not for use in /book/. It's the reference for later QUERY work (blog posts, social replies, video outlines, app explainers), where outputs should sound like the book without quoting it. The short hook below has the same status.

The test: what would someone say about the book to a friend at the pub? Layer 1 has to stand alone. Layer 2 is the natural answer to the follow-up question. It doesn't need to match the book's wording, but should sound like it.

**Layer 1 — the explainer**

> "…he says sleep runs like a company. Dozens of hormones, all doing different jobs, and there's no one thing that can make them all behave. So you focus on the four at the top, the executives. Get them clocking on and off at the right times, with a clean handover between the day shift and the night shift. Do that every day, and everything else falls into place."

**Layer 2 — "Well, how do you do that?"**

> "You build habits that send them regular signals, either activating or calming. Morning light, coffee early, exercise, that clocks the day shift on. Dim lights, winding down, meditation, that's the handover. They don't respond straight away, but keep the signals coming every day and they learn."

Short hook (knowledge-base use, for later content openers; not for the book): "Bad sleep isn't a sleep problem. It's a rostering problem: four good executives, turning up at the wrong times."

Wording notes from the drafting:
- "No one thing" is the book's phrase (Ch03 L34, L70). Not "whip" (coercive) or "tool" (invites the in-the-moment fix).
- "Falls into place" (Ch04 L49) over "falls into line" (discipline) or "follow their lead" (obedience): it carries the reassurance that things will work out.
- "Regular signals" carries consistency in one word. Lead with habits, because habits are what the reader does and signals are what habits send.

## What each concept does

### Company — the model

The company is the single frame. Jobs only the company does in the book (review of 2026-11-12):

1. **One picture of the whole system**: an org chart everyone already understands (Ch04 L79 "a map of your own system").
2. **Permission not to understand all of it**: managers and workers mostly follow (Ch03 L72–76, Ch04 L11, L29–31).
3. **Leverage**: why these four; influence flows downhill (Ch04 L39, Ch03 L76).
4. **Indirect control**: you can't place an order or operate the machinery (Ch04 L5–7).
5. **Sleep as an output of the whole day**: "downstream of everything else" (Ch04 L5, L49).
6. **Opposing forces (tired but wired)**: executives working against each other (Ch04 L37, L65; Ch05 L33; Ch06 L33–35; Ch09).
7. **Timing as shifts**: clock on, clock off, day shift, handover (Ch07, Ch09, Ch17–20, Ch22, Ch25).
8. **Ownership without blame**: the reader is the CEO (Ch10 L5, Ch26 L35).
9. **Diagnosis without defect**: "Not broken, just not well trained" (Ch04 L67, Ch20 L29).
10. **Why quick fixes fail**: consultants bypass the hierarchy (Ch04 L51–53, L69–71; Ch10 L7).
11. **Somewhere to put the exceptions**: manager-level problems such as menopause (Ch04 note 2; Ch25 appetite managers).
12. **Personification, labels and structure**: Sleep/Wake Executives as working labels; Part 2 "meet the executives"; the title.

**The CEO's action is setting the expectations**: when the staff clock on, when they clock off, and a clean handover from the day shift to the night shift (Ch04 L7). Not "shaping the culture" (abstract and not actionable). The CEO can't do the executives' jobs. Signals are not named at L7; they arrive at Ch04 L79.

Considered and rejected (2026-11-12): removing the company and keeping only fitness and signals. Only the company does 2, 3, 6, 7 and 11, and the program chapters rely on its shift vocabulary.

### Signals — the lever

- **Two kinds only: activating and calming.** Not a numbered framework. Timing is *when* they're sent (the shifts), not a third signal. That was the flaw in the retired "three signals" (activate / calm / timing).
- **Habits send signals.** In plain-English summaries lead with the habit: "you build habits that send regular signals".
- **"Signal" implies indirect and not immediate.** You send word, you don't do the work, and the company responds in its own time. One signal changes little, which is why Ch20 L25 rejects calming yourself in the moment.
- **Activating and calming stay as the book's vocabulary**; clock on and handover are the plain-English version. Never run them as two competing models.
- **The labels don't drift.** In descriptive prose the two systems are "the activating system" and "the calming system" (or the company version: day shift / night shift, the handover). Not "side", "the calm", "the activation", "calm takes over" or "the activated state". Plain adjectives are fine ("still activated", "go-mode", "you're activated") as long as they describe a state and don't stand in for a system's name. (2026-11-12 sweep: Ch06 L29, Ch18 L45, Ch20 L3, L51, endnote L885.)
- **A rejection of signalling is a rejection of the single in-the-moment signal** (Ch20 L25), not of signals.
- Already used in this sense: Ch07 L79, Ch18 L37–43, Ch19 L3 and L9, Ch20 L61, Ch23 L5.

### Training — the change process

- **Describes only how change happens, never what sleep is.** The company is the model.
- **"Trained" is the book's word**: three jobs, the training program, "Not broken, just not well trained". Keep it. "Practice" is plain description of how training works, not a refrain. Avoid "out of practice": it assumes the reader once slept well.
- **Good employees, not well trained** = a management problem, which makes it the CEO's (links to Ch10 L5 "I wasn't the problem. But I was the solution.").
- **Fitness is an illustration only**: Ch03 for "not overnight", Ch20 for the calming system as a muscle. Not a concept and not the thesis. Keep the images apart: you send the signals, the company gets trained. Executives don't do push-ups.
- **"Reps" is not needed.** The bridge is repeated signals.

## The three jobs in company terms

The three jobs are what the signals achieve, not a second list beside them.

| Job (book wording) | Company version | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| Train the body clock | **The roster**: each executive clocks on and off at the right times, the same every day | All four executives. Morning light starts the day shift and the countdown to the night shift (Ch18) |
| Build the calming system | **A clean handover**: the wake side actually clocks off so the sleep side can take over and hold the night | The evening handover and 2am (Ch09 L72). At 2am the handover has already happened; the job is to hold the night (Ch17 L40 "the night shift isn't over yet", mirroring Ch07 L79). Payoff: Ch20 L121 "It will show up at two in the morning." |
| Remove the disruptors | **Calling the wrong executives in at the wrong time** | Both sides: late coffee, phone, sugar call the wake side back in; long or late naps and sleep-ins disturb the sleep side (Ch21 L15 "calls the Sleep Executives in early"; Ch18 L39) |

**Clocking on and off covers all four executives.** "Going home" covers only the wake side (cortisol and dopamine), so don't narrow the action to it. (Correction made 2026-11-12.)

Ch04 L77 carries the three jobs in company terms: the executives know when to clock on and off; the day shift hands over cleanly to the night shift; a CEO who stops calling the wrong executives in at the wrong times. Ch04 L79 then names the signals and the training.

### Agreed framing extension

The executives are good employees. They work plenty of overtime, but don't always show up when you really need them. What fails to show up is the handover, not the executives as people. This keeps "not broken" intact.
