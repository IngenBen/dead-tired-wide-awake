## Pillar definitions (for reference)

- **Train your body clock** — habits that set and reinforce the circadian rhythm (light timing, wake/sleep anchors, adenosine management)
- **Build your nervous system** — habits that strengthen the calming (parasympathetic) system so it can bring activation down when needed
- **Remove disrupters** — habits that eliminate inputs that undermine sleep architecture, cortisol regulation, or body clock signalling

## Habit assignments

### Ch14 — Caffeine | Remove disrupters

*Author reasoning: caffeine is a disrupter only — it disrupts sleep architecture (blocks adenosine signal, keeps Wake Executive active during sleep window) and disrupts the body clock (morning coffee competes with the cortisol awakening response and adenosine clearance, training the body to rely on the cup instead of its own waking mechanism). Both mechanisms are disruptive, not constructive.*

> "The adenosine is still accumulating; your brain just can't feel it… still circulating well into the evening, still blocking the signal your brain needs to sleep."

> "Drinking coffee in this window competes with both. Over time, it appears to train the body to rely on the cup rather than its own waking response."

---

### Ch15 — Alcohol | Remove disrupters

*Author reasoning: alcohol disrupts sleep architecture — it sedates rather than induces natural sleep, suppresses REM, and triggers metabolic rebound in the second half of the night.*

> "Alcohol sedates rather than induces natural sleep… even a drink or two measurably eats into that restorative sleep."

---

### Ch16 — Food timing | Remove disrupters

*Author reasoning: disrupter only. Eating too close to bed activates the metabolic system (temperature rise, insulin, digestion) at the exact moment the body needs to be cooling and calming. The "food timing keeps everything aligned" line is about not disrupting the clock, not training it.*

> "You're asking the body to cool down while it's generating heat — the activating system running when it should be standing down."

---

### Ch17 — Can't sleep routine | Train your body clock

*Decision (author, 2026-09-25): reassigned from Remove disrupters to Train your body clock, giving the 12-habit split 3/3/6 (body clock: Ch17, Ch18, Ch19; nervous system: Ch20, Ch21, Ch22; disrupters: Ch14, Ch15, Ch16, Ch23, Ch24, Ch25). The same day the book was restructured so this chapter now opens Phase 3 ("Train your body clock"), so book phase and pillar now agree. The earlier reasoning is kept below for the record.*

*Previous assignment (Remove disrupters):*

*Author reasoning: disrupter removal — not a substance disrupter (like caffeine or alcohol) but a behavioural pattern that disrupts. Lying awake frustrated in bed activates cortisol, reinforces the bed-equals-wakefulness association, and compounds the sleep problem. The routine removes that disruptive pattern.*

> "In the language of the program, you're refusing to feed the activating side — no frustration, no dopamine — so the calming side can come back on its own."

> "The routine works on two timescales: tonight it breaks the spiral, and over weeks it rewrites the association — bed equals sleep, in the language the brain actually speaks."

### Ch18 — Morning routine | Train your body clock

*Author reasoning: body clock only. The morning light signal is the primary circadian cue. Everything in the habit (consistent wake time, natural light within 30 minutes, movement) is clock-setting behaviour.*

> "You are not building a morning habit. You are setting a clock."

> "The morning signal is the most powerful cue your body has to set its internal clock."

---

### Ch19 — Evening routine | Train your body clock

*Author reasoning: body clock only. The evening anchor (consistent bedtime, lights down, removing activation triggers) is the closing bracket of the clock the morning opens. The handoff line mentioning the nervous system at the end of the chapter is a transition to the next phase, not an assignment to this habit.*

> "You have a morning anchor and an evening one. The day has a shape your body can recognise, and the system has the inputs it needs to do its job."

> "In practice, the day ends in the same place every time, so the body can begin to recognise it as a signal for sleep."

---

### Ch20 — Meditation | Build your nervous system

*Author reasoning: nervous system training. Meditation is explicit in the text as strength training for the calming (parasympathetic) system — not a calming tool used in the moment, but a daily rep that builds the capacity to come down from activation.*

> "Breathing — done consistently, even if done badly — is nervous system strength training."

> "A racing mind isn't something to soothe in the moment… it's a strength problem. The thing that needed fixing was never the content of the thoughts. It was the capacity to come down from them."

---

### Ch21 — Napping | Build your nervous system + Remove disrupters (body clock)

*Author reasoning: two distinct functions. The meditation-as-nap reframe is nervous system building — another training session for the calming system regardless of whether sleep comes. The conventional napping guidance is body clock disruption removal — napping too long or too late depletes adenosine pressure and shifts the sleep window.*

**Build your nervous system:**
> "Another session of nervous system training. The same work I was doing at night, now running during the day as well. The calming system getting stronger."

**Remove disrupters (body clock):**
> "A long nap, or one too close to bedtime, and you arrive at your sleep window with the pressure already partially reduced. The Sleep Executives don't have enough to work with."

---

### Ch22 — Exercise | Build your nervous system

*Author reasoning: nervous system, via two mechanisms. Yoga and somatic movement actively build the calming system (same bottom-up vagus nerve channel as meditation, in motion). The timing rule — intensity early, nothing vigorous after 4pm — is about reducing activation load, keeping the calming system able to do its job. Both are nervous system management, not simple disrupter removal.*

> "Yoga actively trains the nervous system while you move… Where meditation trains the calming system at rest, somatic movement trains it in motion. More signals for the same bottom-up channel."

> "The problem isn't the spike. The problem is what happens after… intensity as early as you can manage, nothing vigorous after 4pm."

---

### Ch23 — Social media | Remove disrupters

*Author reasoning: disrupter only, via two pathways. It disrupts the nervous system (dopamine-and-cortisol spike from activating content, particularly late at night) and disrupts the body clock (blue light suppresses melatonin and nudges timing later). Both are disruptions being removed, not a system being built.*

> "Late-night, that's activating — both Wake Executives running on full, priming you for novelty and threat exactly when you need to be winding down."

> "The light is real: it can suppress melatonin and nudge the body clock later."

---

### Ch24 — Hydration | Remove disrupters

*Author reasoning: disrupter removal — mild dehydration raises baseline cortisol unnecessarily and slows the body's ability to cool down for sleep onset. Fixing it removes friction; it builds nothing.*

> "Hydration, in this sense, is not about optimising. It's about removing an unnecessary stress signal."

---

### Ch25 — Sugar & food sensitivities | Remove disrupters

*Author reasoning: disrupter only, via two pathways. Late sugar is a sleep disrupter (blood glucose crash → cortisol surge → pulled from deep sleep). Chronic sugar overuse is a nervous system disrupter (the Wake Executive becomes overworked, reactive, and unable to clock off). Both are disruptions; neither builds anything.*

> "A late sugar hit… a blood sugar dip provoking a cortisol surge of its own, pulling you out of deep sleep at the worst possible time."

> "The Wake Executive can become overworked and reactive. Firing when it shouldn't. Flat when you need it. And not clocking off when he's supposed to."
