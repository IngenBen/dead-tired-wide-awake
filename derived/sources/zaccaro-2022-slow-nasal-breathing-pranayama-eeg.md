## Findings (close summary, not copied text)

- **Design:** within-subject, randomised, counterbalanced crossover, sessions one week apart. 12 healthy, experienced pranayama practitioners (9 women; mean age 48). Each session: 5 minutes baseline rest, 15 minutes of slow breathing at 2.5 breaths a minute (equal 6-second phases), 5 minutes rest after. Slow nasal breathing (SNB) vs slow mouth breathing (SMB) with a nose clip; measured rates about 2.5 breaths a minute in both.
- **Mind:** after SNB, state anxiety was lower than at baseline and than after SMB (borderline significance); joy was higher than after SMB; altered-awareness and altered-experience ratings were higher than after SMB. SMB did not lower anxiety relative to baseline.
- **Brain (EEG):** after SNB, more theta and delta power over prefrontal and midline areas than after SMB, wider theta and high-beta connectivity, and stronger theta–high-beta coupling.
- **Heart:** heart rate and HRV measures (SDNN, RMSSD, HF, LF, LF/HF) did not differ between SNB and SMB.
- **Authors' interpretation:** slow breathing acts through the vagus nerve whichever route is used, but only nasal breathing also stimulates the olfactory lining mechanically; they ascribe the SNB-specific effects "mainly" to that olfactory route, building on Piarulli et al. 2018 (air puffs to the olfactory lining slowed brain rhythms).

### Slow Nose Breathing Calms Meditators More Than Mouth Breathing

In a 2022 study in Pisa, Italy, researchers had 12 experienced breathing-meditation practitioners, mostly women in their late forties, do two sessions of very slow breathing a week apart: once through the nose and once through the mouth with the nose clipped.

Each session was 15 minutes at about two and a half breaths a minute, with rest before and after. Brain waves and heart rhythm were recorded, and participants filled in questionnaires on anxiety, mood and their state of mind.

After the nose session they felt less anxious and more joyful, described a deeper, more unusual meditative state, and their brain waves showed more of the slow rhythms linked to deep relaxation. Mouth breathing at the same pace didn't do this. Heart-rhythm measures were the same either way. The researchers think air flowing over the smell-sensing lining of the nose is what makes the difference.

**The main takeaway:** For people who meditate, slow breathing through the nose seems to quiet the mind more than the same breathing through the mouth.
