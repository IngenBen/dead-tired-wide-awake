## Findings (close summary, not copied text)

- Demand-selection task: people repeatedly chose between two options paying the same reward, one of which involved more task switching, harder arithmetic (carrying) or more strategy changes.
- Across experiments, the low-demand option was chosen about 61–73% of the time.
- The preference held when controlling for errors and session length, and was not explained by saving time.
- People who said they had not noticed the difference still favoured the easier option.
- Paying people for performance reduced the avoidance; people with larger switch costs avoided more (r = 0.54).
- Limitations: small university samples; avoidance graded, not absolute; which aspect of demand drives it is unresolved.

### People Avoid Mental Effort When Rewards Are Equal

In 2010, researchers ran six experiments with young adults from university communities, about 270 people in all.

Participants repeatedly chose between two options, such as two decks of cards, that paid exactly the same. One option quietly demanded more mental work: switching between rules more often, or doing sums that needed carrying.

Most of the time, people drifted towards the easier option, even when it was no quicker or more accurate, and even when they said they hadn't noticed any difference. Offering money for good performance made them more willing to take on the harder one.

**The main takeaway:** People treat mental demand as a cost and steer away from it when they can, often without realising.
