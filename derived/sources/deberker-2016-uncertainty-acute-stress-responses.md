## Findings (close summary, not copied text)

- 45 healthy adults (25 women), aged 19–35.
- 320 trials: participants guessed whether a snake was under one of two rocks; a snake meant a painful electric shock to the hand. The odds changed across blocks (90/10, 70/30, 50/50). Small payments for correct guesses; shocks did not depend on accuracy.
- Participants rated their stress 65 times during the task; pupil size and skin conductance were recorded.
- Stress ratings were best explained by irreducible uncertainty, which peaks when the odds of a shock are 50/50; participants were most stressed when outcomes were least predictable, not when a shock was certain.
- Pupil size and skin conductance also rose with uncertainty.
- People whose stress tracked uncertainty more closely made better predictions in the task.
- Authors note acute lab stress may not behave like chronic stress.

### Not Knowing Is More Stressful Than Certain Pain

In a 2016 study at University College London in the UK, researchers had 45 healthy young men and women play a guessing game in which some turns ended in a painful electric shock to the hand.

On each turn they guessed whether a snake was hiding under a rock. A snake meant a shock. The odds changed without warning, from nearly certain to a coin toss. Every few turns they rated how stressed they felt, while sensors tracked their pupils and sweating.

People felt most stressed, and their bodies reacted most, when they could not tell whether a shock was coming. Knowing a shock was almost certain was less stressful than not knowing. Those whose stress rose and fell with uncertainty actually got better at predicting the shocks.

**The main takeaway:** Uncertainty itself drives stress, so a predictable bad outcome can feel easier to bear than an unpredictable one.
