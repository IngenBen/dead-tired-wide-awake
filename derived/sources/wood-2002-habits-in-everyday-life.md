## Findings (close summary, not copied text)

- Study 1: 70 students (35 women, 35 men), 1 day; Study 2: 209 students (131 women, 78 men), 2 days. A watch chimed hourly and they logged what they were doing, thinking and feeling.
- About 35% (Study 1) and 43% (Study 2) of logged behaviours were habits (done almost daily, usually in the same place).
- During habits, thoughts did not match the action about 60% (Study 1) and 51% (Study 2) of the time; during non-habits, thoughts matched the action about 70% and 60% of the time.
- Habits were rated as needing less attention, less forethought and less difficulty.
- Hours made up only of habits had slightly lower stress ratings than hours with at least one non-habitual action; fatigue did not differ.
- Limitations: correlational; students only; self-report; one emotion rating per hour in Study 2.

### Habits Free the Mind to Think About Other Things

In a 2002 study at Texas A&M University in the USA, researchers gave about 280 university students, men and women, a watch that chimed every hour while they were awake.

At each chime, students wrote down what they were doing, what they were thinking about and how they felt. Later they rated each behaviour for how often and where they usually did it.

Between a third and a half of what students did was habitual. While doing habits, their minds were on something else about half the time, and they rated habits as taking less attention and effort. Hours that included something out of the ordinary were rated as slightly more stressful.

**The main takeaway:** Habits run with little attention, which frees the mind for other things; non-habitual actions take more of it.
