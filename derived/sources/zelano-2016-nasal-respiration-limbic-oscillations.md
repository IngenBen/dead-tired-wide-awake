## Findings (close summary, not copied text)

- **Brain recordings:** electrodes implanted in 7 people with epilepsy, recorded while they rested and breathed naturally through the nose. Activity in the piriform (smell) cortex, amygdala and hippocampus was locked to the breathing cycle; oscillatory power "peaked during inspiration and dissipated when breathing was diverted from nose to mouth".
- **Behaviour:** in healthy adults, fearful faces were recognised faster, and objects were remembered better, when met during a nasal in-breath than an out-breath. The significance statement says these effects "diminished when breathing was diverted to the mouth".
- **Not measured:** relaxation, anxiety or sleep.

### Breathing Through Your Nose Sets the Brain's Rhythm

In a 2016 study at Northwestern University in the USA, researchers recorded brain activity directly from electrodes already implanted in seven adults with epilepsy, and ran memory and emotion tests on healthy adults while tracking their breathing.

The patients simply sat and breathed through the nose while their brain activity was recorded; then they switched to breathing through the mouth. The healthy volunteers judged faces and memorised pictures while a belt tracked each breath.

Activity in the brain's smell, emotion and memory areas rose and fell with each nasal breath, strongest on the in-breath. When breathing moved to the mouth, that rhythm faded. People also spotted fearful faces faster and remembered pictures better when they saw them on a nasal in-breath.

**The main takeaway:** Breathing through the nose ties the rhythm of emotion and memory areas in the brain to the breath, in a way mouth breathing doesn't.
