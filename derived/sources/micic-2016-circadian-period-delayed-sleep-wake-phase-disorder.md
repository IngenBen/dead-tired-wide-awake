## Abstract (close summary, not copied text)

The study measured the natural length of the body-clock cycle (tau) in people with delayed sleep-wake phase disorder (DSWPD) and non-24-hour sleep-wake rhythm disorder, compared with normal sleepers. Over an 80-hour protocol of 80 one-hour "days" (20 minutes to sleep, 40 minutes kept awake), core temperature was recorded continuously and saliva melatonin every half-hour. 26 community-recruited DSWPD patients (mean age about 22) and 18 controls (about 24) took part, plus 4 non-24-hour patients. DSWPD patients' melatonin rhythm ran 24 h 34 min (±17 min) against 24 h 22 min (±15 min) for controls; temperature 24 h 34 min vs 24 h 13 min. Non-24-hour patients ran longer still (about 25 h). Longer cycles explained 12–19% of the variation in DSWPD patients' sleep timing. The authors suggest longer cycles may contribute to the persistent tendency to drift late, frequent failure to respond to treatment and relapse afterwards, alongside other factors.

### Night Owls' Body Clocks Run a Little Long

In a 2016 study in Australia, researchers measured the natural length of the body-clock cycle in 26 young adults diagnosed with a late-running body clock and 18 normal sleepers. Over three and a half days in the lab, everyone lived on one-hour "days" of a 20-minute nap and 40 minutes awake, so outside time cues were removed, while their temperature and melatonin were tracked.

The night owls' clocks ran about 12 minutes longer per day than the normal sleepers', at around 24 hours 34 minutes. That's enough to keep nudging them later every day, and it explained part of why their sleep timing ran late.

**The main takeaway:** People with a genuinely late body clock often have a clock that naturally runs long, which helps explain why it keeps drifting late and slips back after treatment.
