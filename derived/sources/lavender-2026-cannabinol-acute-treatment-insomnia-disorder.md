## Abstract

Insomnia disorder is harmful and requires novel treatments. Cannabinol, an oxidative by‐product of Δ9‐tetrahydrocannabinol, is claimed to be a hypnotic, but its effects on objective sleep and insomnia remain unknown. This randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, three‐arm, single‐night crossover trial evaluated the acute efficacy and safety of cannabinol for insomnia disorder. Twenty adults (aged 25–65) with physician‐diagnosed insomnia disorder (meeting DSM‐5 and ICSD‐3 criteria; Insomnia Severity Index ≥ 15) were enrolled at the Woolcock Institute of Medical Research (Sydney, Australia) between August 2022 and September 2023. Participants received a single 2 mL oral dose of 30 mg (1.5%) or 300 mg (15%) cannabinol, or matched placebo (2‐week washout). All participants (17 female and 3 males; mean ± SD age 42 ± 13 years) completed the protocol and were statistically analysed. The primary outcome was wake after sleep onset (WASO) minutes, measured by overnight polysomnography. Cannabinol did not significantly change WASO (300 mg: −6.3 min [95% CI: −18.2, +5.5], p = 0.29, dz = −0.22; 30 mg: −4.0 min [−15.9, +7.9], p = 0.50, dz = 0.11). However, 300 mg cannabinol increased non‐rapid eye movement‐2 sleep (p = 0.03, dz = 0.54), subjective sleep quality (p = 0.005, dz = 0.56); and reduced sleep onset latency (p = 0.004, dz = −0.74) and electroencephalographic arousal indices (p = 0.02, dz = −0.65). There were 247 mild‐to‐moderate adverse events across arms. Larger, longer trials are warranted.

## Methods (two paragraphs)

This was a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, single‐dose, three‐arm, crossover, proof‐of‐concept clinical trial, where participants received two CBN doses and placebo in random order (≥ 2‐weeks washout). The 30 mg dose corresponds to typical community use of over‐the‐counter CBN products (Corroon 2021; Full Spectrum CBN Oil (60mg/mL) 2022) while the 300 mg dose equates to the preclinical study of CBN (Arnold et al. 2024) (10 mg/kg in rats adjusted for species using interspecies scaling (FDA 2005) and route of administration [i.e., low oral bioavailability]). The indication and treatment both lend themselves to a crossover design, which is more statistically efficient and precise, requiring fewer participants. The washout period is a trade‐off between the known persistence of cannabinoids in plasma (McCartney et al. 2023; Johansson et al. 1987), the low probability of a two‐week carryover effect with a single‐dose hypnotic, and minimizing the time that participants remained untreated. In‐laboratory polysomnography was necessary because CBN's effects on sleep architecture were unknown and subjective‐objective sleep misalignment is well described (Manconi et al. 2010); with subjective sleep susceptible to placebo effects (Yeung et al. 2018). Ethical approval was granted by Bellberry Limited (2021‐08‐907; 04AUG22). The study was conducted and sponsored by the Woolcock Institute of Medical Research (Sydney, Australia) in accordance with ICH Good Clinical Practice guidelines, the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and Federal and local regulations. New South Wales Health granted Authority for the Principal Medical Investigator to prescribe a Prohibited Drug (H22/48537‐2; August 02, 2022). We prospectively registered the trial on ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT05344170; April 25, 2022) and published the study methodology elsewhere (Lavender et al. 2023). We include the Participant Information Statement, Statistical Analysis Plan, and protocol as Supporting Information. No significant protocol changes occurred after registration of the trial.

All study personnel, outcome assessors, and participants were blinded to the allocation. The investigational product was an oral liquid formulation of CBN suspended in medium chain triglyceride oil, containing paprika oleoresin colourant, to maintain blinding and prevent oxidation (‘ECS310’). The placebo contained the excipient only. Participants received a single 2.0 mL dose of the following in random order: 1.5 mg/mL CBN (30 mg CBN), (2) 15 mg/mL CBN (300 mg CBN), and (3) matched placebo. The maximum detectable THC content was ≤ 150 μg. Medropharm (Zürich, Switzerland) manufactured the investigational products, which were sourced by BOD Australia (Sydney, Australia); neither of which had input into the study. The investigational products were identical in visual appearance, smell, and taste and the packaging contained no treatment‐identifying information (i.e., labelled with randomization code and visit number only).

### A Typical CBN Dose Didn't Change Night Waking in Insomnia

In a 2026 study at the Woolcock Institute in Sydney, Australia, researchers gave 20 adults aged 25 to 65 with diagnosed insomnia, mostly women, a single dose of cannabinol (CBN) oil on three separate nights at least two weeks apart: 30 mg, 300 mg, or a placebo, in random order. Neither participants nor researchers knew which.

Each night was spent in a sleep lab with full sleep recordings. The 30 mg dose matched typical over-the-counter use; the 300 mg dose matched animal studies.

Neither dose changed the main outcome, time spent awake during the night. The high 300 mg dose shortened the time to fall asleep, reduced brain arousals, increased light (stage N2) sleep and improved how well people rated their sleep. There were many mild-to-moderate side effects across all arms.

**The main takeaway:** A single typical dose of CBN didn't improve sleep in insomnia; only a dose ten times higher showed some benefits.
