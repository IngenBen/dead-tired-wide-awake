## Abstract (close summary, not copied text)

Double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial in 320 volunteers flying eastward across 6–8 time zones. Groups took 0.5 mg fast-release melatonin, 5 mg fast-release, 2 mg controlled-release, or placebo at bedtime for 4 days after the flight. 234 (73.1%) were compliant and completed. Fast-release forms worked better than slow-release. 5 mg fast-release significantly improved self-rated sleep quality, shortened sleep latency, and reduced fatigue and daytime sleepiness (p < .05). 0.5 mg was nearly as effective as 5 mg for most outcomes; only the sleep-promoting measures (sleep quality and sleep latency) were significantly better on 5 mg.

### For Jet Lag, 0.5 mg Nearly Matched 5 mg

In a 1998 study, researchers gave 320 travellers flying east across six to eight time zones one of three melatonin pills or a dummy pill, taken at bedtime for four days after landing. Nobody knew which they had. The pills were a quick-release 0.5 mg, a quick-release 5 mg, or a slow-release 2 mg. About three in four took them as told and finished the study. Everyone rated their own sleep and jet lag.

Both quick-release doses eased jet lag, and the slow-release pill did less. The low 0.5 mg dose was nearly as good as 5 mg on most measures. The exceptions were falling asleep and how well people felt they slept, which were better on 5 mg. The abstract doesn't say by how much.

**The main takeaway:** For jet lag, a small quick-release dose does most of the job, though a larger one may help people drop off a bit faster.
