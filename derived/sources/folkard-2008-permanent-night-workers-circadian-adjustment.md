## Abstract (close summary, not copied text)

Fixed night shifts are sometimes argued to be better than rotating shifts because workers should adjust their body clock to them. Longer day sleeps in permanent night workers have been cited as evidence, but they may just reflect greater sleep pressure. The review examined the melatonin rhythm, the best marker of the body clock, in six studies of permanent night workers, excluding unusual settings such as oil rigs and remote mines where adjustment may be easier. Fewer than 3% of permanent night workers showed complete adjustment, and fewer than 1 in 4 showed enough adjustment to benefit. Lighting (normal or dim) made no difference, and there was no sex difference. The author concludes that in normal environments, permanent night shifts are unlikely to produce enough adjustment in most people to benefit health and safety.

### Almost No Permanent Night Workers Fully Adjust Their Body Clock

In a 2008 review, a researcher pulled together six studies that tracked the body clocks of people working permanent night shifts in ordinary settings, using their melatonin rhythm as the marker. The question was whether working nights for long enough lets the clock flip to match.

Almost never. Fewer than three in a hundred workers had a clock that had fully switched to nights, and fewer than one in four had shifted enough to gain anything from it. That held in brightly and dimly lit workplaces, and for men and women alike.

**The main takeaway:** Even on permanent nights, most workers' body clocks stay largely on daytime, so night work means living out of step with your own clock.
