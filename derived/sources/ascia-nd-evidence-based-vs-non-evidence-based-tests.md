## Content (close summary, not copied text)

- **Purpose:** patient and carer FAQ on what makes allergy tests and treatments reliable. Accurate diagnosis needs a clinical history plus evidence-based testing interpreted by a qualified doctor; results alone are not enough.
- **Recommended tests:** skin prick tests and blood tests for allergen-specific IgE; supervised food or drug challenges in a medical clinic.
- **Evidence-based treatments listed:** adrenaline for anaphylaxis; allergen minimisation or avoidance; non-sedating antihistamines; corticosteroid and combined nasal sprays; allergen and venom immunotherapy.
- **Named as unproven and not recommended:** cytotoxic food testing, kinesiology, hair analysis, Vega testing, electrodermal testing, pulse testing, reflexology, bioresonance, Bryan's or Alcat tests, VoiceBio, allergy elimination techniques, and "IgG testing for foods". Also advises against online allergy testing services (hair or blood-spot samples).
- **Reasons given:** misdiagnosis; ineffective treatment; "costly and often dangerous dietary restrictions"; a missed real allergy leaves a person at risk; online tests are costly and have no Medicare rebate; wider harms to nutrition, growth, work and social life, and delays to effective care.
- **Advice:** be wary of providers claiming to detect or treat many unrelated problems; ask about published evidence, risks, costs and qualifications; have food and drug challenges only under medical supervision.

### Australia's Allergy Specialists List IgG Food Tests as Unproven

In a patient FAQ (content updated March 2024), the Australasian Society of Clinical Immunology and Allergy, the peak specialist body for Australia and New Zealand, sets out which allergy tests and treatments are backed by evidence. It recommends skin prick tests, IgE blood tests and supervised food challenges, and lists IgG testing for foods among a dozen unproven tests it does not recommend, warning that wrong results can lead to misdiagnosis and unnecessary, costly and sometimes unsafe diets.

**The main takeaway:** ASCIA does not recommend IgG food testing; it advises clinical history, evidence-based tests and supervised challenges instead.
