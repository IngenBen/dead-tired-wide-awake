## Findings (close summary, not copied text)

- 607 empirical estimates of loss aversion from 150 articles published 1992–2017, across economics, psychology, neuroscience and other fields.
- Mean loss aversion coefficient about 1.96 (95% probability interval about 1.82 to 2.10): a loss weighs about twice as much as an equal gain.
- Few study characteristics were strongly linked to differences in the estimates.

### Losses Weigh About Twice as Much as Gains

In a 2024 analysis, economists Alexander Brown, Taisuke Imai, Ferdinand Vieider and Colin Camerer pooled 607 measurements of loss aversion from 150 studies published over 25 years.

On average, people treated a loss as about twice as important as a gain of the same size. The figure was fairly stable regardless of how the studies were designed.

**The main takeaway:** Across many studies, people weigh losses about twice as heavily as equal gains.
