## Abstract

Background: Nasal and oral exclusive breathing modes have benefits and drawbacks during submaximal exercise. It is less known whether these responses would extend to anaerobic work performed at high intensity. The purpose of this study is to find the most efficient mode of breathing during different phases of a maximal exercise at cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET). Methods: Healthy subjects were recruited to perform 4 maximal CPETs (standard conditions (STD), exclusively nasal breathing (eNAS), exclusively oral breathing (eOR), partial nasal breathing (pNAS) with just one blocked nostril) using the same ramp protocol on an electronically braked cycle ergometer. Before the exercise a standard spirometry was executed in the same order. Twelve healthy subjects (28.6 ± 5.2 y, 50% males) performed the 4 CPETs within one month. Variables were analysed at rest, at anaerobic threshold (AT), at intermediate exercise steps, and at peak. Results: Compared to STD, eOR, and pNAS conditions, eNAS was associated with a significant lower peakVO2, peakVCO2, peak ventilation, respiratory rate, VE/VCO2 slope, respiratory exchange ratio, and workload (p < 0.05 for all). Moreover, peak inspiration and peak expiration time were augmented, while forced expiratory volume and vital capacity at rest were reduced. Only minor differences were detected at rest or AT. eNAS breathing Borg scale was higher in all phases of the exercise. Conclusions: In young healthy subjects, an exclusively nasal respiration induces significant impairment on peak exercise capacity at CPET due to ventilatory limitation, with only minor effects on metabolic parameters at rest and in submaximal effort.

## Relevant text

Table 1 shows metabolic and ventilatory data at rest and at the AT. In brief, VO2, VCO2, end tidal partial pressure of CO2 (PetCO2), end tidal partial pressure of O2 (PetO2), VE, tidal volume (TV), and respiratory rate (RR) did not change in the 4 different conditions at rest. Notably, at the AT we observed in the eNAS condition a significant reduction in VE, PetO2 and a higher value of PetCO2.

Our study demonstrates that a breathing technique involving just nasal respiration (eNAS condition) is related to a significant impairment in exercise performance at CPET, mainly linked to a reduced capability to increase VE during maximal exercise. In particular, we show a ~ 40% reduction in peakVE compared to the normal condition (STD), trigged by a negative effect on both RR and TV. This translates in a reduction in peak workload and namely in key exercise metabolic parameters like peakVO2, and peakVCO2. Despite lower peak RER values, these healthy subjects, compelled by the experimental conditions not to breathe through their mouth, reached their true maximum perceived exertion, as evidenced by markedly elevated respiratory Borg values.

### Nose-Only Breathing Matches Mouth Breathing at Rest, Limits Peak Effort

In a 2025 study in Italy, researchers had 12 healthy young adults (about 29 on average, half men) do four maximal exercise tests on a bike within a month: breathing normally, through the nose only, through the mouth only, and with one nostril blocked.

At rest, breathing rate, breath size and ventilation didn't differ between the four ways of breathing. At peak effort, nose-only breathing cut maximum ventilation by about 40%, lowering peak workload and oxygen use, and people rated the effort as harder.

**The main takeaway:** Breathing only through your nose makes little difference at rest or easy effort, but limits how hard you can go at maximum.
