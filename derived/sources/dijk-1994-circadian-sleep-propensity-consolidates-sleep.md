## Abstract (close summary, not copied text)

Using forced desynchrony (separating the sleep-wake cycle from the body clock), the study measured how much the body clock and sleep pressure each contribute to the tendency to sleep and to staying asleep. Eight men lived in time isolation for 33–36 days; 175 recorded sleep episodes were analysed. The body clock and sleep pressure contributed about equally to sleep consolidation. Their timing relative to each other under a normal 24-hour day is uniquely arranged to allow one consolidated block of sleep at night and one consolidated block of wakefulness through the day.

### Sleep Holds Together Only When Clock and Pressure Line Up

In a 1994 study in the USA, researchers had eight men live for over a month with no clocks or daylight, on a 28-hour "day", so their sleep fell at every point of their body clock. Each sleep was recorded in the lab, 175 in all.

Sleep pressure and the body clock turned out to matter about equally for staying asleep. On a normal schedule, the two are timed so that just as sleep pressure drains through the night, the body clock's pull towards sleep strengthens, keeping sleep in one solid block; through the day, the clock's wake signal rises as sleep pressure builds, keeping you awake.

**The main takeaway:** A solid night's sleep depends on sleeping at the right time on your body clock; sleep at the wrong time and it doesn't hold together.
