## Abstract (close summary, not copied text)

Bright light and darkness can fully shift night workers' clocks, but few would want that because it leaves them out of step on days off. This study aimed for a compromise: moving the sleepiest point of the body clock out of the night shift and into the first half of day sleep (target melatonin onset about 3 am). 19 healthy adults did 3 simulated night shifts (11 pm–7 am), 2 days off, 4 more nights and 2 more days off, working in the lab and sleeping at home. The experimental group got four 15-minute bright-light pulses each night shift, wore dark sunglasses outside, slept in dark bedrooms at set times, and got outdoor afternoon light as a "brake" to stop the clock delaying too far. Controls had normal room light, lighter sunglasses and unrestricted sleep and light. The experimental group's final melatonin onset was about 3:20 am, close to target, against about 11:25 pm for controls. They slept nearly all their allowed time. Anyone who reached the compromise position, in either group, performed better on night shifts. The authors conclude that cheap, practical steps can produce this compromise position.

### Sunglasses and Dark Bedrooms Help Night Workers' Clocks Partway Over

In a 2009 study at Rush University in Chicago, USA, researchers put 19 healthy young adults through a simulated run of night shifts with days off in between. One group got short bursts of bright light during each shift, wore dark sunglasses whenever they were outside, slept in a blacked-out bedroom at set times, and got some afternoon daylight to stop their clocks drifting too far. The other group carried on as normal.

The light-managed group's body clocks moved most of the way towards nights, to a compromise point where their sleepiest time fell during their morning sleep rather than at work. They slept well in the day, still had usable time on days off, and performed better at work.

**The main takeaway:** Managing light (bright at work, dark glasses on the way home, a dark bedroom) helps night workers' clocks shift enough to work and sleep better, without cutting them off from normal days.
