## Findings (close summary, not copied text)

- **Design:** double-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial at three Australian sites (2012–2014). Patients aged 16–65 diagnosed with delayed sleep-wake phase disorder by a sleep physician and confirmed by lab testing to have a late body clock (melatonin onset after, or within 30 min before, their desired bedtime). A third of those tested did not have a late clock and were excluded. 116 randomised (mean age 29), 104 completed.
- **Treatment:** 0.5 mg fast-release melatonin or placebo 1 h before the desired bedtime, at the same clock time, and bed at the desired bedtime, on at least 5 nights a week (nights before work or study) for 4 weeks. Bedtime was on average about 2 h earlier than usual.
- **Results vs placebo (wrist actigraphy):** sleep onset 34 min earlier (95% CI 8–60 min); time to fall asleep about 12 min shorter (diary about 18 min); sleep efficiency up 3% overall and about 5% in the first third of the night; diary sleep onset 44 min earlier and wake time 38 min earlier. Insomnia severity, sleep quality, sleep-related impairment and functional disability improved. Clinician-rated much or very much improved: 53% vs 24%.
- **Body clock:** in a subset (n = 43), melatonin onset moved 0.73 h earlier on melatonin vs 0.24 h on placebo, not significant. The authors attribute the benefit mainly to melatonin's direct sleep-promoting effect in the wake maintenance zone, when it is hardest to fall asleep, rather than a clock shift.
- **Timing figures (Table 1, mean ± SD, placebo / melatonin):** body's melatonin onset 22:53 ± 1:16 / 22:54 ± 1:27; desired bedtime 22:32 ± 0:56 / 22:23 ± 1:01; habitual bedtime from actigraphy 00:59 ± 1:27 / 01:11 ± 1:14 (habitual bed about 2.1–2.3 h after melatonin onset); planned pill time 21:32 / 21:23. So the targeted shift averaged about 2½ h (habitual about 1 am to desired about 10:30 pm).
- **What happened (Table 2, actigraphy, placebo / melatonin):** bedtime 00:58 → 23:23 / 01:11 → 23:15 (both groups about 1½–2 h earlier; difference between groups not significant). Sleep onset 01:20 → 23:59 / 01:32 → 23:41, a change of −1:17 vs −1:52; the 34-min result is the difference between these two changes. Time to fall asleep 20.7 → 35.6 min on placebo (+17.5) vs 21.1 → 25.4 min on melatonin (+3.7, not significant); between-group difference −11.9 min. Total sleep up 0.41 h vs 0.74 h (difference not significant). On average neither group got into bed at the desired time; actual treatment bedtime was about 50 min later than desired.
- **How the shift was made:** abruptly, not stepped. Patients were told to take the pill and go to bed at the desired time from the first treatment night, on at least 5 nights a week (nights before work or study). Results are averages over all treatment nights across 4 weeks; the paper does not report a week-by-week trajectory of sleep timing. On non-treatment nights timing could revert (median work-free vs treatment-night mid-sleep difference about 1 h, similar in both groups).
- **On placebo,** going to bed earlier made falling asleep take longer, as expected; melatonin brought it back to near baseline.
- **Side effects:** similar rates in both groups; none serious.
- **Dose choice:** 0.5 mg chosen as the lowest effective dose, to avoid next-morning effects; the authors note the 0.5 mg and 3 mg phase response curves do not differ much.
- **Limits stated:** long-term benefit and safety not assessed; unknown whether it helps people with late-sleep symptoms but no late body clock. Sample size short of target.
- **Funding:** NHMRC; recruitment advertising supported by Philips Respironics; several authors report industry links.

### Half a Milligram Helped Night Owls Sleep Half an Hour Earlier

In a 2018 trial at Monash University, the Woolcock Institute and Flinders University in Australia, researchers worked with 116 young and middle-aged adults diagnosed with a body clock that runs late. Lab tests confirmed that their melatonin rose too late for the bedtime they needed. For four weeks, on at least five work or study nights a week, they took either 0.5 mg melatonin or a dummy pill an hour before the bedtime they wanted, then went to bed at that time, usually about two hours earlier than normal. Nobody knew which pill they had.

On the dummy pill, going to bed earlier just meant lying awake longer. On melatonin, people fell asleep about half an hour earlier than the dummy-pill group and woke earlier too. They rated their sleep and daytime functioning as better, and twice as many were judged much improved by a doctor. The body clock itself didn't move significantly; the melatonin seemed to work by helping sleep come during the stretch of evening when the body normally resists it.

**The main takeaway:** For people with a genuinely late body clock, a small 0.5 mg dose an hour before the bedtime they want, plus actually going to bed then, helps them fall asleep about half an hour earlier.
