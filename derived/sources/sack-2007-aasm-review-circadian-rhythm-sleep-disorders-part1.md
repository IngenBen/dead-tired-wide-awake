## Findings (close summary, not copied text)

- **Scope:** AASM systematic evidence review (graded) of the basic body-clock principles behind the circadian rhythm sleep disorders, and of assessment and treatment for shift work disorder and jet lag disorder. Treatments fall into three groups: scheduled sleep, shifting the clock, and symptom treatment with sleeping or alerting medicines.
- **Basic principle (section on homeostatic and circadian interaction):** under the opponent-process account, the body clock generates an alerting process during the waking hours. Trying to sleep at the wrong body-clock time, in the body's daytime, worsens sleep quality and shortens sleep because of this competing arousal. The shortened sleep can then build up sleep pressure.
- **In these disorders generally:** the clock's alerting signal falls during the time the person needs to sleep, producing insomnia, usually as sleep cut short.
- **Night workers:** rarely shift their clocks to match daytime sleep because of continued exposure to the natural light–dark cycle; in shift-work simulations, dark goggles on the morning commute improved adaptation. Morning types are thought to get shorter daytime sleep after a night shift than evening types.

### Sleeping in Your Body's Daytime Gets Cut Short

In a 2007 evidence review, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine in the USA set out the body-clock science behind shift work and jet lag problems. It explains that the body clock sends an alerting signal through the body's daytime, so sleep attempted then is lighter and shorter, because it is competing with that signal. Night workers rarely shift their clocks, because daylight keeps resetting them, and dark goggles on the drive home help. The basis is a graded review of the research literature.

**The main takeaway:** For shift workers, daytime sleep runs short mainly because the body clock is pushing them awake, not because they can't fall asleep.
