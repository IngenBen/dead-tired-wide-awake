## Abstract (close summary, not copied text)

For about three decades, the two-process model has been a main framework in sleep research, used in studies of fatigue, performance and individual differences. It proposes that a homeostatic process (Process S, sleep pressure) interacts with a process driven by the body clock (Process C), with time courses taken from physiological and behavioural measures. The model successfully reproduces the timing and intensity of sleep across many experimental protocols. Recordings from the brain's master clock suggest the two processes interact continuously. The review also covers clocks outside the master clock linked to metabolism, a proposed role of Process S in depression and the antidepressant effect of sleep deprivation, and treatments built on manipulating body-clock timing, sleep and light. The authors conclude the model remains useful for joining sleep and body-clock research.

### Sleep Is Set by Sleep Pressure and the Body Clock Together

In a 2016 review, the scientist who first proposed it and three colleagues looked back at the "two-process model" of sleep. It says two things decide when and how deeply we sleep: sleep pressure, which builds the longer we're awake and drains as we sleep, and the body clock, which runs on a roughly 24-hour cycle of its own.

The model has explained sleep timing and depth across a wide range of experiments for over thirty years, and recordings from the brain's master clock suggest the two systems talk to each other continuously.

**The main takeaway:** Whether you can sleep at a given moment depends on both how long you've been awake and what time your body clock thinks it is.
