## Findings (close summary, not copied text)

- Effort is treated as a cost across psychology, neuroscience and economics: people and animals avoid it, and accept less reward to avoid effortful work ('effort discounting').
- Effort feels aversive: it raises sympathetic arousal (blood pressure, sweating, pupil dilation), frowning-muscle activity and reported anxiety, stress and frustration; anterior cingulate activity tracks effort and its aversiveness.
- Effort can also add value: people value things they worked for more (the 'IKEA effect', effort justification, preference for earned money), and some people seek out effortful thinking.
- Proposes a value-based model in which effort's cost is weighed against expected outcomes.

### Effort Feels Costly, Yet We Value What It Earns

In a 2018 review, psychologists Michael Inzlicht, Amitai Shenhav and Christopher Olivola drew together research on how people and animals treat effort.

They show that effort is consistently treated as a cost: people avoid it, give up rewards to escape it, and find it unpleasant, with rising blood pressure, sweating, wider pupils, frowning and frustration. Yet effort also makes results feel more valuable, as when people prize furniture they built themselves.

**The main takeaway:** Effort is something people instinctively avoid and find unpleasant, but it also makes what it earns feel worth more.
